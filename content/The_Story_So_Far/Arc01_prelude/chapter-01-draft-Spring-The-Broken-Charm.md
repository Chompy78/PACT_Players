# Chapter 1 — The Council Tree

Looking back, none of us should have been heroes. Not that we were trying to be. Heroes are brave, heroes are wise, heroes make sensible decisions — and at eight years old, we were none of those things.

We met every morning at the council tree, once our chores were done, because that's where everyone met, and because the fields past it had a cubby we were all slowly building — plank by stolen plank, whatever we could sneak away with. That particular morning we heard the shouting before we saw the tree.

Bram had Wren backed against the trunk, shouting about a charm. His grandfather's charm — a small, finely carved wooden thing, the kind of carving you don't get told the story of until you're older, and by then his grandfather was dead, so nobody ever would. Bram never went anywhere without it. Wren swore she hadn't touched it. Neither of them was telling the whole truth.

We should have talked them down. We didn't have that in us at eight. What we had was Fenwick and Archer trying to pull Bram off Wren by main force, Caspian diving for his legs, and within about four seconds the whole thing had turned into every child in the crowd deciding this looked like fun. Nobody won. Somewhere in the scrum Wren slipped free — with the charm, though none of us clocked that at the time — and ran, and Bram stormed off with his two friends still swearing revenge on people twice his size, and the rest of us limped home with grass stains and a story we'd need to get straight before our parents heard a worse one first.

* * *

We found Wren at the bridge — well, one of the bridges; Amble had three, by her count, though her own map of the village put the well in the wrong place entirely. Skylar went up first. Girl talk, she called it, which meant the rest of us stood back and pretended not to listen.

It didn't take long. Wren had borrowed the charm — "just for five minutes," which by her own admission had somehow become an entire morning — to draw it, because Wren drew everything, sketchbook always somewhere on her, and Bram never let her hold it if he was asked properly. She'd lost it out past the old shrine, near the wood line, right around the time she should have been drawing something much more alarming instead: a dead sheep, mauled and half-eaten in a way no wolf bothers with, not fifty feet from where she'd been sitting.

We went to look. Getting there meant crossing the stream, which Fenwick achieved by knocking Skylar straight into it — an accident, he'd say for years afterward, and none of us ever quite believed him. We found the sheep first: throat opened, only parts of it eaten, tracks the size of a big dog leading off into the trees and vanishing after twenty paces. Then, nearly an hour later, half-buried under a fallen log, we found the charm. Undamaged. Wren cried a little, from relief or from being eight — we never worked out which.

* * *

Getting it back to Bram turned out to be harder than finding it. We were still deciding who'd be brave enough to knock on his door when Moss's mother spotted us from fifty feet off and came storming over, and that ended most of our planning on the spot. Fenwick — small, loyal, hopeless at lying — tried to take the whole blame himself. It didn't work, but it was the kind of thing you remembered about a person afterward.

In the end it was Archer who carried the charm to Bram's front door. Bram's father answered instead. He was drunk before noon most days, everyone in Amble knew it, and he snatched the charm out of Archer's hand and hurled it into the yard rather than say thank you to an eight-year-old for finding it. *That stupid goddamn charm of his*, he said, and slammed the door. Archer picked it back up, quietly, and decided he'd give it to Bram himself, later, properly, when Bram's dad wasn't there to throw it away again.

None of us said much about that afterward. We were eight. We didn't have the words for what we'd just watched yet. We just knew Bram's house was one you didn't want to be dragged home to.

* * *

By then we'd half forgotten the charm anyway, because Wren had seen something in the woods worth drawing more than any of it — a small animal, hurt and glowing faintly blue, that fled the moment Archer announced himself to it by summoning, very loudly, a tiny loaf of bread. It was, we would all agree later, a very Archer way to meet a fairy.

We watched from a tree for most of the afternoon. Wren wouldn't leave. Neither would Skylar, mostly to keep Wren company, though she'd never have admitted that was the actual reason. When the creature finally crept back for the meat we'd left out, it turned out to be small, fox-shaped, deer-shaped, neither, limping badly on one leg. Fenwick called it a ghost. It wasn't a ghost.

The grown-ups had their own plan for it. A hunting party was already tracking it through the wood line, certain it was the thing killing sheep, and not especially interested in whether it was hurt first. We told Old Marn, the scout who watched over us most days — kind, unhurried, about the only adult in Amble who ever really listened when we talked — and he gave us until nightfall to prove we were right about it before the hunters found it themselves. It felt, for the first time that day, like something that actually mattered.

* * *

We went back after dark, which none of us were supposed to do, with a stolen lantern and a blanket and more food than we needed. It came back for the meat, wary, limping worse than before, and when it looked up at us — all of us, together — something passed between it and every one of us at once: a flash of a leg caught in an iron trap, of pain, of being alone with it. We didn't ask for that. It just arrived, the way real fear does, uninvited.

Fenwick went for it with a stick before Skylar hauled him back by the collar. That was the closest we came to ruining it. After that we did the only thing that actually worked — we sat still, and let it decide we weren't a threat, and it believed us long enough to let itself be wrapped in a blanket and carried, half-asleep, back to our cubby. Skylar stayed with it through the night. The rest of us went home to be yelled at by our own families for being out so late, which, in fairness, we deserved.

* * *

In the morning Old Marn came and knelt beside it and put one hand against its side, and a warm blue light passed between his palm and its fur, and he told us it would heal clean. He said we'd done a good deed. He also said, in the way adults say things they don't quite mean as a joke, that it might remember us for it, one day, if we ever needed it to.

We carried it back out to the wood line ourselves — Skylar and Moss, mostly, because it had stopped flinching from them — and let it go. It licked Skylar's hand once before it ran, which none of us have ever quite let her live down.

Archer finally gave Bram his charm back that same afternoon, properly this time, on his own two feet at the front door, no father in sight. Bram said thank you like he meant it, and noticed the bruises we were all still wearing, and told us if we ever needed anything, to ask him. We didn't know yet how much that offer would end up meaning.

And Wren — who had spent the whole day proving she belonged with us whether we'd decided that yet or not — got voted into the cubby that evening, by a show of hands and a very serious, entirely made-up blood oath none of us actually understood the rules of. She fell asleep against Skylar's shoulder that night, the six of us camped out under the roof we'd built ourselves, plank by stolen plank, talking about fairy foxes and stolen charms and everything we'd gotten away with.

We didn't know it yet, but that was the last summer any of it would be this simple.
