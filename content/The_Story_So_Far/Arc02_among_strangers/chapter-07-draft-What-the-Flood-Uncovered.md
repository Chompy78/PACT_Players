# Chapter 7 — What the Flood Uncovered

We went out that night to save people. We came home having hurt some of the very ones we saved.

Harvest month brought the first bruised fruit to market, and underneath it, same as always, the usual quiet traffic of things nobody official wanted named.

* * *

It had been a good stretch, on the whole. Two gold pieces a month more once our training formally ended, which felt like riches until you remembered what riches meant to a guild that owned the difference. We'd heard, in passing, that Dela had been overheard arguing in her own kitchen about giving too much away for free — soft, everyone agreed, too soft for a woman running a business, and none of us thought to wonder yet what a woman like that looked like once she stopped being soft. We'd heard that Wren's training had bent sideways from ours: less swordwork, more the quiet business of finances and files and popping up behind you unseen to see how you'd react. She wouldn't say much about it. She didn't have to. We already knew we were going to regret how good she'd gotten at noticing things.

And Anders had a debt of his own, smaller than the guild's but louder in his ear. Some months back, a man in a dockside tavern had handed him a small glowing vial in exchange for a story about goblin prisoners neither of them could confirm — the last thing we'd carried out of Low Quay before winter turned to something worse. The man wanted it back now, with ten gold on top, and had made noises about *problems* if it didn't happen. Anders had waved it off at the time the way you wave off anything you've decided not to think about yet.

"He's making himself sound bigger than he is," Anders said, working it out loud in the barracks. "A man that dangerous doesn't drink in a tavern that bad."

We didn't argue. None of us had seen the potion again since that first night, or asked what Anders had actually done with it. That would matter later. It always did.

We also heard about Sena, the same rumour we'd already lived the ending of — that she'd disappeared with a year left on her contract and nobody in the guild could say why. We knew why. We just let the guild keep guessing, because the alternative was saying it out loud, and none of us were ready to be the ones who did.

Then Hobbern found us at dusk, arms folded, and told us Low Quay was drowning.

"The storm's breached the sea wall," he said. "The Kestrel's run aground — the one you unloaded last time. It's not paid work. But it'd be looked on favourably."

"So nobody's making us," Anders said. "There's just a heavy suggestion."

"We've done a lot of good for this town already," Moss said, entirely straight-faced — and it took us a moment to realise he meant it. We had, in a manner of speaking. We had also crossed a picket line, wrecked a netmaker's hovel, and killed one child, and no matter how many times we ran the sum, the ledger between those things and *good* refused to balance. We went anyway. We always went anyway. Going anyway, we were starting to understand, was the only trick we actually knew.

* * *

By the time we reached it, the water had already swallowed Sabine's street — her door standing open onto black water, with no lights showing behind it.

Moss went in first, because Moss always went in first, wading through water that came up past his knees and rising. Anders stopped at the threshold and said, plainly, that he'd watch from outside — no shame in it, just an honest accounting of what he was and wasn't built for that night. Fenwick was nowhere to be found. He'd left the barracks late, we'd learn afterward, chasing his own rolling pin halfway across Stoneharbour after it worked loose from his pack in the crush of people fleeing the flooding streets — the one thing he carried that he wouldn't leave behind for any storm, gnome-sized and absurd and, to him, entirely non-negotiable. He would turn up later soaked through and furious at himself, and none of us ever teased him about it twice, because the second time he actually looked like he might cry.

Inside, in flashes of lightning, Moss found Sabine tangled in her own nets, the water climbing toward her face. He waded to her, tried to pull the netting free with his bare hands and only tightened it, and for one long stretch of dark it looked as though he was making things worse rather than better. Caspian arrived at a run, dropped in beside him, and went to work with his dagger instead of his hands, cutting the net away thread by thread while lightning found them every few seconds and then dropped them back into black. Between the two of them, working half-blind, they got her free. It was only then, with her sagging half-conscious between them, that Moss placed her face — the net-mender from the courier round, the frame they'd carried out of her one room in a crate not two months before — and something in his chest went tight that had nothing to do with the cold. She came up gasping something at both of them, over and over — *thank you, thank you* — before either of them had worked out which one she meant, or whether she'd have meant it at all if she'd known.

"Very welcome," Moss told her — quieter than he meant to, and neither of them asked him why.

Her home was ruined behind her — nets and floor and the small good order of a life all under water now — but she was breathing, and they walked her to somewhere dry.

* * *

We found the next trouble a few streets on, at a converted net shed the locals called the Long Table, where a soup kitchen fed whoever Low Quay couldn't otherwise feed. Figures moved in and out of it in the dark, hauling sacks and crates, and someone inside shouted for everyone to grab what they could and not worry about the rest.

We decided, with the confidence of people who had recently killed a child, that we'd found looters.

Caspian went in reciting the guild's own name like a threat, cracked one of the men's skulls with a blow from his staff, and had him down before anyone inside had understood what was happening to them.

It took a shout of *Jory, we've got looters!* and the answering voice of a woman screaming from inside a collapsing warehouse to tell us how badly we'd read the room.

The building went down around the last of its supports while a woman we'd learn was called Wenda fought to save the last crates from inside it. Another man ran in after her — Jory, as it turned out, not the one Caspian had already dropped, but someone else entirely — and Caspian went in behind him; between the two of them they dragged Wenda clear seconds before the roof came down where she'd been standing. We came out soaked and coughing into the dark, and Jory looked at the six or seven of us who'd just attacked his people over a rescue, and did not, remarkably, hit anyone.

Once the shout made it clear who we'd actually attacked, Anders tried to make it right — offering help to the very people we'd just beaten, in a voice pitched for menace rather than reassurance, which did nothing but confirm every suspicion the terrified man in front of him already had. Fenwick's voice reached us out of nowhere in the middle of it all — *hey lads, what's going on* — soaked through and out of breath, and none of us asked yet where he'd come from. There'd be time for that later.

"Us Low Quay people need to stick together," Jory said instead, once he'd caught his breath, and did not seem to mean it as an accusation, even though it clearly could have been one.

"I'm Anders," Anders said, stepping forward with his most winning smile on, "and this is my gang."

It did not land the way he'd hoped. It rarely did.

We patched it over as best we could — an apology dressed up as gratitude, a story about looters that wasn't quite a lie — and Jory let it go with more grace than we'd earned, the grace of a man who'd just been reminded, twice in one hour, exactly how little his own safety was worth to people richer than him. None of us thought about it again that night. We would, before long.

* * *

The Kestrel was worse than we remembered her — low in the black water, listing, taking on more with every gust, lantern-light spilling from an open hatch onto men hauling crates across to a second boat that had no business being there.

"Looters," Moss said, already moving.

"Or salvage," Anders said, which was, on a ship going down in a storm, a genuinely harder question than it sounded.

Fenwick found himself arguing exactly that with one of the men mid-deck — *you're taking it, but not to the owner, that's not salvage, that's looting* — a debate neither of them won so much as abandoned when the sound of pounding and screaming reached them from below, coming from behind a door jammed shut by the ship's own listing. Whatever the argument had been about, it stopped mattering the moment people started dying on the other side of a door.

Moss found an axe and went at the door with everything he had, Caspian beside him doing the same, until the frame gave way all at once and they found what was inside: a dozen sailors trapped in a flooding hold, and something else in there with them — a huge, formless, glistening thing, half-drowned in its own filth, with one man already caught and suspended inside it, mouth open on a scream that never quite reached the air.

Nobody had a good word for it. We settled on *the bilge blob* and left it there.

Moss hooked the trapped sailor free with a whip of thorned vine and hauled, and the man came loose limp and unresponsive, and for one terrible moment we thought they'd killed the person they'd come to save. Moss knelt over him and pushed breath and warmth back into a chest that had stopped moving, and the man coughed, and lived, and none of us said much about how close that had actually been.

The thing turned its whole slow bulk on whoever hit it hardest and forgot completely that it should have been afraid of anything smaller. Anders slid blades into it from angles it never saw coming, laughing about whether a mind could even be cut in something that didn't look like it had one, and found out, startled, that it apparently did. Caspian swung his staff into it again and again, standing his ground when it lunged, going down once under its grip and coming up furious. And when it finally caught Moss and started to draw him in, arms burning where its substance touched bare skin, Moss did the only thing left to him. None of us had seen him do it before, and none of us asked how — he became something the water didn't frighten and the blob couldn't easily hold, and a giant badger tore itself free of the thing's grip with Caspian's help on the other end of the pull.

We got them all out in the end — sailor after sailor, coughing, terrified, alive — while the last of the crew abandoned ship around us into the storm. In the cargo hold, tucked where the ship's own listing had hidden it, Anders and Fenwick found one of the same fine crates they'd unloaded from this ship months before. Nobody asked why it mattered. We just carried it off the sinking ship along with everyone we'd managed to save, and left the rest of her to go down without us.

* * *

Cutter's Lane was where we found the sandbags, and the fight over them.

Four people were dragging bags off a half-built wall while others cursed and swung at them, and it took Caspian stepping bodily between two of the combatants to learn that the man being called a thief was only trying to save his own house from the same flood the wall was meant to hold back. We left the crowd to their wall and went with him instead — down a short muddy street to a small, leaking home where eight children waited upstairs, and where a giant badger with a talent for digging turned out to be exactly what a drowning family needed.

"There's a giant badger," someone shouted, more delighted than frightened, and Moss dug a rough levee around the foundations with his own claws while Fenwick found, of all things, a scrap of old sailcloth wedged into the wreckage nearby — the kind of small, unearned luck that followed him everywhere and that none of us had ever managed to explain — and jammed it into the last gap the badger couldn't reach.

The man's name was Halder Kest, and he shook Caspian's hand afterward like he meant to remember it. "If you ever need a hand around town," he said, "let me know." We didn't know yet how many more evenings like this one it would take before offers like that stopped coming so easily.

We went back to help finish the main wall once Halder's house was secure, Moss himself again by then, tired down to the bone. It was there that Moss, hauling a sandbag too heavy for one person at the wall's furthest dark end, away from the lanterns and the shouting, found Ordis beside him — soaked through, working without a word to anyone, the way she always seemed to be doing the hardest job in the quietest corner of any night this town had. Neither of them said anything for the length of three or four bags. Then, low enough that it barely carried past the two of them, Ordis said: "Don't shout my name. Just pull." He didn't. When the wall held and the others had already moved on ahead, out of earshot, she looked at him for a moment too long to be nothing. "…You're alright, you know that?" And then she was already moving toward the next rope, gone before either of them had to decide what to do with it.

We didn't see her again that night. Moss didn't mention her, and none of us asked. Some things you let stay exactly as private as they were handed to you.

Exhausted, aching, half-drowned ourselves, we started back toward the barracks — and walked, out of nothing but tired habit, straight past Brant's cookshop, where Dela ran her kitchen with Toby at her side. He wasn't her son by blood — nobody's, as far as any of us had ever heard, just a boy she'd taken in years back and never once made feel like a debt — but he worked that kitchen like the place belonged to him twice over, which, in every way that mattered, it did. A scream reached us before we'd even registered the broken roofline above it. Inside, a fallen rafter had come down across Toby's arm, pinning him to the floor while Dela hauled uselessly at a beam far heavier than she was, and somewhere in the black water pooling around him something had gotten to his skin as well — he was screaming from two different kinds of pain by the time we got there, and it took Fenwick's own rolling pin, wedged in as a lever, and every ounce of strength Moss had left in him, to shift the beam enough to drag Toby clear.

Dela barely thanked us before she'd loaded him onto a cart and was gone into the dark toward the guild medic, promising free meals for a month over her shoulder as she went. We didn't hold her to it. We didn't need to. We fell into our own cots not long after, half of the others already there and unconscious before we'd even got our boots off, and slept the kind of sleep that doesn't dream because it doesn't have room to.

* * *

Morning came grey and wrung-out over a town that looked worse in daylight than it had in the storm — mud and wreck and salvage as far as the eye reached, the poor half of Low Quay far worse off than the rich half we called home. We heard that Dela had taken Toby to the guild medic, at her own cost, and that the guild itself had cancelled the day's work for everyone not specifically needed.

We were, of course, specifically needed.

"From the accounts I've heard, you did well last night," Slate told us, and for a moment it was almost a nice thing to hear. Then he set us straight to work: a bonded guild warehouse in Low Quay had been forced open during the storm and robbed — a handful of coin, some nails, some pitch, some lamp oil, nothing that mattered on its own, but the guild's reputation for keeping its own secure mattered a great deal more than the goods themselves. "Find who did it," he said. "The materials matter less than the culprit."

The inventory had been counted twice, agreed by two hands, and still came up four gold light. The man who'd done the counting was named Fred — a junior guild member, grade one same as us, easy to dislike on principle alone.

* * *

Anders went looking for him without the rest of us, which felt right at the time and looked worse in hindsight.

He found Fred finishing a stock-take in the armoury and put the accusation to him directly — *why'd you take the gold, Fred* — and when Fred, quite reasonably, told him to get lost, Anders reached for the one weapon he actually trusted. He told Fred, with a straight face and total conviction, that Slate himself already knew and that Fred would be excommunicated from the guild entirely if the gold wasn't returned by morning. It was a lie built from nothing, and it came out of him so smoothly it startled even him. Fred didn't believe a word of it anyway, and kept counting arrows, ignoring him entirely, which only made it worse. Somewhere in the same stretch of frustration Anders scratched Fred's name onto the warehouse's own inventory note, in Anders's own hand — turning an honest counting discrepancy into something that would read, to anyone who found it later, like a signed confession. Then, out of pure spite, he snatched up a quiver of Fred's arrows, hurled them across the room, called him a few names we won't repeat, and stormed out.

Fred, it turned out, hadn't taken anything. He'd been counting arrows the whole time, uninvolved and entirely innocent, and would go on being uninvolved and just as innocent no matter how many names got thrown at him.

All the guild had to say about it afterward was that guild reputation had already been poor — said quietly, in the kind of tone that meant a note was going into a file somewhere with Anders's name on it. Nobody at the table that day thought it worth arguing over. We should have. A man wrongly accused, publicly, by a guild member acting on nothing, is exactly the kind of thing a town remembers long after the guild has stopped caring.

* * *

Together again, we searched the warehouse itself and found the lock forced by something pointed rather than flat — a dock tool, not a crowbar — which put the thief somewhere among Low Quay's own working people rather than anywhere the guild would have thought to look first.

We found our answer canvassing the streets nearby, in the shape of one unusually well-mended roof: fresh pitch, new glass, nails that cost more than anyone poor should have been able to afford. It belonged to a little shrine called the Anchor Light, and its keeper — a soft, grateful woman who introduced herself only in passing — told us proudly that a young man from town had paid for all of it himself, out of simple generosity. While Anders slipped around the back and confirmed pitch tins and a flask of lamp oil that hadn't come cheap, the keeper told us, without being asked twice, exactly where to find her benefactor.

"Is it Jory?" Fenwick asked, already knowing.

It was Jory — the same man who'd saved a woman's life while we cracked one of his own men's skulls open first and asked questions after, the same man we'd wrongly accused of looting the night before.

* * *

We found him at the tavern that evening, buying rounds for half a dozen friends, in a mood so grateful it made what we already knew feel worse rather than better. He bought us drinks in turn, thanked us again for Wenda, told us proudly about the roof he'd paid to have mended and the small donation he'd made to a shrine-keeper who clearly adored him for it. None of it sounded like guilt. All of it, we already knew, was paid for with the guild's own stolen gold.

We argued it out quietly among ourselves once we'd left him — pitch-stained hands, a shrine with a suspiciously generous new roof, a known hangout, and nothing else that would hold up anywhere that actually mattered. It was thin. We decided it was enough anyway, the way you decide most things are enough once you've already spent an evening convincing yourself of it.

Slate agreed it was strong circumstantial evidence, and sent us back with instructions plain enough that even we couldn't misread them: offer Jory the choice to confess quietly, and only bring him in by force if he refused.

* * *

We went to his house that night with manacles and a crowbar we'd promised ourselves we wouldn't need.

A girl answered the door — thirteen, maybe fourteen, introducing herself as Nessa before we'd even asked — and told us her brother was asleep, exhausted from a morning spent fixing a roof. She went to wake him. She tried to close the door behind her while she did.

Caspian didn't let her.

He put his whole weight and strength into that door — the same strength he'd have used against a grown man twice his size, and Nessa was neither — and the door caught her hard enough that she screamed and staggered back half-stunned, one hand to her face, before turning and bolting up the narrow stairs in pain and pure animal fear. Caspian went after her at a run. None of the rest of us stopped him. I have never found a good enough reason why not. That would sit differently with each of us later, in the quiet after, in ways we didn't say out loud that night.

Jory woke to a stranger's weight coming through his door and did the only thing anyone half-asleep and ambushed in his own bed would do — he reached for whatever was nearest and came up swinging, shouting for Nessa to run while Caspian grappled him down and got manacles onto wrists that never once stopped fighting him. Nessa, still screaming, still terrified, threw herself at Caspian's back and hit him again and again with everything a frightened girl not yet fifteen has, which is not much in a fight and everything in a memory. Anders came up the stairs into the middle of it and helped subdue Jory rather than the girl, at least, while Fenwick braced the door below against a gathering crowd of neighbours drawn by the screaming, and Moss stood outside trying to explain to people who'd heard a child scream in her own home why that was, somehow, a misunderstanding.

It took a stolen blanket thrown over Nessa to finally still her, sobbing, on the floor. It took Jory seeing his sister subdued and his own wrists bound to finally stop fighting and simply ask, in a voice gone flat with exhaustion, who we even were.

"Why did you take the gold?" Anders asked him, once there was room in the noise for a question at all.

Jory didn't argue, and didn't lie either. He'd taken it — the coin, the nails, the pitch, the oil, all of it — to pay for the shrine's repairs and, quietly, for whatever kept his sister fed and safe while he worked himself half to death doing right by a town that had never once done the same for him. He signed a confession Anders wrote out on the spot, in a house that still smelled of fear, with his sister crying somewhere behind him and a crowd of his own neighbours outside already calling us guild thugs through the door.

We told ourselves, walking back, that we'd done our job. We didn't quite believe it. None of us said so.

* * *

The statements we gave the next day left almost everything out. Moss admitted to standing guard and calming a crowd. Fenwick admitted to bracing a door. Anders admitted to entering, to leaving, to nothing in between that mattered. None of us wrote down that a girl not yet fifteen had been struck by her own front door hard enough to scream, or that she'd spent the whole encounter fighting for a brother who couldn't fight for himself. We told ourselves it wasn't lying. We just weren't saying the part that would have made someone ask harder questions than we wanted to answer.

That same day, at his own leisure, Anders finally opened the fine little crate he'd carried off the Kestrel. Inside was an exotic, sweet-smelling candle and a small jar of something viscous and faintly perfumed, more cream than honey. He sniffed it, rubbed a little into the back of his hand the way you'd test a shop's own wares, and found his skin genuinely better for it — smoother, brighter, whatever unnameable thing a good cream is meant to do. He liked it enough to try it properly the next day: a smear across bread — because if it was doing him good on the skin, he reasoned, muddled and half-serious, it could hardly hurt to eat — then a second, then enough of it over the following days that something in him settled and sharpened in a way he couldn't quite explain to the rest of us, before his own body simply stopped answering it at all, as though it had taken what it wanted and closed the door behind it. He never worked out what it had really been. None of us pushed him to.

* * *

Jory stood before a guild hearing a few days later, charged with theft against the very people whose gold had paid to keep his sister fed. The costs assigned against him ran to a small fortune once fees and interest were added, though the hearing did grant one grim mercy — a partial forgiveness, in recognition of the excessive violence the guild's own people had used arresting him. It wasn't enough to clear the debt outright. He was indentured within the week, the same as the rest of us already were, and asked for the whole of what remained of his new debt to go toward his sister's care. Nessa, with no other guardian and nowhere else to go, became a ward of Dela's household that same afternoon — the same household already holding Toby, taken in the same way, years before, for reasons nobody had ever needed to write down. Dela's kitchen had a way of collecting people the town had nowhere else to put. Now it held two more, and both of them there because of what we'd done to their family.

Dela still spoke to us afterward. She was still, in her own tired way, thankful we'd been there that night. But something in her had gone quietly war-weary around us that hadn't been there before — no more free meals slipped across the counter without being asked, no more warmth in it that didn't have to be earned twice over now. We noticed. We didn't ask her to explain it. We already knew.

Our own reviews, when they came, didn't spare us either — new marks entered quietly against each of our names, the kind that follow a person's file rather than a single evening. We had, technically, saved sailors and a boy's arm and a family of eight, and none of it weighed as much on the guild's own ledger as one girl's scream through a door we shouldn't have forced.

"I kind of just think the town fucking hates me now," Anders said, and for once nobody argued or made a joke of it. Wren heard the whole account secondhand, the way she heard most things by then, and was — by every report that reached us — considerably less impressed with us than she'd been in a long while. We didn't blame her.

The man from the tavern found Anders that same week and asked, plainly, after his potion. Anders admitted, because there was no lie left smooth enough to cover it, that he'd already drunk it — weeks ago, out of boredom, without ever finding out what it did. The man told him, in a voice gone very quiet, that this was going to be a problem. Anders paid it no more mind than he paid most warnings that arrived too late to be useful.

Jory, we were told, would be joining the barracks within the week — bonded, resentful, and entirely unwilling to pretend otherwise. He hated Caspian, Anders and Fenwick plainly and without complication — the three of them had been in that room the night his sister was hurt, and he never once pretended otherwise around any of them. What he felt about the rest of us was murkier: guilty by association more than anything earned outright. We'd see him again. Often, probably, given how small a guild really is once you're all wearing the same brand. None of us looked forward to it, and none of us pretended we didn't deserve whatever he decided to feel about us.

We went to bed that night much as we always did by then — exhausted, paid in nothing that counted, a little more disliked than we'd been the week before, and no closer to working out whether any of it had been worth it. Somewhere in a guild file, our names were already collecting the kind of mark that doesn't wash off by morning. That was the week we stopped being able to pretend otherwise.
