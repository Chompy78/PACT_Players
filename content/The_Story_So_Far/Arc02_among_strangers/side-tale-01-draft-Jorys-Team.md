# Side Tale 1 — Jory's Team

Only three of us went up the coast that week: Caspian, Moss and Archer. The others stayed in Stoneharbour, and heard the whole story afterwards, more than once, mostly from Moss. So this one is mostly theirs. The rest of us have only ever had it told to us, and we have done our best not to improve it.

It had been storming for a week. We had spent most of it cleaning storm damage out of guild buildings, which was the sort of work we had been doing for most of our eighteen months there: guard duty, carrying accounts, sweeping. It was not as bad as the flooding in the Low Quay had been. It was not good either. Down in the Low Quay the old woman who could no longer mend nets had started helping at the soup kitchen. We didn't go down there much any more. Our reputation in that part of town had got there before us.

We were in debt, but we weren't broke, which in Stoneharbour counts as doing well. Skylar was the most in debt of any of us, which surprised everyone, Moss especially. Jory still looked at us with disgust, and at Caspian with a particular kind of disgust. That went back to the night we had broken into his house. Caspian always said he never touched Jory's sister. This was true. Her fist had touched his face, more than once. Fred had it in for Anders, too, because the thing between them had kept escalating, and everybody knew who had started it. And Anders had signed some extra contract with the guild, and would not say what was in it.

Then Wren came and found us with news. A group of people with a grudge against the guild had gone to the woman who ran Stoneharbour's underworld. They had asked her permission to do something to some guild members. Wren didn't know what. She did know that Caspian's name had come up.

I didn't know yet what they wanted with him. None of us did, and that same morning the three of us walked out of town as if it didn't matter.

* * *

We were not asked to go. It was made very clear that we were told. A coaster had run aground on a sandbank north of the city, and we were to go and secure its cargo. The crew would help us, the guild said. We were given a writ that explained what we were doing, ponies to ride, and the right to borrow anything we needed beyond our usual allowance. A merchants' guild was paying for the job. On top of our six gold a month, we would get another six if the danger turned out to be worse than wild animals.

"When we get back," Archer said, "we give our oral report. Really talk it up, boys."

Three others had been told to come with us. Wren was one, and Ordis was another, which pleased Moss more than he let on. The third was Jory. Fred was meant to come too, and didn't. Nobody missed him. The guild reckoned on a day to get there, a day to bring the cargo off, and a day to get back, with one more if things went wrong.

Archer sang on the way. It was some sort of marching song, and he kept it up for hours. He is not, by any measure, a singer. Now and then he was nearly in tune, but he sang for a very long time, and his lack of talent always came back through in the end. Wren, Ordis and Jory dropped back until there was a good fifty metres between them and him. Moss gave him a wide berth too. He said he was walking with Ordis. "Nature," he said. "We're over here with the nature."

He was, to be fair, looking at the plants. Moss spent the whole first day looking for flowers and herbs that didn't grow near town, and he was happier than we had seen him in weeks. He had not been out of Stoneharbour in a long time.

Caspian kept us on track, with Ordis beside him. It isn't hard to follow a beach, but there were small inlets all along it, and he picked out the good places to cross before we reached them. By dusk we were close, maybe an hour from the ship by dark. We could have pushed on. We camped instead, on a rise with a long view and our backs to the dunes.

* * *

Jory said he would keep watch with anybody except Caspian. So Caspian took the first watch, with Wren. Archer took the middle one, with Jory. Moss took the last, with Ordis.

Caspian asked Wren straight out whether she knew any way to shorten our contracts. He really didn't like it there, he said. He was determined to get out.

Wren told him how it worked. A contract was a time of service plus a debt. The debt could be paid off, but only if you went up the grades. At grade one it was almost impossible. If you still owed more than a hundred gold at the end of your seven years, they could simply indenture you again. The higher grades paid a great deal more, she said, and came with jobs that had bonus pay. Fifty gold was not unusual. Grade one, she said, was the dregs. The way out was to go up, and to go up you needed a reputation.

Caspian said he was keen to get one. He didn't understand his reputation as it was. He had made one decision, and he thought he'd been treated unfairly for it. He had been sent to arrest a man, and he had arrested him.

"You weren't sent to arrest him," Wren said. "You were sent to get a confession. If he didn't confess, they'd send you back to arrest him."

"Details, details," said Caspian. "It sounds like justice with extra steps."

"That's exactly how the guild works," Wren said. There was a great deal of paperwork, she said, and the trick was to make the paperwork work for you. She understood completely that people who would rather be out in the wild, hugging trees, didn't want to be anywhere near it. Caspian said, with feeling, that he did not like paperwork.

Wren was expecting to make grade two, possibly straight after this job. Caspian told her congratulations, and that he meant it. Wren said she found that sucking up helped a lot. You didn't have to be genuine, she said, as long as you could seem genuine.

Caspian said it wasn't really his cup of tea. "I know," Wren said. "I've known you all my life, Caspian. I think you and Moss are going to struggle." She said Fenwick was the best regarded of all of us so far.

"That's because he doesn't do anything," Caspian said.

Wren said that he took the credit when things went well, which always helped. She told him one more thing before the watch was over. The guild's training cost half what it would anywhere else, and that was one of the main reasons people joined at all. But if you ended your contract early, she said, they charged you the other half as part of the exit fee.

The middle watch was easier. Jory was cordial with Archer, because Archer hadn't been there the night we broke into his house. It had been Fenwick and Anders who went in. Moss had stood outside and watched. Jory asked how Archer was finding Stoneharbour.

"Not too bad," Archer said. "Being indentured to the guild, it's going to take a while to pay that off. But you do what you've got to do to stay alive."

"It's got its charms," Jory said. "There are a few good spots, if you know where to look."

Archer asked whether he knew any good fishing spots.

"I know them all," Jory said. "It's a fishing town, and I come from a fishing family."

He said he'd take Archer out on one of their days off, when they were back. Then he took out a length of line and showed Archer how to tie a Stoneharbour knot, and told him which pub in the Low Quay was his favourite. Archer learnt the knot. He still ties it.

The last watch was Moss and Ordis. Moss said nothing for a while. Then he said, "You know the deaths of the children weren't intentional, right?"

"I know someone as gentle and soft as you is not that sort of person," Ordis said. "Well. I hope not."

Moss said he was still learning how strong the goat was. Ordis said she had never understood why he'd set Brambleback on that boy in the first place. Moss said he hadn't lost control. The boy had threatened Fenwick, or as good as, and he had defended Fenwick.

"Isn't Fenwick quite good with his rolling pin?" Ordis asked.

"He's my little mate," Moss said. "I've got to protect him."

Ordis thought about this. "Next time there's a child," she said, "maybe don't involve the goat."

She asked how long he meant to stay in Stoneharbour. Moss said that at the rate he was going, the rest of his life. Ordis said Stoneharbour was a training ground for new recruits. Once you made a name for yourself, she said, another chapter of the guild usually bought out your contract. A bad name made your contract cheaper to buy.

"So at least I'm cheap," Moss said.

He said he couldn't stay in that town. He needed to get out of it. Ordis said that the chapters who bought contracts out of Stoneharbour were mostly out in the wilds. Moss said that was the plan, then.

After that he would happily have sat in silence until dawn. Ordis did not let him. She took him for a walk along the dunes. What happened on that walk is Moss's business, and in all the years since, he has never once made it ours.

* * *

In the morning we packed up and walked on, and before long we came to a fishing hamlet: six stone cottages and a lot of nets hung up on poles. Caspian dredged up its name, with some help from Wren. It was a small place, out of the way, and the story went that some powerful adventurer had gone there to retire, a very long time ago. The wind came in off the sea full of salt and spray. Two old men sat on a bench at the end of a stone jetty with their lines in the water, and waved at us as we went past. They didn't seem to be catching anything.

The ship was another hour on. Caspian thought we should stop and ask whether anyone knew anything about it. Archer thought the old adventurer might have a word of wisdom for us. Caspian thought he was probably long dead.

Two small children came out to meet us, about eight years old. They had eyes only for Brambleback.

"It's a goat," one of them said. "Can we pat it?"

Moss said they could. They made a great fuss of Brambleback, and of the blue and pink markings on him, and wanted to know why he had them. Was he magic?

"I wouldn't say magic," Moss said. "But he's sourced by magic."

"Wow. What does he do?"

"Murders small children, mostly," Moss said.

The girl laughed at him. That was like getting coal in your stocking, she said, or being told the bogeyman would get you if you went out at night. We let her think he was joking.

She wanted to know whether we were from Stoneharbour. We had all borrowed plain gear for the trip, with no guild markings on any of it, and Moss said, "Maybe." Archer said we were just passing through. Caspian, who thought we had stopped to get information, asked whether either of them had heard any stories about a shipwreck.

They hadn't. But there had been big storms, the girl said, and all sorts of things had washed up. She had some, if we wanted to buy any.

She led us to her hoard and began pulling out little boxes and bags. Most of it was junk. But there were four bottles nestled in straw in a good, solid crate. She had prised it open. The straw was damp but not soaked, so the crate must have been well made to keep that much water out. There were no markings on the crate. There were markings on the bottles. We knew them. They belonged to one of the slaving companies from the kingdom next door. Slavery was not legal in our kingdom, but it was legal over the border, and some of their ships did come into Stoneharbour.

Archer held one of the bottles for a while. He felt something faint coming off it, a sort of pressure, but he couldn't say what it was. Ordis didn't touch them at all. "Hmm," she said. "Those give me the creeps."

"How much do you want for them?" Caspian asked.

"Twenty gold each," said the girl, who had no idea at all.

Archer held up four copper pieces between two fingers and waved them in front of her face. She wasn't having it. The bottles had funny markings on them, she said, so they were obviously worth more than that. Caspian offered a silver piece each. She said that wasn't very fair. It was, as Caspian pointed out, more money than she had ever seen.

Archer had noticed something by then. Earlier she had started to say something about showing the bottles to somebody, and stopped herself. "Have you shown these to anyone else?" he asked.

"No," she said. "No, no, no." Her face said yes. Then she asked, "You're not from the guard, are you?"

"Most definitely not," we told her.

"They're valuable," she said. "They're valuable."

"All right," Caspian said. "You've convinced us. Five silver pieces for the lot."

"Five silver each," the girl said. "Done."

Caspian bought two, and Archer bought the other two, a gold piece each. It was most of what we had on us. "If it's contraband," Archer said, "it could be worth a lot of money back home."

"It could also get you in a lot of trouble," said Moss.

"Only if they find out. It's only illegal if you get caught."

Later, in the middle of a fight, someone said we had cheated that poor girl out of her findings. Caspian said we had stopped her being arrested and spending the rest of her life in a cell. We have never settled it. She showed us the stretch of beach where she'd found the crate, a few hundred metres up the shore, where there was nothing left but broken bottles and cooking pots. Then she thanked us and wished us well, and ran off, chattering about everything she was going to do with all that money.

* * *

Further up, the shore turned into mangroves, and the going got hard. Ordis offered to go on ahead and find the ship. Since she couldn't fly over the mangroves, we all had suggestions about what she should turn into. Moss thought she could try being a frog. Archer suggested one of those lizards that run across the top of the water. In the end she turned into a fish, a long, fast, toothy one, and swam off. Moss made a mental note to teach her some more interesting animals. Ordis had always liked useful shapes, the ones that got a job done: monkeys, cats, fish. She had never had much interest in anything showier.

While she was gone, Wren sat down on the sand and drew a picture of Ordis turning into a fish. Moss told her it was very good. Archer said it took some artistic licence. The rest of us walked the tideline looking for anything that had washed off the wreck. We found fishing nets, timber, rope, and nothing worth carrying.

Ordis came back after about ten minutes and changed back into herself. For a moment she sounded as though she still had a fish in her mouth. "I found the ship," she said. It was stuck on a sandbank out past the mangroves. She hadn't seen any people on it.

The water out there was shallow enough to wade at low tide. It was high tide now, just on the turn, and the tides on that coast run two or three metres. Low water was six hours off. We found the driest spot Ordis could point us to and waited three or four hours for the tide to drop far enough. Jory suggested we make a proper camp there with a fire going, in case anyone came back hurt.

Then there was the question of who stayed behind. Wren said straight away that she was happy to stay. It was very clear she had no wish at all to get wet. Ordis said she could stay too. She could change shape and be a lot of help out there, she said, but there were already two of us who could do that. Caspian wanted her healing with us. Ordis was happy either way, but somebody really ought to stay with Wren, she said. Moss agreed. Wren was not the most capable when it came to defending herself.

"I reckon Wren's probably more dangerous than all of us," Caspian said.

Wren did not look like the outdoors type. She looked like someone who would be a lot happier with a poisoned crossbow in the dark. Moss said that tracked. In the end Ordis stayed with her, and the three of us went out to the ship with Jory.

* * *

We could see the masts first, sticking out of the water at an angle. The water came up to our thighs, which was no trouble to Moss, and a lot of trouble to Archer, who is tall but not strong. Before long Caspian turned himself into a giant crab, which made the whole business much easier for him.

The ship lay on its side on the sandbank with its masts pointing out to sea. Her name was the *Gull Strand*. Ordis had seen nobody on her, but there were people there now. A rowing boat was tied up at the stern, and a man was sitting on the rail, watching us come. He did not look cross. Not yet.

Caspian stayed underwater as we came closer. A crab like that is wide rather than tall, and he could keep under the surface easily. As we came within shouting distance, someone on the ship called out, "Who goes there?"

"We've been sent by the guild," Moss called back, "to assist in any way we can."

"I don't think there's any assistance needed," the man said. "Everything's in hand."

The ship looked to us as though nothing was in hand at all. "All the same," Archer said, "we wouldn't be doing our job if we didn't check."

They were loading things into the rowing boat as we came. When we got closer, the boat pushed off for the shore, and the man stayed on board and watched us. The men with him looked like sailors, but not sailors dressed for the sea. They looked like Stoneharbour men, and not this ship's crew. The man himself was broad and weathered, with a coat that had been good once, and he had got up to stand at the rail with both forearms on it, as if the ship were already his.

"Which ship do you usually sail on?" Moss asked.

"We're off the *Merchant's Gull*."

Moss pointed out that it was not the name of the ship they were standing on.

"No," the man said. "We've been sent to recover the cargo."

"Where's the crew?" Moss asked. Nobody answered him.

Archer asked whether they had papers to prove it. They did, the man said. He just wouldn't show them. He wanted to know why we needed to see them when we hadn't even said who we were. Moss pointed out that he had. Then Moss asked him his name.

"Arthur," the man said. "Oh, no. Sorry. Mabbot."

While they talked, Caspian swam quietly round towards where the rowing boat had been.

Moss told Mabbot we were there on guild business, and that we would like a look around before anything else was taken off. Mabbot said we could look around if we liked. They were doing their job, he said. They wouldn't get in our way, and he hoped we wouldn't get in theirs. We agreed. He threw down a rope, and Moss climbed aboard.

Mabbot looked at him for a long moment. "Do I know you?"

"Possibly," Moss said. "Do I know you?"

"No."

There were three men pulling crates up out of the hold and stacking them as best they could on the tilted deck. And there was a woman. She was sitting on a big, well-made chest, and she did not get up.

Moss asked to be introduced to everybody. Mabbot said they were his crew. Moss said the guild would ask us questions, and it would be easier for us if we knew who everyone was. "It'd be easier if we didn't tell you," Mabbot said. "Then you don't have to remember it."

He told us not to go too far below. "There's crabs down there," he said.

"I'm fine with crabs," Moss said.

"They're big crabs."

"I'm fine with big crabs."

Moss asked what had happened to the ship's own crew. Nobody would say. Archer watched the woman on the chest. She was putting a good face on it, but she was upset.

"Did you know anybody on this crew?" Archer asked her.

"Yes," she said. "Go away. I don't need your help. You and your guild. You bastards. The guild's evil. Just leave me alone."

Her name was Bette. She did not like the guild. Mabbot suggested we leave her alone. Moss asked whether she had been part of the crew. "Not really," Mabbot said. "It's complicated."

"Was she the morale officer?" Archer asked. She heard him, and gave him a look that could have cut rope.

"I don't need to tell you anything," Mabbot said to Moss. "But I could tell you something. What's it worth to you?"

Moss said he'd have a look around first and come back to him with an answer.

* * *

Moss, Archer and Jory went down into the hold. The first part of it was still fairly dry. Most of the crates had already gone from there. Another set of stairs went down into the lower hold, and that was flooded nearly to the top. Floating in the water were scraps of clothing and bits of what looked like bone. Human bone, Moss thought.

"That's not normal," Moss said, "for ships I've been on."

Two of Mabbot's men came down past us, grabbed a crate, and started hauling it back up. Moss asked them to hold off for a minute. Guild business, he said. "The guild's got no authority here," one of them said. "We're from the merchants' guild. Talk to Mabbot." Then he shouted up the stairs. "Hey, Mabbot! You want us to hold off?"

"Nah," Mabbot shouted back. "Just ignore him. They're only guild wankers. Keep going."

We had been sent to secure the cargo and rescue the crew. The cargo had mostly gone ashore already. The rowing boat was heading for a sheltered spot further along the shore than the camp where we had left Wren and Ordis. Caspian had no way of getting word to them.

Jory went back up to see what he could get out of Mabbot. Archer went down into the lower hold with Moss. It was dark, but Archer could see in the dark, and Moss had already turned into a crocodile. Caspian, meanwhile, had found the break in the hull and crawled in through it from outside.

There were three or four giant crabs down there, and maybe more. They raised their claws at him. It was not personal. They had food, and they wanted him to leave it alone. Caspian looked at the food. It was bits of flesh. He couldn't be certain, but he wouldn't have been surprised if it was human.

He backed out, climbed the side of the ship and came over the rail to warn everybody. Mabbot saw a giant crab coming over the side of the boat and did what anyone would do. He threw a cudgel at it.

"Oi," said the crab. "Stop that."

The cudgel went wide. Mabbot pulled out a short sword. "It's a talking crab," he said.

"Don't," Jory said. "It's one of my team. Don't stress." He looked at the crab with no affection at all. "It's the dickhead."

Caspian told everybody there were at least three giant crabs under the water down there, eating what was left of the crew. Mabbot kept his sword up. "I told you not to go down the lower decks," he said. Archer said we wouldn't be doing our job if we didn't. It would look good in our reports, someone said, to bring back the crew's bodies if we couldn't bring them back alive. And there was one more thing, Mabbot said. As well as the giant crabs, there was one really big one down there.

Caspian sidled up to the rest of us and asked, very quietly, whether we were meant to be stopping these men from taking the cargo.

We thought we probably were.

Jory had a piece of paper in his hand that Mabbot had given him. It said Mabbot's crew were working for the merchants' guild. Moss asked what authority the merchants' guild had over us. "None," Jory said. "The same authority we have over them." We had a piece of paper from our guild, and they had one from theirs, he said, and they said different things. It would not be the first time. It was a bit funny, he said, that our job had been paid for by a merchants' guild too. But there was more than one merchants' guild in Stoneharbour.

Moss asked Caspian what he thought. "We've been given a job," Caspian said. "We stop them taking the stuff."

"All right," said Moss. "I'm on board. How do we stop them without it ending the way it usually does?"

"There are no children on board," Jory said.

"With extreme violence," Caspian said. Then he said he'd be happy for whichever of us was best at talking to go and tell them to stop. We worked out between us who was best at talking. It was not Archer, and it was not Caspian. It was Jory. Archer said he could stand behind Jory with something nasty glowing between his hands, in case it helped.

"And tell them to stop under what authority?" Jory said. "The guild's? What are we going to do if they say no? Because they're going to say no."

"How are they going to say no," Moss asked, "to that?" He nodded at Archer's hands.

Jory went up. He said he assumed we were coming with him, and we went. "Mabbot," he said. "You're going to have to stop. We've got clear instructions from the guild to secure the cargo. I know you've got your orders, but our orders say something else. We don't want a scuffle. I suggest we all stop and work out what to do next."

Caspian said he agreed. Archer said he would prefer a peaceful resolution. "I don't like using magic on people," he added, "who are potentially friends."

"Are you threatening me?" Mabbot asked.

"No," Moss said. "No, no. He's been dropped on his head a couple of times."

Mabbot said that was a bad idea anyway. If we wanted to keep on good terms with anyone who sold anything in Stoneharbour, we would be careful. They had been there first, he said. They had already taken some of the cargo, and what was done was done. Caspian said the rest of it was going back to the merchants. Mabbot said that wasn't fair. We were both there at the same time, so it should be half and half.

"You've already taken half," Caspian said. "We'll take the other half."

"We got here first. You turned up late because the guild was having a lie-in under its feather duvets."

"We're trying to keep this civil," Caspian said.

"We asked you before whether you had written orders," Archer said, "and you said no."

"We said we had them," Mabbot said. "We said we wouldn't show you." It turned out he had, by then, shown them to Jory.

"Sorry," Archer said. "I must have got it backwards."

So Mabbot made his offer again. Whatever was left, we would take half, and they would take half. They would help us carry it. They would even let us use their rowing boat. Caspian said he didn't much mind how the cargo was divided. We had been told to bring back the cargo and the bodies of the crew. Mabbot said, in that case, perhaps we could all work together on the crabs. Caspian said that would be greatly appreciated.

Then Bette looked up from her chest. "I told you you should go down there," she said. "He's still alive. I know he is."

Moss had forgotten she was there. "Who's still alive?"

"Tom."

She said she would pay us as well, if we managed to rescue him.

* * *

The plan was simple. Moss was a crocodile, Caspian was a crab, and the two of them would go down, stir the crabs up, and draw them back towards the stairs, where the rest could get at them. It was a reasonable plan. It lasted about as long as most of ours do.

The crabs met us at the bottom of the stairs and clicked their claws. This was their larder, and we were not invited. There was nothing down there you could call a body, only small pieces floating about. The ship had been on that sandbank for days.

Archer followed us down. The water came up to his shoulders. Jory, who had more sense, stayed at the top of the stairs.

Moss went first. He lunged at the nearest crab and got it in his jaws. A second crab went for him and couldn't get through his hide. A third went for Caspian, and got a claw round him, and held on. For a while it was crabs gripping crabs, and the water was full of snapping claws and a crocodile's teeth. Further back in the hold something big stirred the water.

Archer could do almost nothing. His fire was no use underwater, and the crabs were all beneath the surface. Then one of them came at him. It caught him hard in the side with one claw and dragged him down under the water. He got free and came up gasping, and went back up the stairs to the deck to get his breath. That is the polite way of putting it.

Caspian kept hitting his crab. Moss bit down on his until there was very little left of it. Then the big shape came up out of the far end of the hold.

On deck, Archer asked whether Mabbot hadn't said they would help. They would, Mabbot said. They were just waiting for something to come close enough to stab. Then he had an idea. If Archer stood in the shallow water at the foot of the stairs, he said, the crabs might come for him. Then he and Jory could stab them with their spears.

"That sounds useful," Archer said. He put a shimmer of protection over himself first. Then he walked down into the water up to his calves and splashed about, trying, in his own words, to look attractive.

It worked. Moss's crab finally cracked in his jaws, and bits of it went everywhere. Another crab came for Archer at the bottom of the stairs, caught him and dragged him under again. As it did, Jory and Mabbot both drove their spears into it. Then the big crab reached Moss. It was a great deal bigger than the others. It got Moss in one claw and held him.

Archer tore himself free a second time. He made for the deck, and the crab struck him again as he went. He fell on the boards, and did not get up. Jory grabbed him and hauled him the rest of the way out. Mabbot stabbed down past him as the crab lunged, and hit it, and the crab drew back looking much less healthy.

Caspian finished his crab. It cost him nearly everything he had. He went to help Moss, and Moss came past him the other way, free of the big crab's grip. The big crab caught Caspian instead.

It struck him once, very hard. The crab's shape went out of him, and Caspian was himself again, a young man, not breathing, at the bottom of a flooded hold.

Moss turned round. He went back for him. He took hold of Caspian, and bit at the big crab until it let go. Then the big crab got hold of Moss instead. Moss slapped Caspian with his tail, hard, towards the stairs. Caspian came bobbing up to the surface, and Mabbot ducked forward, grabbed him and dragged him up onto the deck. He kept him alive there. Jory had already done the same for Archer, after his own fashion. He had shouted encouraging words at him.

Below, the big crab had Moss and would not let go. It hit him again, hard, and dragged him further down into the hold. With its other claw it picked up a body. Moss fought until he finally wrenched himself free. As he went, he grabbed the body too. The crab held on to the other end, and the body came apart between them.

The crocodile came up the stairs with half a man in his jaws, the top half, and went across the deck to Bette.

She was not impressed. She looked at it, and there was nothing in her face. It wasn't Tom. It was one of his crew.

"What does Tom look like?" Moss asked. She described him. Not a giant crab, Moss thought. Got it.

Caspian and Archer lay side by side on the deck. Neither of them was moving. They were alive, which was all anybody could say for them.

"I don't think it's a good idea to go and fight that thing," Jory said.

Moss asked him whether he had hurt it. Jory didn't think he had, much. "All right," Moss said. "I'm with you. I don't want to go back down there." He had only been trying to do the right thing.

Jory thought the big crab might have taken itself off, now that its friends were dead. Mabbot went to look. He couldn't see in the dark, so he held on to Jory's hand, ready to be yanked back, and put his head under the water. After five or six seconds he came up. He couldn't see it, he said. He couldn't see any crabs at all, dead or alive. Crabs will take their own dead and eat them, he said.

So Moss went down to look for himself. There were scraps of flesh, and boxes of cargo. There were no crabs. And at the far end of the flooded hold there was a door.

Moss thumped on it with his tail. Nobody answered. He swam back up the stairs. "There's a door," he said.

* * *

In time, between them, Jory and Mabbot got Caspian and Archer breathing properly and awake again, with barely anything left in either of them. Caspian put his hands on himself and healed himself. Then he did the same for Archer.

"That's half of me back," Archer said. "Thank you."

Then Caspian became a crab again, and went down with Moss to look at the door. It was very well made, and very sturdy, and looked as though it had been built to keep water out. There was no gap under it. There was a keyhole, but no handle and no latch, and no key lying anywhere nearby. Moss pointed out that, as a crocodile, he had no thumbs. Caspian pointed out that, as a crab, he had claws. Neither of them could open it.

"Maybe," Moss said, "we should go and tell somebody about the door."

Moss wanted to go and tell Bette himself, as a crocodile. Caspian sent Archer instead, as the least frightening of us. "They found a door," Archer told her. "It's got a keyhole. Do you know where the key is?"

"Oh," Bette said. "I suppose that's the strongroom." Then she said, "Wait. I do have it." She took a key out from somewhere and handed it over.

Caspian took it down. He tapped on the door first, in a pattern no animal would ever make. Nothing tapped back. After a great deal of trouble, he got the key turned with his oversized claws, and felt the lock give. He pushed. The door swung open and a great rush of water went in with it.

There was a man inside, lying down. He did not move. We couldn't tell whether he was asleep or dead. All round him were boxes, and chests, and things that looked valuable.

Moss took the man. He carried him up to the surface and dropped him on the deck. Jory leant over him and slapped him about the face for a while, and the man woke up. The first thing he saw was a crocodile.

"Hi," said the crocodile. "Are you Tom?"

The man looked very startled. He looked at the crocodile for a long time. "Yes," he said. "I'm Tom."

Bette helped him up the stairs, and did not let go of him.

Down in the strongroom, Caspian went in and shut the door behind him, so that as little water as possible got in. Luckily most things weren't kept on the floor. He changed back into himself and got out the sacks he'd brought. He took the scrolls first, since they were worth more dry. Then he went through the chests to find the ones that would keep water out best, and packed the books and papers into those. Moss knocked on the door, and Caspian shouted to him through it. The door flew open anyway and the water came in again. Moss came in after it, turned back into himself and shut the door, and the two of them loaded up with as much as they could carry.

When they came up on deck, Bette and Tom were holding on to each other and crying. Bette was pressing something into Jory's hand. It was a small stone with a rune carved into it. She was thanking him over and over for saving her husband.

"We were glad to help," Caspian said. "Our absolute pleasure."

She was talking to Jory.

Caspian asked Tom whether he knew the men on deck. "That's Mabbot," Tom said. He was still very muddled. Mabbot wasn't from his merchants' guild, he said, only from Stoneharbour. "My wife hired him."

"I used my guild authority to rescue my husband," Bette said. "Is there something wrong with that?"

"No, no," Caspian said. "It all makes sense now." Archer watched her while she said it. She was a ship's captain's wife, frightened half out of her mind, and she had just got her husband back. Nothing about her rang false. A captain's wife has every right to act on her husband's behalf.

"Did you manage to recover the cargo below?" Tom asked.

Caspian showed him what he was carrying. "Well," said Mabbot, "where's the rest of it?"

"Ask his wife," Archer said. "She's the one who sent off a rowing boat full of stuff."

"That's all I can carry," Caspian said. It was true, as far as it went.

"Here," Moss said. "I'll show you." He slid off the rail as a crocodile, came back up over it, and turned into himself right there on the deck. The boxes and chests he had been carrying came down all round him.

Tom stared at it all. "That's amazing," he said. "You're truly fantastic. I can't thank you enough."

"You saved my husband," Bette said, "and you've saved us from ruin. You'll always be welcome at our home. Come and see us when you're back in Stoneharbour, and I'll personally see you get a reward fit for all of you." Then she turned to Jory. "And thank you most of all, Jory, for your team's effort."

"For Jory's team's effort," said Moss.

"Yes," said Bette. "And for saving my husband."

We went back down for the crew after that, as we had been told to. The hold was colder the second time, or it felt colder, and the dead were heavy in the water and heavier once we had them up on the boards. We brought up what bodies we could. We found out how Tom had lived. He had used a charm, a little magical thing that put him to sleep and held him there, outside time, for as long as it lasted. It was expensive, but it was cheaper than being dead. Sailors buy all sorts of charms like that, we learnt. Riggers buy fall charms, little amulets that let you float down slowly if you come off the yards. The cheap ones only last six months. The good ones last for ever, and most people can't afford them. Falls aren't uncommon, so most people pay for the cheap ones anyway.

Mabbot thanked us, which we hadn't expected, and offered to help us get the goods ashore with his rowing boat. We kept to the bargain. He took Tom and Bette back to shore with their chest and some of their things, and we went back to the camp, where Wren and Ordis had kept the fire going, and then we went home.

* * *

It was a full day's walk back to Stoneharbour, along the same dunes, with the wet still in our boots and our packs heavier than they had been coming out. On the way Caspian started thinking about the bottles.

He had two of them. Archer had the other two. Caspian thought he might hand them in at the guild and say we had found them. He asked Wren about it on his watch, the first night back.

"The slivers?" Wren said. "Don't get caught."

What if we just told the guild we'd found them, Caspian asked, and handed them in, just to be rid of them? Would the guild only say it didn't want to know?

"Yes," Wren said. "The guild doesn't like being connected with that sort of thing. They'd rather not know about it at all."

"So who do you hand them in to?"

"You don't," Wren said. "You destroy them. It's what any good person would do."

Caspian asked her what a sliver actually was. It was the life of a person, she said, or of a creature, bottled. It made you more powerful if you took it. It came at a cost.

"What cost?"

"You can imagine," Wren said. "Swallowing someone's essence isn't good for your soul."

The power was permanent, she said. That was why people did it. You could see why it was tempting. Some people said a little of it was fine, and that everyone did it. And she said one more thing. It was no secret, she said, what Anders had done with some of the slivers he'd come across. Everyone either knew or strongly suspected that he had taken some. Fluffy, the dog that had followed Moss home from Dela's, had never been friendly with Anders since. And Anders had come on faster than the rest of us. He was clearly more capable now than any of us.

"So that's what slivers are," Caspian said.

He had the next watch with Archer, and asked him whether he was happy to destroy them. "They seem pretty evil," Caspian said.

"I'm not keen on drinking them," Archer said. "And I don't feel comfortable handing a person's soul off to somebody else."

They fetched the four bottles and threw them into the fire. The glass cracked in the heat. Archer said it would let the souls out. Wren said they weren't really souls, but near enough.

Caspian asked where Anders kept his. Wren said he had used them all.

"I trust him less and less," Caspian said.

He wanted to know whether you could get a sliver from a beast, and he asked Moss and the others about it on the walk. A very powerful creature, a one-off, like that giant crab, often had something special inside it, Wren said. That was how it had grown into what it was. You cut it open once it was dead, and there was a thing inside it like a gemstone. Ordis said that was still not right. These were natural creatures, and you should let nature be. But she agreed it was better than swallowing the memories and the life of another person.

Jory talked to all of us on that walk, except Caspian, of course. He told us there was a strong market for slivers in the underworld. They were illegal or not, he said, depending on how rich and powerful you were. Most of the powerful people in the world had used one at some point, to turn their luck or to get ahead.

Ordis told us there was an opposite to them. There were people at the shrine, she said, who seemed untouched by plague and sickness. The shrine's healing salve never ran out, and she thought it was the goodness of the people who lived and worked around it that kept it going. People like that, she said, were never very good at making money. But they did a great deal of good to everyone around them.

"I suspect Dela's one of them," Ordis said.

When we thought about it, she was right. Dela had always been hopeless with anyone in authority, and wonderful with ordinary people, and with anyone down on their luck. And it wasn't only the food that made us want to go to her cook shop. It was what it felt like to be near her. We thought, too, of the woman we had been ordered to kill in the Ropewalks, and how she had felt like the opposite of that.

"Good to know," Caspian said.

* * *

Back in Stoneharbour, we made our report to Factor Slate. He did not look up straight away.

"So," he said. "You recovered some of the cargo. But you let an unauthorised group see some of it."

"Technically," Moss said. "They had written orders."

"Allegedly." Slate turned a page. "Anyway. I still say well done. I've heard what you did. You saved an influential captain, and it will be very useful to have him on our side." He picked up his pen. "Here's your extra pay. I'm signing off on it now."

He signed it. It turned out Archer had no need to talk anything up at all.

Out in the street, Caspian remembered Wren's news from the morning we left. Somewhere in Stoneharbour, someone had asked permission to do something to him, and we still didn't know what.
