# Chapter 1 — The Council Tree

None of us had any business being heroes. We weren't trying to be. Heroes are brave. Heroes are wise. Heroes make sensible decisions. We were eight, and we were none of those things.

We met every morning at the council tree, once our chores were done. That was where everyone met. Past it were the fields, and out in the fields was our cubby, which we were building plank by plank out of whatever we could steal.

That morning we heard the shouting before we saw the tree.

Bram had Wren backed against the trunk. He was shouting about a charm. His grandfather's charm — a small, finely carved wooden thing, the kind of carving you don't get told the story of until you're older, and by then his grandfather was dead, so nobody ever would. Bram never went anywhere without it. Wren swore she hadn't touched it. Neither of them was telling the whole truth.

We should have talked them down. We didn't have that in us at eight.

Fenwick and Archer tried to pull Bram off her. Caspian went for his legs. Moss waded in after them, which at eight was usually enough to end an argument, and didn't end this one. Inside about four seconds every other child at the tree had decided this looked like fun.

Nobody won.

Wren slipped free somewhere in the middle of it and ran. She had the charm. None of us noticed that until much later. Bram stormed off with his own two friends, the ones who weren't us and never would be, still swearing revenge on people twice his size. The rest of us limped home with grass stains and a story we needed to get straight before our parents heard a worse one.

* * *

We found Wren at the bridge. One of the bridges. Amble had three, by her count, though the map she drew of the village put the well in completely the wrong place.

Skylar went up first. Girl talk, she called it. That meant the rest of us stood back and pretended not to listen.

It didn't take long. Wren had borrowed the charm to draw it. Just for five minutes, she said, which by her own account had turned into a whole morning. Wren drew everything. She had a sketchbook on her somewhere at all times, and Bram never let her hold the charm if she asked properly.

She'd lost it out past the old shrine, near the wood line. She'd been sitting there drawing when she should have been looking at something else. A dead sheep, not fifty feet away. Throat torn open, only half of it eaten. Nothing eats like that. Not wolves.

We went to look. Getting there meant crossing the stream. Fenwick crossed it by knocking Skylar straight in. An accident, he said. He said that for years. None of us ever believed him.

We found the sheep first, and then the tracks — dog-sized, heading off into the trees, gone after twenty paces. It took nearly an hour to find the charm, half-buried under a fallen log. It wasn't even damaged. Wren cried a bit. Relief, or being eight. We never worked out which.

* * *

Getting it back to Bram was harder than finding it. We were still arguing about who would be brave enough to knock on his door when Moss's mother spotted us from fifty feet off and came storming over. That ended the planning.

Fenwick tried to take the whole blame himself. He was small, and loyal, and a hopeless liar. It didn't work. It was the kind of thing you remember about a person afterwards.

In the end Archer carried the charm to Bram's front door. Bram's father answered instead. He was drunk before noon most days and everyone in Amble knew it. He took the charm out of Archer's hand and threw it into the yard. *That stupid goddamn charm of his*, he said, and shut the door.

Archer picked it up again. He didn't say anything about it. He decided he'd give it to Bram himself, later, when his father wasn't there to throw it away twice.

None of us said much about that afterwards. We were eight. We didn't have the words for what we'd just watched. We only knew that Bram's house was one you didn't want to be dragged home to.

* * *

By then we'd half forgotten the charm anyway. Wren had seen something in the woods worth drawing more than any of it: a small animal, hurt, glowing faintly blue. It ran the moment Archer announced himself by summoning, very loudly, a tiny loaf of bread.

We all agreed later that this was a very Archer way to meet a fairy.

We watched from a tree for most of the afternoon. Wren wouldn't leave. Neither would Skylar, mostly to keep Wren company. She would never have said so.

The creature crept back for the meat we'd left out. Up close it was small, and fox-shaped, and deer-shaped, and neither. It limped badly on one leg. Fenwick called it a ghost. It wasn't a ghost.

The grown-ups had their own plan for it. A hunting party was already tracking it through the wood line. They were certain it was the thing killing sheep. They weren't interested in whether it was hurt first.

So we told Old Marn. He was the scout who watched over us most days — kind, unhurried, about the only adult in Amble who really listened when we talked. He gave us until nightfall to prove we were right, before the hunters found it themselves.

For the first time that day, it felt like something that mattered.

* * *

We went back after dark. None of us were supposed to. We took a stolen lantern, a blanket, and more food than we needed.

It came back for the meat. Wary. Limping worse than before. Then it looked up at us — all of us, together — and something passed between it and every one of us at once. A leg caught in an iron trap. Pain. Being alone with it.

We didn't ask for that. It just arrived, the way real fear does, uninvited.

Fenwick went at it with a stick. Skylar hauled him back by the collar. That was the closest we came to ruining it.

After that we did the only thing that worked. We sat still. We let it decide we weren't a threat. It believed us long enough to be wrapped in a blanket and carried back to the cubby, half asleep.

Skylar stayed with it all night. The rest of us went home and got yelled at for being out so late, which, in fairness, we deserved.

* * *

In the morning Old Marn came and knelt beside it. He put one hand against its side. A warm blue light passed between his palm and its fur, and he told us it would heal clean.

He said we'd done a good deed. Then he said, in the way adults say things they don't quite mean as a joke, that it might remember us for it one day. If we ever needed it to.

We carried it back out to the wood line ourselves. Skylar and Moss did most of the carrying, because it had stopped flinching from them. Then we let it go.

It licked Skylar's hand once before it ran, and none of us have ever quite let her live that down.

Archer gave Bram his charm back that same afternoon. Properly this time, on his own two feet at the front door, no father in sight. Bram said thank you like he meant it. He noticed the bruises we were all still wearing. He told us that if we ever needed anything, we should ask him.

We didn't know yet how much that offer would end up meaning.

And Wren got voted into the cubby that evening. She had spent the whole day proving she belonged with us whether we'd decided it yet or not. We did it by a show of hands, and a very serious blood oath we made up on the spot and none of us understood the rules of.

She fell asleep against Skylar's shoulder that night. Six of us under a roof we'd built ourselves, plank by stolen plank, talking about fairy foxes and stolen charms and everything we'd got away with.

We didn't know it yet, but that was the last of it that would ever be this simple.
