# Chapter 10c — The Warning Light

Moss had not slept at all.

He had drunk a bottle of brandy and smoked four cigars the night before, and by the afternoon he was sitting on the end of his bunk, making the dust in the window shaft drift one way and then the other with a twitch of his fingers. He kept doing it. There was nothing behind his eyes. The rest of us had slept most of the day away, and it was afternoon before anyone was properly up.

The smugglers were due tomorrow night, at about midnight. That left us what remained of today and the whole of tomorrow, which felt like a great deal of time when we said it out loud.

Then there was the crate. Peter's people knew Anders hadn't taken it. They also thought he knew who had, and until he told them, the debt was his. Anders asked whether he might be indentured to the gang as well, to pay it off. He could not, not on top of the guild. Indenture to a gang meant being sold, and that was slavery, and Anders decided that was where he drew the line.

"All I have to do," he said to the ceiling, "is snitch. And Moss gets killed."

"Yep," said Moss, and moved the dust again.

And there was Wren's warning from a few days before, which had not gone away just because we'd stopped talking about it. Praed had authorised a contract against guild members. Low-ranking ones, Wren had said, which was why she suspected us. It was not necessarily a killing. It was something bad, presumably. It had nothing to do with Peter's crate. It was a separate trouble, earned some other way.

Moss owed the guild about eleven hundred gold already. He mentioned it as if that settled something. Skylar and Caspian listened to all of it from the next bunks and kept out of it. When we think back on that year in Stoneharbour, a good deal of it looks like that afternoon: owing money to people who could hurt us, and making jokes about it, because the only other choice was to lie awake over it, and Moss was already doing that for all of us.

* * *

Slate sent for us in the afternoon. We had brought him Wren's news the night before, and since then he had worked out exactly what he wanted.

Fenwick was still laid up after his beating in the warehouse, and Archer still had his fever, so it was four of us and Bram who climbed the stairs. On the way up Anders said we ought to get masks, since we'd walked into that warehouse with our own faces on. We were a couple short, he said, if it came to a rumble. Caspian wondered whether we might ambush the boat before it reached the dock, so we'd only have one lot to deal with. Anders asked what everyone's swimming was like. Moss and Caspian could turn into something with fins, he supposed. He himself would sink straight to the bottom of the bay. He said he'd give it a try anyway.

"Good work," Slate said, "finding out what's happening."

"We managed to get that information," Anders said, "without killing or torturing or harming anyone."

"Without even harming anyone?"

"Well, no. Harming, there's always harm. Mostly me. But no torture or death."

"Excellent." Slate moved a paper on his desk. "The incident down in the Low Quay, on whatever street it was. I understand that was likely yourselves, given the description."

"Yes," said Moss. "Hence the harm to me."

Anders said that yes, in front of every resident of the Low Quay, that had been us. Slate said we were making ourselves popular. Caspian had been a dire wolf by the end, which might or might not have been enough to hide him, and Skylar had never got past the crowd at the door. But they had certainly recognised the Goliath and the halfling.

"Our unpopular opinion in the general civilisation of this town," Anders said, "hasn't stopped us from working before."

Then Slate gave us the job. The merchants wanted the influential people, the ones bringing the charms through, and not the rank and file. He needed at least four of them captured. Twelve would be better. They were to be brought back here for punishment, and they were to be brought back alive.

"Four of us," Caspian said, "are meant to capture twelve people?"

Anders said we'd seen Nessa involved, so we could go down to the shrine and bag her now. Moss asked whether that wouldn't give the whole thing away. It would. Anders asked for more recruits. There were none. Most of these people wouldn't be fighters, Slate said; they lived in the Low Quay for a reason. Caspian said you'd still need thirty people to catch twelve, because they would run. Slate said he was sure we'd find a solution.

"Manacles," Anders said. "Can we take manacles?"

"Book whatever you want out of the stores," Slate said, "as per your grade." He suggested we use our contacts in the Low Quay or the Ropewalks. Anders reminded him that everyone there hated us. Slate had known that perfectly well when he said it.

"Well," Bram said, "it can't be that hard."

"Exactly, Bram," said Moss.

Anders had one more idea, and he was pleased with it. Jory could invite his sister to dinner at the guild, and when she sat down, on went the manacles. Skylar said that got us one person. "Quarter of the way there," Anders said. We could ask Ysolt too. Moss thought taking two of the big players before the night might frighten the rest off altogether. Then we'd invite Nessa on the very night, Anders said, and make it so she couldn't refuse, so she missed the landing and they were one short. He was only nominating something that didn't involve injury, harm, torture or death.

"It is kidnapping," Moss said.

Nobody took it up. Skylar pointed out that you could tie someone up just as well with rope, and Moss said we'd knock them all out and put them in the back of a cart.

* * *

The stores were down a short stair, behind a counter with a ledger chained to it. The argument started almost before we reached the counter.

Anders had read the schedule of requisitions, the sheet that said what each grade could take out, and he had found a mistake in it. As grade-one members, he said, we could take anything up to two gold without signing for it, so that if we lost it, it went on our debt. But the schedule listed manacles as a grade-two item, and manacles cost two gold. Either the guild had made a mistake, he said, or Fred had.

It wasn't Fred behind the counter. It was Hobb, the dwarf who ran the stores, and he had been standing there the whole time.

"What's this got to do with Fred?" Hobb asked.

"Fred issues the schedule of requisitions."

"No, he doesn't. I do." Manacles were the cheapest schedule, Hobb said, the kind you didn't sign for. He asked whether they really were two gold. They were.

"Oh, well, yes," said Anders. "You're one hundred per cent right, Hobb. However, there is an inconsistency."

Skylar said, quite quietly, that somebody ought to get Anders a bigger shovel.

"If you had a competent requisitions officer," Anders went on, "I would have assumed they'd have picked that up."

"I am the requisitions officer."

"You're the quartermaster. You shouldn't have to worry yourself with little things like this." He meant the records clerk, he said. Ilsabet Marrow. Somebody under Hobb must have made the error.

"This is a key part of my job," Hobb said. "Are you saying I get someone else to do a key part of my job? You're saying there's an error. I understand that. But you said I'm incompetent."

He let that sit for a moment. Then he told us there was a gold-piece reward for anyone who found an error in a guild document.

Moss leaned over the counter and put one large finger on the sheet. "Yeah, there's a whoopsie here," he said. "If you could just correct that, maybe it'll help the next time."

"Oh, thank you, Moss," said Hobb. He wrote a note in the margin. We could take out a couple of pairs of manacles, he said. Then he turned to Anders.

There had been some sort of paper error, Hobb said. It seemed Anders's entitlements were no longer valid. No requisitions from the stores, and no meals. He wondered how that had happened.

Anders folded the schedule and put it in his coat. It had Hobb's name on the bottom, he observed. That was really interesting. He thanked Hobb and said we'd be on our way.

"And by the way," Hobb said, "next week you're helping clean out the stores. Fred will tell you what to do. You'll be under supervision."

"I will happily," said Anders, "be under Fred's supervision in the stores."

Moss asked, very politely, whether we might have a few manacles after all. "No worries, Moss," Hobb said, and handed them over.

If any of it had been meant to land Fred in trouble, and with Anders we suspected some of it had, it had missed Fred completely. On the stair going up, Anders told nobody in particular that all he had been trying to do was highlight some inconsistencies in the administration.

* * *

When Anders went to the mess hall for something to eat, he was turned away at the door. Somebody had already been told.

He took it well, for Anders. He sat on the barracks step and explained that you could draw as much lamp oil from the stores as you liked, any member at all, with no signature. You could sell it for a silver a jar and say you'd burnt it. He said this was not about the mission. It was purely about Hobb.

It was Bram who came up with something useful. "Why don't we go down to the Low Quay," he said, "and look at the ground? See if there's any good spots to hide out."

Skylar said that sounded intelligent, and it did. Anders said we could watch the shrine too, and see if we knew any faces from the night of the landing. Then he said we could invite them all to dinner with Nessa, and take four people without going near a boat. Moss asked whether he wanted to go and ask Nessa himself. "No," Anders said. "They hate us. They would not come to dinner with us."

There was a problem with scouting, and Anders named it. Everyone down there knew we'd broken into the warehouse. Couldn't Bram go on his own? Moss said we'd go together, and asked who was coming. Skylar said she was.

Moss shrank down into a spider and climbed up onto the back of his goat. He could still talk in that shape, so he could steer Brambleback by telling him where to go. He reckoned this was less obvious than a Goliath walking through town. It was a goat that already looked as if it had wandered out of a fairy story, with a spider riding between its horns. Bram looked at the pair of them for a while. "Well," he said, "I guess I'll walk next to you."

We went in ordinary clothes. Dela's was on the way, so we stopped for a drink. It was mid-afternoon by then, and Anders, who had not eaten all day and could no longer eat at the guild, had a proper lunch.

* * *

We went down through the Low Quay trying to look like nobody in particular. We were not good at it. Skylar was hopeless, Caspian little better, and the goat did best of all of us, which was saying something for a goat that glowed faintly at the edges.

It was Caspian who noticed the children. There were three of them, one girl and two boys, about twelve years old. They were following us at a distance, talking among themselves and pointing now and then. They were better dressed than the children who hung about the Ropewalks. Moss said he would restrain himself, which was kind of him.

We went down towards the water to get away from people, and came out on a street by the river. It was a little down from where the wagon had waited on the night of the landing, and a little off from where the boats had come in. The children came too. The spider looked up at the rest of us from the goat's back.

"They're your problem," it said.

Caspian wanted a look at where the signal had come from. It had come from the shrine. It wasn't a lighthouse, though it did the same job. It had a torch that burned all the time, by some old magic, and it stood a good way off along the shore. Caspian said that was where to catch someone, because whoever lit it knew exactly what was going on. Anders asked whether taking someone from the shrine now wouldn't make them jumpy about the landing.

"When did I say now?" said Caspian. On the night, after they'd signalled. That could get us two.

We went over what we remembered of the landing, leaning on the river wall with the tide on the turn below us. The mud was beginning to show along the bank, grey and shining, and the smell of weed and wet rope came up off it. There had been three boats, with three or four people in each, and about two dozen runners with little carts coming down to meet them. Most were sailors and dockhands. They lived hard lives, so they'd be good at taking a knock, but they weren't fighters. Some of the runners had been as young as fourteen. We looked across at the children watching us. They were about twelve.

After five minutes or so the girl went off somewhere. The two boys stayed, sitting on a bollard a little way along and swinging their heels, and every so often one of them leaned over to say something to the other without taking his eyes off us.

Moss wanted us split between the jetty and the back of the shrine, because the signal would give us at least one person to take. The only one down there we didn't know much about was Ysolt. "Ysolt is definitely capable," Moss said. Caspian reminded us that four was the goal. Moss said we should aim higher, given our record, and be happy to settle for four.

Then Moss had his idea. If he turned into something big enough that swam, he said, a long-necked thing from the deep water, he could simply tow a boat away with him.

"This sounds better than any plan we have ever discussed," Anders said.

"This might be the first plan we've actually discussed," said Skylar.

So Anders went through it, counting it off on his fingers, while the spider sat between the goat's horns and the goat worked at a tuft of something growing out of the wall. Skylar, Bram and he would be at the shrine. "You two jokers," he told Moss and Caspian, would be somewhere in the water. Skylar asked whether she could swap and be down at the waterfront instead. "Please," Caspian said. "We need one sensible person at the shrine."

The light was the cue. When it showed, the water team would take a boat, and the shrine team would take whoever came out of the shrine, manacle them and walk them back to the guild. There were three boats, but we only needed one. If the others came to help, Skylar said, Moss could eat them. Moss said he'd put a hole in two and tow the third.

Last time there had been twenty minutes or half an hour between the light and the boats. Anders could send a message by thought to someone a mile off, while Skylar's barely carried across a street, so Anders would be the one to tell the water team when people were leaving the shrine. If anyone tried to stop us, we were allowed to punch them. We were not allowed to kill them.

The boat would go to the Harbourfront. Not Kingshold, which turned out to be the army's base, and not the Naval Wharf, which was probably no better. We'd ask Slate to have some people waiting at the mooring. Moss thought it was a good idea that Slate wouldn't go for, but worth asking.

The army made Anders think of the crate. That was the only business we'd ever had with the soldiers in this town, if you could call it that. Anders reckoned the brandy and cigars had been meant for one of the naval offices, and the Ropewalks gang knew it had been us. He wondered aloud whether Moss ought to make amends before tomorrow night. The spider took a long time to answer. Moss had still not slept, and even with eight eyes it showed. He had one gold and three copper on him. Six hundred gold was more than a one-night fix.

"I was just going to ignore it," Moss said, "until it becomes a problem. It's a future me problem."

"Which is currently an Anders problem," said Anders.

We liked that plan. Standing on the river wall in the afternoon, with the wind off the water and the whole of the next day still in front of us, it seemed to have everything a plan ought to have: a signal, two teams, a boat, and somebody large enough to tow it away. It is easy to be sure of a plan when nothing in it has happened yet, and we were very sure of that one.

The two boys were still on their bollard. A couple of grown-ups came along the street and started talking to them. We had been there long enough. We turned for the guild.

* * *

Caspian knew one of them. It took him a moment, and then he had it. The half-elf talking to the boys had been in the crowd outside the warehouse, the day we broke in. He had not been inside it. He was talking about us, plainly. We didn't know whether he was a smuggler.

Anders asked whether we ought to follow him home, tie him up and gag him. The half-elf and his friend set off into the Low Quay with the two boys, and Anders decided he'd follow them, at least far enough to see where they lived. Moss, on the goat, was not keen. "Not particularly," he said, "but all right." Everyone in the Low Quay and the Ropewalks knew that goat. There weren't many like it.

Skylar and Caspian didn't come. They walked back to the guild on their own. Anders went, and the goat went with Moss on its back, and Bram went too, because Anders had.

The half-elf, the other man and the two boys walked straight up to the warehouse, knocked, and were let in.

"Hey," said Bram, "isn't this the warehouse that you guys—"

"Got our backsides handed to us," said Moss. "I broke in, just to be clear."

A few people in the street had stopped to look at us. It wasn't a crowd, only a few, but we could see on their faces that they knew us. We did not follow anyone inside. We turned round and walked off as calmly as we could, trying not to make it obvious that we were leaving in a hurry.

* * *

We were almost back at the guild quarter when somebody fell in beside us.

"Hi, Anders," he said. "Hello, spider. Hello, goat."

It was Peter — the Ropewalks man who had, by Anders's own account, more or less killed him at the landing — and he fell into step with us as easily as an old friend, perfectly pleasant about all of it. He'd heard there was some sort of problem at the guild, he said, some issue with requisitions. He'd stand Anders an ale, and maybe a stew, if he liked.

Anders thought it was probably in his best interest to accept, and said so, in the vein of friendship. Peter said he'd buy for Anders's friends too, if they wanted to come along. Bram went on into the guild without us, and Moss climbed down off the goat and was himself again, rather larger than Peter had been talking to a moment before.

Skylar was at the gate when we came up, back from the Low Quay with Caspian. Peter called her "my lady" and said she'd be very welcome to join us. Skylar looked at Anders and Moss, and at Peter, and said she would let us have a boys' night out.

It was a nice tavern, a bit upmarket, the kind with wine and clean tablecloths, nothing like the places we usually ended up in. Peter got us a table by the window and ordered ale and wine and food, and he paid for all of it without looking at the prices. Moss could not help himself, and ordered a brandy. Peter watched him do it, and something in his face settled, as if a sum had come out just as he'd expected.

"Do you know what's going on with your friend?" he asked.

"I suspect," said Moss.

"Excellent," Peter said. "Good work, Anders."

Then he took out a little token on a string, a sort of badge, like something you'd wear round your neck. He understood a few of us weren't welcome in the Ropewalks, he said. "I was going to give this to you," he told Anders, "but I think your friend needs it more." He gave it to Moss. If Moss wore it, he said, it would help him past his reputation, and Peter strongly advised him to wear it, given past history. When we had some time, he said, it would be lovely to see us down there.

After that he talked about the weather and the price of fish and rope, easily and at length, the way a man talks when he has all evening and somebody else is going to pay for it in the end. Then he passed on some gossip. He'd heard an interesting rumour, he said. Apparently there was a whole lot of orcs in a warehouse somewhere.

Anders sat looking at him for a while, his stew going cold, until it came back to him. Six or eight months ago he had sold Silas a story about goblins being carted about as prisoners, and taken a sliver from him in exchange. The story had not been true, and it had apparently grown orcs since.

"Silas sold me the debt," Peter said. "It cost me forty gold. I'm pretty sure I'll make a return on it."

Silas had been charging a hundred gold for that sliver, Peter explained. Anders had paid him with a rumour that turned out to be worth nothing, so the hundred still stood, and with a little interest since then it came to about a hundred and twenty. Silas couldn't collect it, so he had sold it to someone who could.

Anders put down his cup. "So, Peter," he said. "You know our situation, and our indenture to the guild. You're a smart man. How do we come to some agreement?"

Peter suggested we go down and see Praed, at our convenience. Anders said a couple of days, perhaps, since we had some prior commitments to take care of first. Of course, Peter said, whenever suited. Then he said, without any threat in his voice at all, which was somehow worse, that it would probably be helpful if we came sooner rather than later, given Praed's position and her authority to sign off on certain things.

We remembered, then, what Wren had told us about Praed authorising something, and the stew stopped tasting of much.

By the time we had eaten and had a few drinks the light outside had gone gold, and it was early evening. Peter stood up, brushed down his coat and said it had been lovely meeting us again. "I'll see you around," he said, and went. The Ropewalks were open at all hours, and Odalys Praed was the one who ran them, so Anders said we had better go and see her now, before either of us thought better of it.

"Let's go for a walk, then," said Moss.

* * *

Anders sent his thoughts after Skylar before we went. We'd been told to see the head of the underworld soon, he told her, about our debts. Moss's was bigger than his, he added. Would she meet us in the Ropewalks? Or, if not, would she ask Slate about having some people at the mooring tomorrow at midnight?

She had already asked Slate, in the couple of hours we'd been eating Peter's food. She came anyway, she told us later, because she was afraid of what we'd do without her. Caspian came with her.

Moss wore the token down into the Ropewalks, and he was proud of it — he kept taking it off to look at it, turning it over in his big fingers, and putting it back on again. He had been drinking, which explained some of that.

We ended up at the tavern where the smuggled goods had gone on the night of the landing, which none of us mentioned. The man at the door knew exactly who we were, and greeted us as warmly as if we'd been expected. "It's good to see guild members visiting our fine establishment," he said. "Take a seat. Order whatever you like. You can have local rates." A good brandy, at local rates, was five copper.

"I will have nothing," Anders said, "because I have no money. Thank you for your hospitality."

Moss asked if they had change for a gold piece. He had a feeling, he said, that this wouldn't be his only time in this tavern, so he might as well get to know the locals. The man said they'd let her know we were here.

We waited an hour and a half at a corner table, with the room getting louder around us. Moss had three brandies. Skylar had water, because she didn't like the idea of any of this and wanted to be awake for whatever came next.

Then a man came down and said, "Odalys will see you now," and led us up the back stairs to a room with a desk in it, a good lamp and a thick rug. The woman behind the desk looked familiar, and she looked at each of us in turn as we came in, as though she was counting.

"Wait," Moss said. "Odalys is a woman?"

She was, and we had met her before, eating an apple, on a worse day than this one. Skylar said that would be the brandy kicking in.

"Good to see you," Odalys said. "Sit down, sit down. Would you like something to drink? Brandy?" Anders said he was fine, thank you. Odalys or Miss Praed, he said, whichever she preferred. Skylar asked for water with a slice of lime. When Odalys turned to Caspian and asked if he'd like a drink, he said, "No, thank you."

There was some polite talk while the drinks came, and she was good at it, warm and unhurried, like an aunt asking after your schooling. How were we finding Stoneharbour? A bit different from Amble, she imagined. We hadn't told her we came from Amble. In her work, she said, you needed to know a few things. Anders said Stoneharbour was a fine town, an oasis, a metropolis. Odalys said she didn't expect we'd be in it much longer.

"Why?" Anders said. "Have you heard something?"

"You're guild members," she said. Guild members only lasted two or three years in one place before their contracts were bought. Other branches bought them up when they had places to fill, often after somebody died, and Stoneharbour was where the guild sent all its newcomers to learn the ropes.

Anders said he didn't think any other branch would want to buy our debts. "Oh, there's always someone," Odalys said. "And I hear your rates aren't too expensive."

Then she got down to business. Her business was really with Anders and Moss, she said, though there was something for Caspian too. Moss had a modest debt with them. Anders had a small one. "A hundred and twenty or something?"

"That is correct, Miss Praed," said Anders.

"Here is some good news," she said. "We're not like the guild. We don't make you work off your debt over many, many years. We expect a much faster return." They were always looking for help collecting on debts, she said, a bit of muscle. There was a military presence in town, and there was always an opportunity to help them deal with it, either supply or demand. When we were less busy, we should come back and pick up a few jobs.

"That sounds like a great plan," said Moss.

Then she turned to Caspian. "I believe you've heard from your little tiefling friend," she said. A contract had been authorised against guild members. It was against him.

Caspian had heard Wren's warning along with the rest of us, a few days before, but nobody had told him it was him, and it showed on his face. "Pardon?" he said. "What? Sorry?"

Odalys explained it. Anyone who wanted to do something to a guild member had to get her authorisation first, because there was an understanding with the guild. When she authorised it, she had to tell Slate. Slate had received the notice. It wasn't necessarily an assassination. It could be any sort of contract.

"I suspect we can figure out who," Moss said.

"I can rescind that authorisation," Odalys said.

"How much is it going to cost him?" Anders asked.

"Ah, you're very astute. Something appropriate. Maybe two hundred gold."

Skylar asked what had been authorised against Caspian. Odalys said she couldn't divulge that. If we wanted to know, we would have to see Slate. Caspian had watched her face closely while she named the price, and he could tell she was starting high, like a market woman with a stranger. Everybody knew Anders liked to open a negotiation by calling the other side incompetent. Skylar said that never ended well.

"It ends in not being able to eat," said Anders.

Odalys made another, gentler offer. She would put the contract on hold for a week, for ten gold. Caspian didn't take it.

"Two hundred gold," he said. "Let's be honest, that's just ridiculous."

"We have expenses."

"What expenses?" He was a good person, he said. He didn't have that much money and never would. They were wasting each other's time. Odalys said he could always take the same offer as his friends here and work off his debt. Anders murmured, "One of us," twice.

"I am not worth two hundred gold," Caspian said.

"If you're such an expert on how much things should cost," Odalys said, "what do you think it should cost?"

"Fifty. Without a problem."

She looked pained, as if he'd said something faintly rude about her mother. It was a very expensive service, she said. There was a lot of paperwork involved. She couldn't possibly part with it for less than a hundred and fifty. Then she looked at him for a while longer, tapping one finger on the desk, and said she appreciated where he was coming from. She thought they could agree on ninety gold.

"If that's what it costs," Caspian said, "that's what it costs. All right. Fine. Done." He paid her there and then.

"That is a very good purchase," Odalys said. She wrote something with a fancy-looking pen, in writing we couldn't read upside down, and rang a little bell. A man came in. "Can you take this, please, and action it?" He took the paper and went. "Quickly," she called after him. "It's time sensitive."

Moss had one question, he said, before his fourth brandy. Since he was more or less in her debt now, could he walk through the Ropewalks without getting knifed? If he was on guild business, Odalys said, or on business of hers, and wore the token, yes. Otherwise the same rules applied, and he was best not to be there at all.

"Why would you want to be here otherwise?" she asked.

"Short cut," said Moss.

Anders asked what happened to our debts if we were killed. Moss asked what happened to them if the guild sold us on. "That's a very good question," said Odalys. "Don't worry about it. We're used to it. You're not the first guild members to get yourselves in trouble."

"Has Slate ever been on this side of the table?" Moss asked.

"I couldn't possibly say." She smiled. "We do like to work with people who understand how we work."

Anders wondered aloud whether, if we proved we were worth our debts and paid them back quickly, it might be in her organisation's interest to have our indentures transferred to her. Moss thought it might be a faster way to pay off what we owed. It didn't work that way, Odalys said, unfortunately. They were always looking for people to help. Skylar spent the whole of this exchange staring at the pair of them as if she could kill them with it.

As we stood to go, Odalys gave Anders a pass. He, Archer and Moss had been barred from the Ropewalks since the business at the tannery, but he had a debt here now, so he would need to come and go. Caspian didn't need one; he was allowed in the Ropewalks anyway, because he hadn't killed anyone. Then she handed us a folded piece of paper. "Would you mind giving that to Slate for me?"

"I'll see you around," she said. "When you head back to the guild, I suggest you go through the northern gate, near Kingshold. Not your usual one. Just in case the message hasn't come through."

* * *

We went back by the northern gate. It was quite late in the evening by the time we reached the guild, and the yard was dark except for the lamp over the barracks door, with the cold coming up out of the flagstones. Moss still could not sleep.

Skylar had asked Slate about the pickup crew while we were eating Peter's food, and now she told us his answer. "You need to sort it out," Slate had told her. "That's why you're being paid. You've been in the town a fair while. If you need some help, I'm sure you know someone by now."

We did know someone. Anders said we could ask Bram and Wren. Skylar pointed out that Bram was meant to be at the shrine with her and Anders. Anders asked if we even needed him there.

"I'm coming," Bram said. "I'm not waiting there."

There was the question of walking our prisoners home. We talked it over in the yard, stamping our feet against the cold, too tired to think well and too wound up to go in. Straight down the main road was quickest. Anders didn't fancy marching them down the main road. Skylar said that at midnight nobody would see, and what was wrong with frog-marching people down the main road in silence? Bram had been sitting on the edge of the horse trough with his elbows on his knees, and at that he stood up, pleased with all of it. "Right, cool," he said. "Can't wait for tomorrow night."

Caspian had a better idea. We could take a wagon out of the stores, put the prisoners in the back and throw a tarp over them. A cart was fifteen gold, inside what we were allowed to draw, so he drew a cart and a mule, since Anders could not draw anything at all. We'd leave it somewhere out of the way, where nobody would see it, and bring the prisoners to it, and Caspian said we'd ask Wren to guard it.

Wren was not having it. "How am I going to guard a wagon? Have you seen how big I am?"

"Sit in the wagon," Anders told her. "You've got this."

"If someone comes along and wants the wagon, I'm not going to be able to do much, am I?"

"You just say, I'm a guild member, don't take this wagon."

Wren said no thank you. "I'll let you know if it goes," she offered instead.

Someone had to be good with a mule. Bram was, and Caspian offered to drive. Anders asked Bram whether he'd sit in the wagon while he and Skylar fetched the smugglers.

"What?" said Bram.

"We'll need help with the wagon," Anders said. Bram did not look convinced.

So that was what we took to bed with us: a cart, a mule, a tarp, the guild's manacles, and Bram, who could not wait. It felt like enough. We have thought since that a good part of being nearly grown is that everything feels like enough the night before, when you are cold and tired and want the talking to be over.

* * *

The next day Anders went down to the stores, to see whether Hobb had told everybody. Fred was behind the counter.

"Anders," Fred said. "How are you? You've been making friends and influencing people, I hear."

Anders said he was just there for some lamp oil, if he could.

"Absolutely. Just let me check your authorisation level." Fred smirked as he turned the pages, slowly, one at a time. "Looks like there's a problem here. The records say you're grade zero. You've got no authorisation."

"I don't need authorisation to take out lamp oil," Anders said, "according to the latest revised schedule of requisitions."

"Nobody needs authorisation for that," said Fred. "But you're practically not a guild member, when it comes to stores. I'd love to help you, but I won't be able to. Sorry." He brightened. "I hear you're helping me out next week, though. That should be exciting."

"Looking forward to it. What day are we doing that?"

"Not sure. Hobb's still working it out."

"I will wait to be informed by Hobb, then."

"Lovely seeing you again," Fred said.

Caspian, meanwhile, took Praed's paper up to Slate. Slate unfolded it, read it and said, "Ah. Good work." It was a short note to say the contract against Caspian had been rescinded. What the contract had been, nobody asked, and Slate didn't say.

* * *

We went down to the Low Quay late that night. We expected the light at about eleven.

Moss and Caspian went first, hours early, and found themselves a place in the dark under the jetty, where they meant to stay until a boat came in. In the end it was Skylar who sat in the wagon with the mule. We parked it close to the shrine but out of sight, in a side street where a cart would not be noticed. Anders and Bram had the manacles, and their hoods up and scarves over their faces. They waited by the wagon in the shadows to see who came out of the shrine. They hid well enough. We have never been able to agree, since, whether the light flashed before anyone came out. It was what we had been waiting for, and it may be that some of us only remember it because we were waiting for it.

Ysolt and Nessa came out of the shrine door in a hurry.

They were walking far too fast to creep up on, and Bram's chain shirt chinked with every step he took. The street was empty. There was no one to see.

"Bram," Anders said, "let's just chase and tackle them."

They ran out of the dark at the two of them. Anders went for Ysolt and missed her completely. Bram went for Nessa and brought her down hard on the cobbles.

"Nessa, run!" Ysolt shouted.

"No!" Nessa was flat on the ground with Bram on top of her. "I won't leave you!"

Ysolt had a quarterstaff, and she hit Anders with it twice, fast, one blow and then another. They were not an old woman's blows. Nessa twisted out of Bram's grip, and he caught her a rough knock as she pulled away, then got hold of her again and pinned her down.

Anders slipped away from Ysolt, knelt and put the manacles on Nessa while Bram held her. She fought it, and couldn't get free. Ysolt swung at Anders again and missed, then caught him another, lighter blow, as if she didn't mean to break anything. "Get away!" she shouted. "Who are you?"

"Wouldn't it be easier," Bram said, "if we knocked them out?"

He punched Nessa in the face. Anders punched her too. She was still awake. Nessa screamed "Run!" right at Anders, and the word pushed at him the way an order does, and he stayed exactly where he was. Ysolt said something under her breath and swung the staff at his head, and missed.

Bram hit Nessa again. She went down and stayed down. She was breathing.

We were nearly eighteen. Nessa was sixteen.

Bram straightened up and turned towards Ysolt. Anders had a pair of manacles in his hand. "Put these on," he told her, "otherwise we kill her." Ysolt looked at the girl on the ground.

"You heard us," Anders said. "She's dead."

Ysolt believed every word of it. She still didn't put the manacles on.

She said a few words instead, and spirits came out of nowhere and began to fly round her in a ring. They were friendly-looking things, bright and kind in the face, and they burned. Anders felt one go through him like a hot iron laid flat against his skin. Bram, closer, took the worst of it. Ysolt had something crumpled in her hand, like a bit of paper. "You better run," she said. She looked angry now. She wasn't watching either of them. She was watching the girl.

Bram had seen Ysolt helping people all over the Low Quay, and anyone watching his face could tell he knew she was not a bad person. He hit her anyway, square in the face, as hard as he had ever hit anyone. Her head went back. The spirits stayed. She did not look at him. Her eyes went past him to Anders, and all her anger was for Anders.

Anders ran back towards the wagon. He turned and threw a blade of nothing but thought, which grazed her, and then he kept running. The spirits burned Bram again.

Nessa's eyes came open, and she lay there staring. Ysolt pointed at Bram. "Approach," she said, in a voice that expected to be obeyed. Bram shook it off. Then she turned her back on him and knelt to see to Nessa. Bram swung at her and missed.

So Bram did the only thing he could think of. He grabbed Nessa and ran.

Ysolt caught Nessa's leg as she went. For a moment they had her between them, Bram pulling one way and the old woman hanging on to the other. Bram was stronger. But the spirits went through him a third time, and he went down in the street and didn't get up.

Anders thought about telling Skylar to drive the wagon over the lot of them. He didn't. Skylar would never have run down Bram, whatever he'd done.

Ysolt knelt over Bram. She pulled his hood back to see his face, and put her hands on him until his breathing steadied. Then she helped Nessa, still in our manacles, back inside the shrine. She left Bram lying on the street. Anders stayed where he was and watched all of it. He did not try to stop her.

When the door had shut, he went and got Bram. He dragged him, very awkwardly, back to the side street. Skylar brought the cart round, and between them they got Bram into the back. Skylar thought she might have a healer's kit somewhere. Bram was breathing.

* * *

Moss and Caspian had been under the jetty a long while by then. It was cold and wet down there, and they had been waiting all that time for boats, meaning to hit one before it reached the jetty. From the water they could see nothing of what happened at the shrine.

As the wagon turned away, Anders looked back. The light at the shrine was flashing.

He sent his thoughts down to Moss. *Did not go well. Lighthouse flashing. We're compromised. Maybe best to abort.* Then he added that Bram had punched Nessa out, but she was back inside.

"I'm not surprised," said Moss, to Caspian.

There were no boats. There were no boats anywhere on the water, and there were not going to be. Moss sent that back up the same way, and in the wagon, with Bram's head on a folded tarp, Anders said it aloud to Skylar. "She's flashed the warning light." The light had not been calling the boats in. It had been sending them away.

The rest of it went back and forth by thought, a sentence at a time across the dark between the wagon and the jetty, with Moss passing on to Caspian whatever came down to him and sending back whatever Caspian said. Moss suggested a second option, which was that the two of them mount an assault on the shrine. Anders pointed out that we had talked this plan through together, and everybody had agreed to it, and Ysolt was more than any of us could handle. Caspian said she was the most capable person we'd met outside that warehouse. Everyone else down there was only ordinary people.

*Who's thinking,* Anders sent, *that my original suggestion is not a bad one now?* Invite them all to dinner, he meant. Knock them out at the guild.

"I'm done for the night," said Caspian, under the jetty, and Moss sent it on.

*Goodnight,* Moss sent.

We went back to the guild in two lots, by different streets, and got Bram to someone who could mend him. The yard was dark and empty. The lamp was still burning over the barracks door, and nobody who mattered had waited up for us. We had caught nobody. We had needed four. We had stopped the smuggling for one night, by assaulting an old woman and the girl she was teaching. Ysolt had seen Bram's face. We were sure she would want to know who the rest of us were, and that Jory would hear of it. We lay in our bunks and listened for the barracks door, and nobody came through it.

* * *

Slate sent for us early the next morning.

Nobody had come after us in the night. No boats had come in to the jetty. When we got in, nobody was waiting for us, and nobody seemed to care. Bram had gone to the infirmary. The rest of us went up to Slate's office and stood in a row in front of his desk with our hands behind us, as we had stood for his briefing.

Slate had a sheaf of papers in front of him, and he was going through them one at a time. He did not raise his voice. He never did.

"I don't think there's much need for you to tell me what went down," he said. "I've got a few reports here." He turned one over. "From the Low Quay. Already, at this hour of the morning. Impressive." He read a line or two more. "They are saying a girl of sixteen was beaten in the street, and that the old woman who looks after half that district was struck for her trouble. They know it was you. They did not like you before."

He squared the papers. "You brought me the smuggling, and I told you it was good work, and I meant it. You won't be getting a bonus for this one, obviously."

"What about a pro-rata bonus?" said Anders.

"Pro-rata zero against any sum is still zero."

"Luckily," Slate went on, "I believe contract season is coming up. With any luck I may be able to get rid of you."

"You don't want us to go," said Anders.

Slate let that pass. "I won't put you down as a failure, because that makes it harder to get rid of you. I can't quite rightfully put you down as anything better. So we'll put something in between." He looked at us over the papers. "And we'll both agree, I think, pretty much never to talk about this again. Shall we?"

We agreed.

"Obviously," he said, "you'll probably want to stay out of the Low Quay."

"Really?" said Moss.

"So we stay out of the Low Quay," said Anders, "and you won't put this on our guild record."

"Oh, you can do whatever you want. I'm only suggesting it, for your own health and safety." He went back to his reports. "And I don't know what you're talking about."

"Right," said Anders. "Excellent. Well, I'll see you later. Much later."

At the door he remembered, aloud, that he had some cleaning work to do in the stores. Three days this week, he believed. Slate said he would leave him to sort that out with whoever it was, and did not look up.

We have thought about that meeting a good deal since. Slate was upset with us in the dry, professional way of a man who has written the same report on you before and expects to write it again, and who would very much like the next one to be somebody else's job. We went out into the yard and stood in the sun, and nobody said a word about the shrine. We had agreed not to.
