# Chapter 10b — Tea Leaves

We woke late the next morning, which turned out to be the most sensible thing any of us did all day.

Nobody had slept more than a few hours. We had spent the night in a blind watching boats, and the evening after that arguing about them, and by the time we sat down to talk it over the morning was mostly gone. Moss came in last, smelling faintly of smoke. Nobody asked.

We had two leads. One was the warehouse in the Low Quay, where the crates from the landing had gone. The other was the tavern in the Ropewalks, where Caspian had watched a wagon unload. Caspian favoured the warehouse. If a few of us made a distraction, he said, one of us could slip in at the back and look around, and he had taken up the shape of a rat for the purpose, or a mouse, something small enough that no one would look twice. Moss said he thought we were back at the wrong tree. But if that was where the party was heading, he would come.

We went over what the merchants wanted, since it had drifted a little in the telling. The merchants suspected charms. They wanted to know who was bringing them in, when the next shipment would land, and where it all came from. It was not enough to find out what was in the crates.

The Ropewalks were a problem. We were known there, though not barred, and half of us were not welcome at all. Three could walk in and three could not. Moss was one of the three, for reasons everyone knew. Anders was another, and Archer a third, for reasons of their own, though Archer was laid up with a fever and wouldn't be going anywhere. Moss said he would go as a spider and ride on Anders, so that when it went wrong, as it usually did, Anders would have some backup. Caspian had offered Fenwick's hat. Moss preferred Anders.

We settled on the tavern after dark, dressed as ordinary young people out for a drink. Until then we had the whole day, and Skylar had an idea for it. Nessa was normally at Dela's at midday. We could go and have lunch and watch her. Nobody argued, least of all Moss, who had had the night he'd had and was not about to turn down food.

* * *

We kept our eyes open on the way, in case anything wanted noticing. Nothing did.

At Dela's we found the usual faces. Dela was behind the counter and Toby was wiping tables. Nessa was carrying plates, and beside her was Cass Tiller, who had become Nessa's friend since we'd last paid attention. We made sure that Nessa did not bring us our food. She was making sure of the same thing, and she gave several of us the cold shoulder as well. Caspian said he would leave her alone, and he did, very thoroughly, not saying a word.

Lunch was good. Afterwards we watched Nessa take off her apron, say goodbye to Dela and yawn, as anyone would after a late night on a riverbank.

Anders raised the subject of kidnapping, as Anders would. We might follow her home, he said, and take her out of her house after dark. Skylar said that would warn the whole ring as loudly as a bell. We would have to hold on to her somewhere afterwards, where she couldn't learn who we were, and it was not worth the trouble. The idea was put down again, not for the first time.

We were still talking when Skylar got up and left. She told us later that she'd said so, and that she'd stood up and walked out of the door a few steps behind Nessa. We had been too busy discussing kidnapping to notice. She followed Nessa through the streets towards the Low Quay, keeping well back. Nessa never looked round. At the end of the road she went into the Anchor Light, the shrine whose lamp had flashed across the water the night before, where Ysolt was keeper. Skylar found a shady spot out of the way, opened her sketchbook, and settled in to see how long she would be.

Back at Dela's, Anders asked about the house where Nessa and Jory had lived. Somebody else was in it now. Then he asked Dela why Nessa looked so tired. It was her training, Dela said. Nessa was learning healing from Ysolt at the Anchor Light, and everyone knew that was what Ysolt was good at. She helped people.

Dela wanted to know if anyone had seen her leave, and then answered herself. Skylar had, and had gone after her.

We looked at the empty chair. Anders suggested, with some relish, that Nessa had finally kidnapped Skylar instead. Fenwick said Skylar was the one of us least likely to get into trouble, which on reflection was not much of a comfort. We paid Dela and went to find her.

* * *

The Low Quay was hot and bright. We came down the road in a loose bunch and did not see Skylar at first, because she had not been trying to be seen. Moss found her, sitting in the shade with her sketchbook on her knee, drawing the shrine door.

"Glad to see you," Anders said. "We thought you'd been kidnapped."

"If you hadn't been so busy talking about kidnapping people," Skylar said, "you might have noticed I left to follow a certain someone."

"To kidnap her?" one of us asked.

"To follow," Skylar said.

Nessa had gone up the tower with Ysolt, she told us, and was still up there. Nobody else had gone in. Skylar hadn't wanted to give herself away, so she had stopped to see how long Nessa would be and whether we would ever turn up. Moss offered to help her look even more suspicious, hanging about outside a door, and Skylar said she could manage that without training.

We argued the sensible thing to do. We could creep up and listen, or knock and ask. If we knocked, Anders said, they would simply stop talking. Moss could spider his way in. Fenwick offered to throw him at the wall and let him grip on from there, which Fenwick said he'd read about as a child. Then we noticed people coming and going from the door. Some of them limped in and walked out easily, so Ysolt was plainly everything Dela said. Anyone could walk in, and we hadn't done anything to the shrine.

So the plan became simple. We would go in and make a donation. Moss would ride on Anders' shoulder as a spider, and we would drop him somewhere inside, where he could listen. Then, with luck, nobody need kidnap anyone, and we wouldn't have to go to the Ropewalks either.

* * *

Fenwick and Anders went in, with Moss on Anders' shoulder, small and quiet.

Ysolt was just finishing with a woman, giving her advice about her children. She looked up as the woman left, saw the two of them and said, "Ah, I know you." We had been helping with the floods, she remembered, and had come to ask her about Jory. "I never forget a face. How can I help you both?"

"We just came in to make a donation," Anders said.

She pointed them to the donation box. Fenwick asked if there was an afternoon of work he might do for her, and Ysolt said he could sweep the shrine, and handed him a broom that was taller than he was. The courtyard would have to wait, she said. Her apprentice was training there. "She needs to concentrate," Ysolt said. "You know what teenage girls are like."

While this was going on, Anders walked over to the donation box and put Moss on the lid. A slot was wide enough for a spider. Moss went in.

Ysolt gave them a quizzical look. Anders stood there holding a broom, sweating, and told her the truth, or something near enough. They'd made the donation because they felt bad. They were friends with Jory now that he was in the guild, and he was in the guild partly because of them, so they wanted to make amends with Nessa.

"What did you do?" Ysolt asked.

"We had to help collect the funds," Anders said.

"Oh," she said. "You're the ones that injured Nessa."

"No, no," Anders said. "We're not the ones who injured Nessa."

"Didn't the ones who went to collect the funds hurt her?"

"That's true," Anders said, "but we're not the ones that injured Nessa."

"You broke into the house."

"Again, we're not the ones who broke into the house," Anders said, and added that they had made the donation and were happy with that.

Ysolt watched him for a long moment, and Fenwick began to look at the floor. Then she thanked them for their help and for the donation. "Maybe you should be on your way," she said.

"Are you sure?"

"Yes."

We came out believing nobody had caught us out. Ysolt, we have since decided, had watched Anders sweat over a broom and caught him out completely, and was too polite to say so.

* * *

The box was dark and smelled of metal. Moss counted what he could from where he sat. There were plenty of coppers and the odd silver, and he took none of it. He sat quietly among the coins until the voices outside had gone.

Out in the street, Fenwick and Anders told the rest of us they'd left Moss in the donation box. Anders thought they hadn't been found out, though Ysolt had asked why they were there at all. Anders said that was what came of sending in the two most tactful people we had.

Inside, Moss waited until nobody was near. Then he slipped out of the slot and worked out where the back of the shrine was. A door led to a courtyard, with a gap beneath it just wide enough, and he went under it. Nessa was out there with a quarterstaff, hitting a straw dummy over and over, in a yard about five paces square. Moss settled in a corner to watch a teenage girl train, and tried not to think about how that looked from outside.

He had no plan beyond that. Outside, Skylar sent a message to find out how he was doing. He told her he was fine and was just watching Nessa. She asked if he'd like to look around while he was in there, instead of only watching a girl exercise. He said he hadn't come in with a plan. "Hint, hint," Skylar said. "Nudge, nudge." Moss said fine.

There was a set of stairs, and he went up. Another set rose further on, and rooms opened off the hallway, left and right. Below, next to the courtyard, he found a kitchen. He found a cellar door and went down to look at what was kept there. It was ordinary stores, casks and jars and sacks. He found no crates and nothing like a hidden room. He went back the way he came, crept out through the front, and climbed up onto Anders' shoulder.

"Anders," Skylar said, "you have a spider on you."

All of us saw him. Anders did not swat him, which we thought was generous.

* * *

It was early afternoon, about two o'clock. We'd had lunch, and then spent an hour being turned out of a shrine, and the day still stood open before us.

We had found where Nessa trained. It was not a secret, and never had been. Moss had gone through the shrine in the thorough way of a spider and found nothing, and when Skylar asked what he'd been looking for, he said that was what he had wondered the whole time. He wasn't good on the spot.

"Note to self," Skylar said. "Don't send Moss in as a spy."

"Moss seconds that," Moss said.

We still had to decide what to do with the afternoon. The Ropewalks would wait for dark, and we meant to go to the tavern like young people on a night out. Skylar remarked that this had worked so well for us at taverns in the past. Fenwick said we could scout the place first, and come back at night when we knew where the doors were. Caspian said, why not look at the warehouse where the shrine's crates had gone? It was only a couple of streets away.

So we walked there, Moss on a shoulder, and nobody noticed us follow.

* * *

There was a queue outside the warehouse. It was a short one, made up of women and fishing folk and no children, and one at a time they went in through the front door and came out a few minutes later with a bundle or a sack or a small box. They were not hiding any of it. Nobody looked behind them.

"We could just line up," Caspian said. He looked at Fenwick. We had decided Fenwick would do it, because he was the one we could most easily spare. We said so, out loud, and regretted it within the hour.

Before Fenwick got in line, Skylar spotted a man coming out, and knew him. It was Halder Kest, the man who had been called a thief at the sandbag wall on Cutter's Lane during the flood, when all he had been trying to do was save his own house. He knew her in return. "You're one of the ones who helped me with the sandbags," he said.

Skylar said she hoped things were going well since the flood, and that he looked well. He had some supplies, he said. Just stuff. He opened his bundle and showed her fishing hooks and line.

Skylar said it looked as though he was set up for some good fishing, then, and that she wouldn't hold him up any longer. It was good to see him doing well. Halder thanked her and went on his way.

Once he'd gone, Skylar admitted she hadn't been sure what charm stuff was, and had been picturing fishing lures. Anders asked if she'd thought they were spell-charms. Fancy lures could get quite grand, she said, and a magic one might be rather worth having. We told her what charms were: little amulets and necklaces that people wore, as anyone in Stoneharbour would have known who had been paying attention. That made a good deal more sense than lures.

Fenwick had lined up. About fifteen minutes later he reached the front. The man at the door asked who he was and what he wanted, and Fenwick said he'd come for one of the items he'd ordered. Which item? "One of the more sensitive ones," Fenwick said, "if you know what I mean."

The man called, "Blake!" through the door. Another man came out, looked Fenwick up and down, and frowned. "I don't think I was dealing with Blake," Fenwick said, and Blake said he didn't recognise him. "My name's Benwick," Fenwick said. "I thought I placed an order."

The rest of us were standing a long way back, trying to look as though we had no connection to anything. We watched Fenwick go in through the door with Blake behind him, being shown the way by a man who was very polite about it. Blake opened an inner door for him, and the door swung shut.

Skylar sent a message. "Ben, what's going on?" There was no answer. She kept the message open, and waited.

* * *

Blake led him into an office. He took a small wooden box from a shelf crowded with kitchen things, jars and spices and bits of crockery, and put it on the table. "That's for Benwick, yeah?" he said. Fenwick said yes. Blake said he should have a look and make sure he was happy, since he'd paid a lot of money.

Fenwick sweated, and opened the box. It was full of tea leaves.

When he looked up, the door had been shut and Blake was leaning against it. "Now," Blake said, "would you like to tell me who you are? We can do this the easy way or the hard way."

"What do you mean?" Fenwick said. "The easy way or the hard way?"

"Would you like me to show you the hard way?"

"Not really," Fenwick said. He said he'd only come for an order. He added that it was obvious what went on here, with all those people going in and out; they were helping the community. But he was aware there were other shipments that came here, and he was rather more interested in those. He'd never been sure there was an order for Benwick, now he thought about it.

"You can tell me who you are," Blake said, "and who sent you, and why you're here." Then he punched him.

"None of the other customers were treated like this," Fenwick said. "Why did you hit me?"

Blake hit him again. Outside, about five minutes had gone by, and Skylar couldn't bear it any longer and sent a message. *What's going on?* Fenwick sent one back. He was being beaten, he said, quite thoroughly, and the beating was not very nice. Skylar told the rest of us we needed to send in reinforcements.

The door had been shut on the warehouse itself, and nobody would let us near it. Moss said he would go as a spider again and try to sneak in, but there were people standing right in front of the line, and he could not get by them. Anders offered to make a distraction. Caspian said a cat was the smallest he could manage just then, which was not small enough to slip in anywhere, so he would go up and knock on the door. While everyone looked at the knocking, Moss went under it.

In the office Fenwick said he'd hit back, and did. He had nothing but his fists. It was not a great deal to hit with. He swung, and landed one, and it did almost nothing. Blake took it with a look of mild interest and hit him twice more.

Fenwick dug in. He threw everything he had left into the next blow, and missed completely, and Blake hit him in the stomach so hard he doubled over. Blake stood back and waited while he got his breath.

"Do you want to talk yet?" he asked.

"You tell me why you're hitting me," Fenwick said. "You aren't telling me anything either."

Moss had slipped past a man standing at the office door and under it. Inside, a large man stood in front of Fenwick, saying, "Are you going to tell me what's going on, or are you going to get a bit more of this?" Fenwick looked hurt, but not too hurt. Moss decided to let it play out for a moment and see what he did.

Fenwick asked what was in that box, as though he didn't know. "It's tea, you idiot," Blake said.

"I know there was more coming in the other night," Fenwick said, "and I want in."

"Go for it, then," Blake said. Fenwick swung, and this time he connected. Blake seemed not to notice. He hit Fenwick again, and again, and when it stopped Fenwick was still on his feet, with a thin line of blood coming down his face.

* * *

Moss took his own shape back, slowly and with reluctance. He slipped between them and came up to his full height, and Blake said, "Who the hell are you?"

Moss hit him with his staff, clean and hard, then reached out for the goat that wasn't there. Brambleback was at home. Moss had been told to leave him at home, and it was the one time that day he had done as he was told. Blake said, "You guys pissed me off," and pulled a cudgel out from under his coat.

"Jack!" he shouted. "Get in here!"

Outside, Caspian sent word to Moss. *All good?* "No," Moss said. Caspian said we should race for the door and bust it in, or at least make a noise. Inside, Moss had become a brown bear, and he was biting and clawing at the man in front of him, and not very pleased about it.

The door of the office burst open, and a man came running in with his own cudgel, and shouted, "Bloody hell, a bear!" Jack and Blake went for Moss together. Fenwick kept on hitting the man he'd been hitting the whole time, because it was all he had. Moss's bear caught Blake with a bite and a long claw, and drew blood, and we were glad of it. He had had enough, he thought. "We should go," he said.

That was when Fenwick went down. A blow caught him in the middle of a swing, and he fell where he stood. The man who had done it looked none the worse for the bear.

Outside, we had reached the door. The people queuing told us to go to the back of the line and wait our turn. We pushed and pulled and slid and lifted, and the door would not open, however we tried it. "Who are you lot?" they said. "Wait your turn." Skylar said our friends hadn't come out, and we were trying to get to them. "Why would your friend be in there?" they said.

Inside, Moss bent to scoop up Fenwick, and fumbled him. He was a clumsy bear. Fenwick slipped through his arms and hit the floor, and Moss said something we are not going to repeat. Skylar sent him a message. *What's going on?* "Nothing good," Moss said. "Get in here."

"Moss is in trouble," Skylar said. "They both are." She told Anders to get us in, and not to burn down the warehouse doing it. A man at the door said, "Don't burn down the—who the hell are you?" Then somebody shouted, "Someone, quick, go and get Ysolt!" and the crowd started shoving. About ten of them pushed up against us, telling us to bugger off. Anders wanted to know if there was a back door. There was not. We had already looked.

Inside, Moss tried again. He lifted Fenwick this time, the bear's arms closing round him, and charged for the door. Jack and Blake each got a swing in as he passed, and both hit. They chased him, snapping at his heels. There was a table in the way, and doorways, and people, and a bear that could not shove past without knocking something over.

He was halfway to the door when he fell. It was not a dramatic fall. He just stopped, and went down on top of Fenwick, and the bear shrank away until there was only Moss, flat on the floor with his friend beneath him. Skylar's messages went unanswered. Neither of them was dying, thank God. The men had been fighting to hurt, not to kill, and had stopped short of it. But neither of them was getting up either.

* * *

Skylar tried the lock with a ghostly hand. It did nothing we could see.

The crowd was growing angrier. It was shoving and swearing at us, and it showed no sign of letting us near the door. Caspian asked whether Anders had no talent for locks, and Anders said he had plenty of talent for locks, just not in the middle of a mob. We would have to think of something.

Anders was small enough to go low. He ducked between legs and shoulders, wriggling through the gaps, and Caspian pushed the crowd back on either side to make room. It was undignified. But it worked, and he reached the door.

It was barred from behind. He took out a very small knife, which turned out to be exactly the right size, and slid it through the crack. He found the bar and lifted it, and it fell to the floor with a clunk that must have been heard in the next street. Then he pushed on the door, and it opened.

He could see two figures lying on the floor of the warehouse, Fenwick and Moss, with two men standing over them. Moss was himself again. The men looked up at the door as it swung open, since the bar had just crashed to the ground. Anders walked in, as confidently as he could.

"Who the hell are these guys on the ground?" he asked.

"Get lost," one of them said, "or else."

Anders said he'd been sent to come in. He had only opened the door. Behind him, Skylar was being kept out by the crowd, and Caspian was still pushing. One man came towards Anders, the one with the bear's bite on him.

Caspian called down rain. It fell in a sudden cold sheet over the crowd in front of the door, and several of them stood there stunned and dripping. It was a nuisance more than a storm. But a nuisance was what we needed, and while the crowd was shouting at the sky Caspian shoved through into the doorway.

Anders slipped around the man who had come at him and ran for the two on the floor. He grabbed the smaller one, which was Fenwick, and began to haul him towards the door. "I'm taking this guy with me," he said. The men said what we imagined they'd been saying all day, and struck him twice, hard. Anders let go. He ran to the back of the warehouse, shouting that they would never catch him, fatty. One of them shouted, "Jack! Get that guy and deal with him!" The other man stayed where he was, going through Fenwick's and Moss's pockets to learn who they were.

Anders ran in circles. Half a minute later Caspian came in off the doorway. "Stop robbing these people," he said. They asked who the hell he was. "Get lost," Caspian said, "before you make me angry." Then he knelt and laid a hand on Moss, and healed him as well as he could.

One of the men swung at him and caught him a heavy blow. Anders, hiding among the crates, said he would wait until Skylar was in and then bar the door behind her. Caspian made up his mind. He became a dire wolf, a huge grey beast, and bit one of them hard, though only to knock him down. The men had been fighting to hurt and not to kill, and he would not repay them with more.

Moss opened his eyes. He saw Fenwick on the floor beside him, picked him up, and stepped through the air. A moment later he was at the doorway, with Fenwick in his arms, which no one in that warehouse had expected. He woke Fenwick with a quick healing. "You're up," he said. "And I'm holding you."

The wolf took two hard blows. Fenwick tried to reach his healer's kit, but he couldn't while Moss was holding him, and Moss was not going to let go. "I say enjoy it," Fenwick said, and gave up.

* * *

Moss stepped through the air again, out of the warehouse door and into the street. He meant to keep walking, quietly, away from the crowd. He missed his footing and fell flat on his face, shouting, with Fenwick still in his arms.

A good many heads turned. "Who's that guy?" someone said. A few of them started towards him. "Oops," Moss said. "We appear to have entered a bad moment."

Inside, Caspian let go of the man he was fighting and made for the door, and the crowd was still thick there. Skylar had been trying to think who could help. Were there any guards in this part of town? They came through now and then, but the Low Quay was never well patrolled, and nobody cared much what happened there. There were no guard posts. There was a shrine, and nothing else.

She saw Moss come out with Fenwick. So she did the only thing left: she began to walk slowly away from the door, taking the crowd with her. People drifted after her. The doorway thinned. Caspian shoved his way out of it.

Then a wolf came out of the warehouse, and people shouted. They scattered, naturally. Nobody tries to grab a dire wolf. Skylar kept walking, and the crowd lost interest in her. "Right," she said. "Everyone out. Get out."

We scattered to the wind. Anders was last through the door. Two men grabbed for him in the crowd, and he twisted out of their hands like an eel, using the small size that had been such a nuisance to him all his life. He slipped between them, past the wolf's empty place, and out. Moss picked Fenwick up again and ran into the shadows, still stumbling.

Nobody had died. Nobody had been taken. Nobody was hurt badly enough to die of it. It was the best result any of us could have asked for, and we got it by accident.

* * *

Anders lost the men behind him in the merchants' quarter. They didn't follow him that far, and with a little running and a little care he left them behind. After that he walked back through the crowds like anyone else, and he was a little hurt and a little tired. He was nearly at the guild gate when a hand clapped down on his shoulder.

It was Peter, with a couple of men behind him. "Silas said we could find you skulking about the guild," Peter said.

Anders said, "Silas?"

It was Silas, we worked out later, from the tavern. He was the man Anders had taken a sliver from, a long time ago, in exchange for a story that was not true. The underworld knew about it. That was how Peter's people had recognised Anders and no one else. He was the best known of us down there.

"Come and have a drink," Peter said.

It was not an invitation. But he led Anders to a tavern, a perfectly decent one, a short walk away. He sat him down and bought two ales.

"Now," Peter said. "Who are your friends?"

"Acquaintances," Anders said.

"We were down at the river mouth last night," Peter said.

"It's a shame the only one we know is you." Peter turned his mug. "There was a box that went missing. You wouldn't happen to know anything about that?"

Anders did not know anything about a box. He knew there had been boxes, and that Peter's men had knocked him out. He asked if Peter thought he was in a state to carry one off. Peter said probably not, but one of his friends might have been.

"Then tell us the names of your friends," Peter said.

Anders gave him the people we knew in town, the harmless ones, Dela and Nessa and Ysolt. He gave him Fred, who worked in the guild stores and would, we hoped, forgive us. He did not give him Moss.

"Here's what we'll do," Peter said. "You bring us the brandy and the tobacco, and we'll let it slide. If you don't, there's going to be a problem."

Anders sat very still. Then he remembered, with the sudden clearness of a man who has been hit on the head, that Moss had smelled of cigars that morning, and Moss did not smoke. Moss had not slept in the barracks last night either.

"We know where you are," Peter said. "We know who you are. We know you aren't leaving."

"And if I find the person who took it, what will you do to them?"

"They give back the gear."

"What if they can't give back the gear?"

"They pay for it."

"How much?"

"Six hundred."

"Silver pieces?" Anders asked.

Peter laughed at him. Gold, of course. Anders said the guild already owned him for life, so he could take out a loan of six hundred gold and give it to Peter, to leave his friend alone. "We don't care how you find it," Peter said. "We want the gear."

He finished his ale, patted Anders on the shoulder, and left with his men. At the door he turned and called back, "Hope you get those smugglers with their charms."

Anders finished his own ale, slowly. Then he went back to the guild.

* * *

We were all there by then. Fenwick was awake, with a swollen face and a great deal to say. He'd only asked for the special goods, he told us, and they had seen through him almost at once. He had at least not given them his real name. Caspian said there must be a great many Fenwicks in Stoneharbour. Fenwick pointed out that there were rather fewer halflings, and fewer still who arrived with a giant and a wolf.

Anders asked if anyone had noticed anything in that warehouse, besides Fenwick being beaten. There'd been crates, Fenwick said, and plenty of them. Most of it was fishing tackle, hooks, line, lead weights, and there were chests of tea leaves. "Bloody tea," he said. The import fee on that sort of thing was heavy, and it was cheaper to buy elsewhere, so it had very likely come in the same way as everything else. It wasn't what we were looking for, but it was still smuggled. Skylar said we could report it.

"And you know what?" Anders said. "Nobody died."

"For a change," Fenwick said.

"Either us or them," Anders said.

Anders looked as if he'd swallowed something sour. "Just some bad decisions catching up with me," he said, when Skylar asked. Those men from the Ropewalks, who had knocked him out and left him for dead, thought somebody had taken a box from them, and they thought it was him. They wanted it back.

"Why would they think you'd know?" Skylar asked.

"Because I'm the only one they know," Anders said.

We looked at each other. Moss said nothing at all. It was a very deliberate not-saying. Somebody asked where everyone had been during the landing. Fenwick had been among the first to run for the wagon. Then, at last, Moss spoke. He thought it might have been Bram, he said. Bram had come with us that night.

It was a very bad lie. Caspian watched Moss's face and said that Moss had told it badly. Somebody said that someone was about to blame Bram, and nobody believed it for a second.

"I had an opportunity," Moss said at last. "I took an opportunity."

"So you took the crate Anders has been blamed for?" somebody said.

"I like brandy," Moss said.

It was good brandy. He said that, too. He had not slept, and could not sleep, and was perfectly well in every other way. Skylar suggested, in the most reasonable voice, that Anders might want to give them Moss's name at this point, since Moss was so obviously prepared to put him under the cart. "I'm no snitch," Anders said. He had given Peter the random people we knew in town, and Fred, and Peter hadn't bought it. But he had not said Moss.

Anders told us that Peter's last words had not sounded sarcastic. *Hope you get those smugglers with their charms.* He was nearly sure Peter had meant it. We could not make that fit with anything we'd assumed since the landing, so we filed it under jibes. We didn't know yet that it was the truest thing anyone had said to us all day.

And then there was the brandy. Six hundred gold was a great deal to ask for brandy and cigars, however good. Anders told us he had drunk a sliver once, the one he'd taken from Silas, and it had made him feel on top of the world and as if he'd never need sleep again. "You're drinking people's souls," Caspian said. "It was good brandy," Moss said. Somebody pointed out that a sliver did not have to be a person's soul. It could be a beast's. It could be many things.

We could take it all to Slate. He would probably say it was not our job, since the merchants paid us for the charms, though he might find a use for it. Then somebody thought of the sandbag man with his fishing gear. Could the tackle be hiding the charms? It was only fishing gear in a box, Moss pointed out, and not loose. Which meant there could be more in the box, or inside the tea. We should go back to Halder's house and see.

"Can we have a nap first?" someone asked.

Somebody told him he couldn't. He was wired.

* * *

Caspian went up to the druids, in the early evening, for a little mending. It was a small comfort, but he came back standing a little straighter.

We had to decide what to do with the evening. Halder's house was the obvious thing, but Halder looked after a great many children, we remembered, which was why he'd been so desperate to sandbag it. We could hardly go barging in. Caspian said he could become a cat and have a look around. If he left it late, the children would be asleep, and he could creep about at midnight.

Before that we asked ourselves what to take back to Peter. There was nothing. Moss had smoked all the cigars and drunk all the brandy. What was left was an empty box and an empty bottle, somewhere in the woods, and they were worth six hundred gold. Anders had not tried to bargain at the time. We agreed, in a way that nobody enjoyed, that the debt was Moss's. The rest of us were on the hook with him.

That night Caspian, as a cat, went into Halder's little house. In the shed behind it he found a pouch of fishing tackle that looked as if it had never been used. He searched the house. It held nothing suspicious at all. On a hook beside the front door hung a charm, an old one, much handled and worn smooth, hung there by someone who had owned it for years.

* * *

We had no leads. We had a guild-mess supper behind us and nothing in front. The shrine had held nothing of note, healing charms of the plain sort, and none of the sailors' kind we were looking for. There were legitimate charms, which came through the merchants, and others that skipped the merchants' charge, and a good charm was worth fifteen gold. Fishermen bought them every season, to keep them from falling and drowning, and they did not come cheap.

Someone observed that the guild cared a great deal about a fifteen-gold charm missing its duty, and not at all about six hundred gold of slivers going down Moss's throat. At the guild, morals mattered less than the contract.

The ideas came in a heap. We could take six hundred gold to Peter and ask him about the smugglers, if we ever found six hundred gold. We could take out a second loan, and learn that the guild did not allow it. We could ask the merchants who had stopped buying charms. We could go back to the beginning, and kidnap Nessa. Moss wanted to go and find Jack, or Blake, whichever of them it had been that hit Fenwick so hard, and Skylar reminded him that it had been two on one and that Fenwick had been knocked out, and he might want to reconsider.

Slate, we knew, would ask the one thing we could not answer, which was when the next shipment was coming. We still had no idea.

* * *

Wren found us at the guild that evening. She looked us over once, and said we looked a bit beaten up. She had heard there'd been a commotion down at the Low Quay, she said, and from the look of us she could guess who it involved.

She had been down there asking. She had a meeting that night, with contacts, she said, and would not say more. We could not think what contacts she meant. She went off before we could ask.

When she came back, she had it. She'd found out what they were doing. They smuggled in the cheap goods first, fishing tackle and the like, to see whether the route was safe. The charms would come on the next shipment, which was tomorrow night. They were bound to be nervous after today, and there would be more men guarding it.

"The charms are here already?" one of us asked.

"The shipment's waiting to be delivered," Wren said. "The ship should be arriving." That was how she put it.

Then we understood. The wagon at the river mouth, with Peter and his crossbows, and the warehouse full of tackle and tea, had both been smugglers, and neither was the one we'd been hired to find. It was nobody's fault but bad luck, since Slate had sent us to a landing where two lots happened to come in on the same night. We had learned a good deal, though not what we were meant to.

Skylar said that next time we saw Slate, she was going to be perfectly frank. For the stealthy jobs, send Wren. For the killing jobs, send us.

* * *

Slate heard us in his office, behind his desk. He asked if anyone wanted an apple. Moss took one. Slate listened to all of it, Wren's news too, with an expression nobody could read. When we'd finished, he said, "Good work."

"Rest up tomorrow," he said. "If they're landing tomorrow night, I need you to do the sting."

"Are you sure?" somebody said. "We tried to do a sting, and we got the stuffing knocked out of us."

"Do you need a warehouse swept?" Moss said. "We might not mess that up."

"We need to seize those charms," Slate said. "And I need you to get it done."

"I was afraid you were going to say that," somebody said. Skylar asked, "Is killing permitted?"

"You're some of the only people out of town," Slate said. "So you get the job."

"My question still stands," Skylar said. "Is killing permitted?"

"Of course not."

Skylar said she'd only wanted to be sure. Slate said he was well aware of that, because she had asked more than once.

* * *

We were told to sleep, and that was an instruction we all liked. It was night by then. The sting was tomorrow, not tonight, so there was nothing left but to lie down in our own bunks and let the day fall off us.

Everybody got a full night's rest except Moss.

He lay on his back and watched the beams. He felt fantastic. He wasn't tired at all, and nothing hurt, and he could have gone out and walked the Low Quay from end to end. Wide awake, he thought about brandy. People who took slivers, we gathered, did it a little at a time, when they needed an extra boost for a day. They did not drink a whole bottle, and smoke four cigars after, all in one night.

The lamps in the yard went out. Moss counted the beams, and then counted them again.

We slept. He didn't. It was the first thing that week none of us could blame on Anders.
