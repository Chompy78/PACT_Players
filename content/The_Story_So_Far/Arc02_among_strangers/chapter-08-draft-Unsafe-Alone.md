# Chapter 8 — Unsafe Alone

We picked up more or less where we'd left off, which was another way of saying we hadn't learned much. Two months went by the way months do once you've stopped counting them one at a time — in a blur of contracts and drills and small humiliations we were getting almost good at absorbing. We didn't know yet how much of what came after would trace back to a girl at the shrine nobody there could be bothered calling by her name.

The guild's arithmetic never stopped, even when nothing seemed to be happening. Cheap training, bigger debt. Faster progress, slower freedom. We'd worked out by then that the whole shape of it was a wheel, and that the trick — the only trick anyone ever really taught you — was to keep walking on it and call that living. Jory had joined properly since we'd last counted, indentured now same as the rest of us, and he'd asked, or been given, a bunk in a different barracks from Caspian's. Nobody blamed him for that. We didn't blame ourselves for it either, out loud, which was its own kind of answer. Every time we went to Dela's for lunch, Nessa still crossed herself, or whatever the shrine version of that was, the moment she saw Caspian's face in the doorway. Dela herself had thawed back to something like ordinary politeness. Nessa hadn't, and probably never would, and we told ourselves that was fair.

The rumours came the way rumours always did that winter — sideways, half-finished, passed on by people who'd only half-believed them themselves. Sena, missing since before the flood, had apparently been seen here and there around the town, though nobody who claimed to have seen her could ever quite say where. Factor Slate, our own branch factor, was rumoured to be moved on come spring, for reasons nobody offered and we didn't ask after. Up at Shrine Hill, some of the older keepers had started blaming the bad winter — the sickened trees, the thin tithes, the odd stray sheep taken in the night — on newcomers, outsiders, anyone who hadn't always been there. And down in the Ropewalks, people had started talking, low and careful, about slivers: a shortcut to power that didn't ask you to earn anything first, smuggled in off fishing boats after dark and sold to whoever was desperate enough or ambitious enough to want it. We didn't know yet what the word would come to mean to us. We only knew it made the guild uneasy, and the guild being uneasy about anything was itself worth remembering.

Wren, in among all of it, kept doing the quiet, useful thing she always did. She went over our accounts one evening, sifting for whatever a guild that size inevitably got wrong, and found a training fee still billed against Fenwick's name months after it should have stopped. She fixed it without being asked and without making a show of it. We thanked her. She shrugged it off, embarrassed by the thanks more than by the mistake itself.

We hadn't worked out yet how much that sharpness of hers — the noticing, the correcting, the quiet habit of being right about things other people would rather she wasn't — was going to cost her.

It was dusk when Wren came in at a near-run and told us the shrine's alarm bell was ringing.

* * *

Moss and Ordis knew the place better than the rest of us, and they talked about it the whole walk out — a charity shrine, they said, older than Stoneharbour itself, run on nothing but what people chose to leave behind them: a coin, a bundle of flowers, an afternoon's weeding. A tree-hugging sort of community, Moss called it, not unkindly, full of keepers who lived off their own gardens and mostly left everyone else alone to live off theirs. It sounded, from the outside, like the gentlest place in the world to have a crisis.

We arrived to find it wasn't, tonight. People were hauling livestock indoors at a run, shouting after chickens, and someone spotted Moss and Ordis and called out that a calf had been taken — dragged off and killed near the pens, fast enough that whoever heard the bell hadn't even had time to get outside before it was over. Moss went out to the pens and read what the ground told him: a single animal dragged a long way in a short time, torn apart by more than one set of teeth, the prints themselves too large and too many to belong to anything but a proper pack — somewhere between three and six of them, he guessed, and guessed right more often than any of us liked to admit.

We followed the trail out past the shrine's own fences and into the dark beyond it, torches up, Fenwick going first because he was the only one who genuinely liked not being able to see what was coming.

* * *

They came out of the darkness all at once, and only Caspian and Archer saw it coming in time to brace for it.

Caspian tried the gentlest thing first — words, calm ones, the kind that had worked on frightened animals his whole life — and for one full heartbeat it actually worked. The nearest wolves slowed, uncertain, ears back, deciding whether to believe him. Then something older and larger howled from further back in the dark, and whatever Caspian had built in that one heartbeat came apart at the root. The pack surged forward together, and this time there was no talking to any of it.

It was a bad fight before it was anything else. Archer and Fenwick went down hard early, blood already running by the time the rest of the party had even found their footing, and Caspian — closest to nothing, protected by nobody — took the brunt of it twice over, once early and once late, both times dropped low enough that the world narrowed to a single grey point and held there. Ordis found him the first time, palms glowing faint and warm against the cold, and dragged him back from wherever he'd been heading. The second time there was no time to wait for her. He pressed his own hands to his own ribs, the old trick his mentor had shown him for exactly this kind of stupid mistake, and felt the pain answer him back instead of swallowing him whole.

We fought scattered and desperate for what felt like a long time and probably wasn't. Fenwick, once he'd found his feet, hit harder and faster than any of the wolves expected out of something his size, and Anders went up a tree half by plan and half by panic and did more damage from a bad angle than anyone should have been able to. Archer's spellwork found its mark more than it missed. In the end it wasn't anything we did, in particular, that broke it — the pack simply decided, one after another, that it had had enough, disengaging and bolting back into the dark until only the sound of them was left. The last howl came from somewhere well beyond torchlight, and then that was gone too.

Nobody said very much, standing there afterward. We'd gone out to check a bell and nearly lost someone to a dog.

* * *

We rested only as long as we had to before Caspian, on his feet again and stubborn about it, said we should track them to wherever they'd gone to ground — if only to be able to tell the shrine, honestly, that the threat had moved on rather than simply scattered. Half an hour's tracking brought us to an abandoned den under a stand of trees, empty, the pack long gone. There were old bones scattered through it, and — more curious than anything — a handful of small, bright things the wolves seemed to have dragged in over time and kept, the way some animals apparently just did. Fenwick declared, with total conviction, that this made them thieves as much as predators, and nobody argued the point hard enough to spoil it for him.

We walked the hour and a half back to the shrine in the last of the night, arriving footsore and bled-on to a small, grateful crowd. The senior keeper who met us shook Caspian's hand like she meant it, and thanked Moss and Ordis by name for bringing help so fast. It should have ended there, warm and simple.

Archer was the one who noticed it wouldn't. He caught, at the edge of the gathering, one of the older keepers muttering something to a companion, low and unkind, and nodding toward Wren while he did it. Archer leaned in and told her quietly that the man didn't seem to like her much. Wren's face did something small and practised, the look of someone who'd already made peace with a fact she wished weren't true. She knew him, she said. She'd noticed him dipping his hand into the donation box more than once, and mentioned it — quietly, she thought, to one person she trusted — and somehow it had found its way back to him anyway.

Moss put himself between her and the muttering before he'd fully decided to. It didn't help. Wren slipped away from the group not long after, saying she'd go check on something, and by the time any of us thought to follow she was already gone into the dark on her own, which none of us liked and none of us stopped.

We didn't know yet how much that particular evening was going to cost her, or how soon.

* * *

We had a day off owed to us, and split it as we usually did once nobody could agree on a plan. Caspian, still carrying two days' worth of exhaustion in his bones, went looking for nothing more ambitious than a quiet coffee house with Archer, and found one — expensive, unremarkable, entirely free of gossip worth the price of the cup.

The rest of us went to check on old Sabine.

She'd sold off nearly everything since the flood — her nets, her tools, most of what had ever made her a net-mender rather than just a woman with an empty house — and she waved off our coin twice before finally, quietly, taking it. She had a plan, she told us, in the voice of someone who already knew we wouldn't like it: a woman up in the market paid good money for memories, no questions asked, and Sabine had nothing much left to lose that she valued more than staying fed. Fenwick spent half an hour trying to talk a local baker into taking her on instead, and failed kindly, the baker patient but unmoved. It wasn't much of a rescue. It was the only one on offer.

"You keep your money hidden, you hear me," Fenwick told her on the way out, and meant it more than the joke he wrapped around it.

Wren had come with us, quiet at the edges of it, and it was in the crush of the market afterward that we lost track of her for the length of about two streets. When we caught up she was standing very still, holding a scrap of paper someone had thrust at her and walked off without a word. It said, in plain, ugly letters, that tieflings weren't welcome at the shrine and shouldn't come back.

Moss read it over her shoulder and went very quiet in the particular way he did when he was angrier than he had words for yet.

"You may have brought some of this on yourself," he said eventually, not looking at her, "not being as careful as you thought you were being." It came out harsher than he meant it, and he heard it land that way, and didn't take it back so much as soften around the edges of it. "I mean go careful. Not go quiet. There's a difference, and you're allowed both."

Wren folded the note small enough to disappear into a pocket and said it probably wasn't worth taking seriously — whoever had handed it to her hadn't looked like a keeper, hadn't looked like anyone from the shrine at all. We let her believe that, mostly because none of us had anything better to offer her instead.

* * *

We were still curious enough, or reckless enough, to walk the Ropewalks together before the day was out, chasing the same rumour Wren had first handed Anders weeks before — crates coming ashore after dark, carried inland to somewhere nobody official wanted to look at too closely.

A boy not much older than us fell into step alongside Anders almost as soon as we'd crossed into it, smiling in a way that had nothing friendly behind it. He asked what a whole crowd of guild people thought they were doing down here, since they clearly hadn't been good enough for the neighbourhood the last time. He didn't waste the insult on all of us evenly — he saved most of it for Caspian, by name, in a way that told us the story of the dead child in this district had travelled a good deal further than we'd ever wanted it to.

We kept walking anyway, which was its own kind of mistake. A woman stopped us not much further on and told us, plainly and without much heat, to clear off before something happened that none of us would enjoy. We should have listened to her the first time. We listened properly a few minutes later, when a crossbow bolt buried itself in the ground at our feet with no warning beyond the sound of it — no second shot, no shouted demand, just a single, unmistakable point being made from somewhere we never found. We retreated faster than we'd arrived, back down the same street, and didn't pretend to each other that we'd learned anything the woman hadn't already told us for free.

* * *

Back at the barracks that evening we found Tam exactly where Tam always was — cheerful, unbothered, halfway through some small kindness for somebody who hadn't asked for one. Moss handed him the wolf's skull from the den, mostly as a joke, and Tam accepted it with the delighted gravity of someone being given a crown. Corey, predictably, wanted to know what everyone had wasted their day off on, and was told, with equal predictability, that it had indeed been wasted, thank you for asking.

Anders had his own business to settle first. Fred had left something unpleasant in his bed some weeks back, over the business with the counting-points and the false accusation none of us had ever quite finished apologising for, and Anders repaid it in kind and then some — a grim, unmissable trophy from the den left waiting on Fred's own pillow, delivered in near-total silence and total satisfaction. Nobody asked him to stop. Nobody thought it would help if we had.

Caspian, meanwhile, had decided — without asking anyone — to spend his evenings in the guild library, teaching himself survival out of borrowed manuals, quietly and without making a thing of it. It suited him. None of us said so to his face.

That night Anders lit the candle he'd carried off a sinking ship in the storm months before, and swore, straight-faced, that it was part of his religion, which fooled precisely nobody and amused everyone regardless. Caspian called it out for the lie it was, loudly enough that half the barracks laughed along with him, and somebody threatened, not entirely joking, to send a dog to sleep in whoever's bed complained next. The candle stayed lit. Nobody made him put it out.

A few quiet days went by after that. We checked on Sabine again before the week was out, and found her hovel patched and stocked better than we'd left it — food, a mended roof, the small unglamorous kindnesses of people who'd noticed her without being asked to. It was the first good news any of us had had in a while. We didn't ask too hard where it had come from. We were learning, slowly, not to.

* * *

Wren didn't come back that night, which wasn't unusual enough to worry anyone straight away. By morning her bunk was still empty, and that was the part that finally felt wrong.

She was in the infirmary. She'd been found unconscious outside the guild gates by a routine patrol, beaten badly enough that whoever had done it had used something more than fists — clubs, or staffs, something that left the marks it left. The worst of the physical damage had already been closed over by whatever magic the guild kept on hand for exactly this, but she hadn't woken, and the healer on duty couldn't say for certain when she would.

We wanted, badly, to march straight up the hill and start pointing fingers. Moss was the one who talked us out of moving too fast, more or less by refusing to move too fast himself. We'd accused the wrong people before, he said, and we'd all watched what that cost — a warehouse, a man named Jory, a door and a girl who hadn't deserved either. He wasn't going to do that again to a whole community of people because one of them had a grudge and a loose tongue. So we went carefully instead, which felt, for once, like the harder thing to do.

Moss left Brambleback curled at the foot of Wren's cot before we went anywhere, on the theory that she'd want something familiar and warm there when she finally opened her eyes. None of us argued with the theory.

* * *

The shrine's senior keeper received us kindly enough, warm even, right up until we asked directly about Hesk. She didn't deny what we already half-knew — that he was one of the older, more superstitious voices among them, quick to blame outsiders for whatever the season had taken from them, and that Wren's name had come up in his mouth more than once and never gently. She wouldn't say he'd hurt anyone. She said, carefully, that the shrine survived people like him the same way it survived every other hard winter, by simply continuing, and that whatever had happened to Wren hadn't happened on the shrine's own ground where anyone there could have stopped it.

We asked to look around anyway. The grounds were too much ground to search together in any useful time, so we split — Moss and Ordis one way, the rest of us another — covering more of it than we could have walked together. Somewhere among the garden paths and the quiet places a girl who liked to draw might choose to sit unbothered, one of us came across a scatter of crayons in the dirt, dropped or knocked from someone's hand and never picked back up. It was the first real mark of where she'd actually been. We gathered back together to look at it, and none of us said much, because there wasn't much a handful of crayons could tell you except that something had gone wrong exactly there.

We went home with more certainty than we'd arrived with and nothing we could call proof.

* * *

Wren woke two days later, and the first thing she did was apologise for worrying us, which told us more about her than anything she said after it.

She'd been jumped by four figures, hooded, somewhere near her usual sketching spot in the early evening light she liked best for catching birds. One of them had done something with plants she couldn't properly describe — vines out of bare ground, fast enough that she hadn't had a chance to run before they'd wrapped her ankles and pulled her down. After that it was mostly noise and pain and the particular, specific fear of being alone with people who wanted you to be. She hadn't seen a face she recognised. She was fairly sure, from the build of at least one of them, that she'd guess right if pressed, and equally sure she wasn't going to be the one to say the name out loud.

Caspian argued against doing anything about it at all, longer and harder than any of us expected. The guild had told us plainly to leave it. Wren herself had already agreed, quietly, never to go back to the shrine, which as far as the guild was concerned closed the matter cleanly enough. Going after whoever had done this wasn't justice, Caspian said — it was exactly the kind of thing that had already cost a frightened girl a door to the face and cost us a name we hadn't earned the right to use since. Nobody argued that he was wrong. We went anyway, and he came with us, in the end, with great reluctance and no pretence otherwise.

Fenwick tried the honest route first, asking Factor Slate directly for whatever cover or help the guild could quietly offer. Slate heard him out, courteous as always, and said no — plainly, without cruelty, the way he said most things that mattered. As far as the guild was concerned, Wren had already agreed to stay off the hill; there was no ongoing work up there worth protecting, and no reason for the guild to involve itself further in something that had, on paper, already been resolved.

So we built the trap ourselves. Fenwick — near enough Wren's size and build to pass for her at a distance in poor light — would sit in her usual spot at dusk with her sketchbook, and the rest of us would wait close by among the trees, and see who came looking for a girl who wasn't supposed to be there anymore.

* * *

They came just after dark, three of them, hooded, shouting something about having already told her to stay away before any of us had properly seen their faces.

Caspian's vines answered first, bursting up out of the ground to catch two of the three flat-footed. One of them turned out to be exactly what Wren had described — a caster of some kind, plant-magic of their own answering Caspian's, immobilising one of us in turn before the fight had properly begun. Moss shed his own shape for another, coming up as something with weight and speed and a temper, and put himself between the attackers and everyone slower than him without waiting to be asked. It was a hard, close fight, fists and clubs and a sling bolt that found Caspian more than once, and it ended the only way it was ever going to when three people meet six who came prepared for it: all three of them down, unconscious rather than dead, because that had been decided on and held to without much discussion needed.

We pulled the hoods back once it was over. Strangers, mostly — human, unremarkable, nobody we could put a name to with any confidence, and certainly not Hesk himself, whatever any of us privately suspected about who'd sent them. There was a short, unhappy argument about what to do with them. The guild wouldn't want them and had no evidence to hold them on even if it had. The shrine already half-knew what Hesk was and had done nothing about it on its own. In the end it was Caspian — of all of us, the one who'd argued hardest against any of this happening at all — who pulled a balaclava over his own face and waited for the first of them to wake up.

"Don't touch the tiefling again," he told him, low and even, and it worked exactly as well as that kind of thing usually does. The man went pale and swore he understood. Caspian put him back under anyway, not ungently, and we left all three of them tied at the shrine's own door for someone to find in the morning — a message with no signature, the only kind we could afford to send.

* * *

Wren never asked how the men who'd hurt her ended up unconscious outside the shrine's own front door, and we never told her the whole of it. She looked at us differently after, in a way we hadn't earned cleanly and didn't examine too closely, for fear of what we might find if we did.

The morning brought its own smaller reckoning first — Anders woke with his own face smeared in grease, some small justice finally catching up with him after weeks of Fred going unpunished for the manure and the rest of it, and the laughter that followed was the first easy one we'd had in days. News came with it that Fred's own contract had gone up for sale, debts finally outrunning him the way they eventually outran everyone in the guild's arithmetic, and nobody at our table pretended to feel sorry about it.

We were still together. We were, for the first time in a while, a little more trusted than we'd been the week before — not by the guild, not by the shrine, but by the one person whose trust had actually cost us something to earn. That mattered more than we let ourselves say out loud.
