# Chapter 6 — The Line We Crossed

We found out what paying meant sooner than we expected.

Two more months went by the way months went by then: counted in coin, not in seasons. Training in the mornings, the roster in the afternoons, and every evening the same tired walk back to bunks that were never quite ours. We learned the shortcuts. We learned which cookhouse did the best food, which turned out to be the one we'd stumbled into that first week without knowing any better. We learned that a normal animal cost three silver a month to keep in the barracks and a magical one cost double, which none of us thought was fair, and none of us were asked whether we thought it was fair. Moss now had two animals under that roof and paid for both without complaint.

Corley told us how to make a bunk properly, and disapproved of how we did it anyway. Every morning, across the room, one of the other indentured wrote something in a small notebook before anyone else was awake, and put it away before anyone could ask what. We never did ask, that first stretch of weeks. She was quiet in the particular way that meant she'd decided quiet was safer, and we were sixteen and busy and let her be quiet. Her name, we learned eventually, in passing, the way you learn most names in a barracks: Sena. Four more years on her contract when we arrived. One, by the time any of this mattered.

The ledger kept its own accounting, separate from ours. We paid off four gold that stretch, which felt like something until Rhys did the sum out loud and it came to seven hundred and forty-four still owed between the five of us, Moss's share alone closer to eight hundred, on account of the boy. Nobody said the boy's name. Nobody had ever had it to say. We had stopped flinching at the number by then, which was its own kind of cost, quieter than the first one.

* * *

The day the wharf strike started, we were three streets from it before we noticed anything was wrong.

We'd gone down to Low Quay on our one day off, all six of us, same as every week, and found more people standing around than usual, standing still in a way that people usually weren't. Word came off a few passersby before we even reached the crowd: the dockworkers were out, wanting better pay, and had been for two days already. Wages hadn't moved with the cost of everything else, not since the military started building up along the coast over some quarrel with a neighbouring kingdom none of us had names for. Ships came in from the fishing grounds and sat unloaded. Someone said the word *union*, and Anders — who by then thought in petitions and legal representation the way some people thought in meals — brightened in a way that should have worried us more than it did.

"At least you're getting paid," one of the picketers told him, not warmly.

"I'd rather be a freeman than a guildman," he said back, which got him a long look and very little else.

We split up after that, as you do on a day off nobody's claimed for anything else. Caspian went to wait tables at Della's cookshop, in want of a wage that wasn't already spoken for by the day he owed the guild. Fenwick, Anders, and Archer went to the tavern, minus one; Moss had already gone off with Ordis.

Ordis had crossed a field for him once, back at the dance, in front of the whole town, and hadn't done anything of the kind since — not because it hadn't mattered, we understood later, but because it had, and she'd spent three careful years building a life nobody looked at twice. Whatever this was between them, it moved slowly and mostly in silence, on afternoons like this one, away from anyone who might have thought to ask about it. Moss never told us where they went. We never asked. That was its own kind of respect, we'd like to think, though it might just as easily have been that none of us knew what to say to him about it yet.

Skylar went sketching, as she always did when she had an hour and nowhere she had to be. Wren went with her — had been going with her most weeks since Stoneharbour, a quieter presence than either of them let on, sitting close enough to see the page and saying almost nothing while Skylar worked. It should have felt strange, a piece of Amble following us this far. It didn't. Some people you don't leave behind so much as they come along without asking.

* * *

The morning after, we were told to report to Hobbern, the guild's quartermaster, a man who managed his indentured with the bored efficiency of somebody who'd done it for a very long time and expected to keep doing it. He was waiting for us with his arms folded when we arrived.

"The dockhands have stopped," he said, like it was our problem now, because it was. "You'll be working the docks."

We didn't understand yet what that meant. We would.

*I didn't know it then, but that was the morning it stopped being someone else's fight.* We were six kids who owed a guild money, being sent to do the one job that would put us on the opposite side of a picket line from people who wanted exactly what we wanted — to be paid fairly for what they did. Nobody explained it to us that way. Nobody had to. We worked it out ourselves, later, standing in the middle of it.

The first day was ordinary enough, in the way the guild's version of ordinary always was: crates and barrels and the endless business of moving fish from one place to another. Anders discovered that a hand of floating magic could lift five pounds and no more, which did not stop him from casting it eighty separate times across the day to shift eighty separate fish, one at a time, with the dogged commitment of someone who had found a genuinely bad idea and refused to let go of it. Moss did the same work with his own two hands in a fraction of the time and told him so, more than once, with the particular relish of a boy who did not often get to be the practical one. Neither of them stopped smiling about it for the rest of the week.

We noticed the picket line properly for the first time that evening, on the way back — a long stretch of people, more than a hundred by anyone's rough count, and a great many children among them, throwing whatever came to hand. A rotten tomato found Archer first. Something worse found Skylar a moment later. We laughed about it more than the moment probably warranted, because that was easier than the alternative.

* * *

The second day, we had to go through them.

It hadn't been a problem before — dockhands had always come and gone through some quiet understanding, guild business being guild business — but the mood had turned overnight, and turned harder than any of us expected. A dozen or so of the line's angrier members had armed themselves with cudgels by then. The rest, women and old men and children mostly, wanted nothing more than to be heard, and had found that throwing fruit was the only language anyone in charge seemed willing to answer.

Skylar tried to talk her way through first, walking straight at the line as she did most things, telling them plainly she wasn't the one they should be angry at. *"No further, missy,"* someone told her, and meant it, and she found out how much a hundred people meant it when she tried anyway and simply couldn't move. Caspian went with her, both of them shoving, both of them shoved back twice as hard, and the two of them gave it up rather than come to real blows with people who had done nothing to deserve them. She tried once more later, with a borrowed face instead of brute force, and got no further for it — the line knew its own, however they were dressed, and turned her away a second time without ever letting on how they'd known.

Anders went a different way. He found the man who seemed to be leading the line and talked at him with the relentless good cheer of someone convinced that any problem could be solved if you were simply willing enough to keep talking, offering himself as a legal representative to a man who wanted nothing from him but to leave. It earned him a tomato in the ear for his trouble, thrown with real accuracy, and a great deal more laughter from the soldiers watching from behind the crowd, who found the whole business considerably funnier than either side actually walking it out did.

Fenwick and Anders tried going over instead of through, and Fenwick paid for it. Three of the line's angrier men caught him on the rise, dragged him toward the water, and had him half under before he broke free — going into the harbour rather than staying in their hands, which by the standards of that morning counted as the better outcome. He climbed out on the guarded side soaking and furious and, from what we could tell shouting up at us from below, entirely unheard.

It was watching that happen — watching Fenwick actually taken, actually hurt, by people whose only crime was wanting to be paid — that put Moss over the edge of patience he hadn't known he had. He went at the line himself after that, straight on, no negotiation in him at all, and the line held. A blow from somewhere he never saw caught him hard across the leg. Three of the biggest men on the picket, men who had done this work with their hands their whole lives and looked it, planted themselves in front of him and simply would not move. For one genuinely uncertain stretch it looked as though he wouldn't get through at all — Caspian pushing at his back the whole time and neither of them making any ground — and then, somehow, between one shove and the next, he did, and was gone from the crowd's edge before we'd properly registered he'd left it.

We got through in the end, all of us, one way or another, the way we mostly did. It wasn't the guards who let us — it was the guards who *made* us, shoving a path clear for the day's other new workers and pulling us through in the gap they made, which felt like the smallest possible mercy and probably was. We didn't feel like winners walking through. We felt like the reason a hundred people were angrier than they'd been that morning.

* * *

Past the line, there was other work waiting, and it was stranger than crates of fish.

A new ship had come in under an escort heavier than any of the others — small boxes and crates instead of bulk cargo, more guards than the job called for, wearing an emblem none of us recognised. Skylar and Caspian took the chance to sketch it while nobody was looking closely at them. Anders tried, and failed, to smuggle a crate back into the warehouse a second time under the nose of the man ticking boxes off a list, a plan that fell apart somewhere around the part where nobody could explain why a halfling was walking back in carrying something he'd already carried out. Moss, with rather more subtlety, pocketed a few cuttings from the strange plants going past — nothing anyone would miss, and more than enough to grow something new, if he ever worked out what it was he'd taken.

Nobody told us what was in the crates. We were guild, which apparently made us trustworthy enough to carry them and not curious enough to be told.

The days after ran together, one hard day much like the next: the same walk down, the same reluctant gap in the line, fewer rotten vegetables as the crowd tired of throwing them, more small injuries as the work wore on us. Caspian strained something lifting a crate that was heavier than it looked and walked with a limp for the better part of a week that no amount of healing seemed able to touch, because it turned out some hurts were just muscle and time, and neither of those cared how many spells you knew.

* * *

Our own turn to guard the docks came around soon enough, in place of the strike's own missing watchmen, and it was as quiet as anyone had promised — until it wasn't.

We split into pairs to walk the rounds: Caspian and Anders together; Archer and Fenwick together; Moss and Skylar left in the break room with nothing much to do, waiting on Anders' borrowed trick of speaking mind to mind across the yard in case anyone found trouble worth reporting. Fenwick and Archer found it first, on one of the fishing boats — water rats, unnaturally large, dragging fish one at a time out of a broken barrel and dropping them, deliberately, into the harbour. Not eating them. Just wasting them, over and over, with a patience no ordinary animal should have had.

"Are rats meant to do that?" Fenwick asked, down the borrowed line to Moss, who would have known if anyone did.

They weren't. Nobody had an answer better than that.

The rats turned on them for asking. One took a bite out of Moss hard enough to put him briefly on the ground; the other came at Fenwick with teeth bared before a bolt of raw force from Archer's hand ended it outright. Fenwick knelt by Moss afterward with the little kit every one of us had learned to carry by then, and gave up some of his own strength to put some back into him — not much, but enough that Moss was back on his feet before either of them had to think too hard about how close that bite had come to being worse.

They should have left it there. Curiosity being what it is, they didn't. Fenwick shifted the gangplank aside to get a better look into the water beneath the boat, and the whole boat lurched under something far larger than a rat climbing aboard from underneath it.

It came up armoured and enormous, more limbs than any crab had a right to, and went straight for the nearest person, which was Archer. It had him by the arm before he could get clear, and the grip was strong enough that breaking it cost him nearly everything he had left in him. He managed it once, only to be caught again a moment later and hurt worse the second time, until he went down entirely, and it was Moss — closest, biggest, already halfway to him — who reached him first and kept him breathing while the rest of the yard scrambled to understand what was happening at all.

Fenwick went to help and paid for it worse than anyone. The creature caught him in its claw and wouldn't let go, dragging at him while he fought it with everything he had, until on the second desperate break he finally tore free and went straight over the side into the water rather than stay in its grip a moment longer. He came up gasping, climbed for a ladder he only knew was there because he'd already used it once that week, and dragged himself back onto the wharf soaked through and shaking.

By then word had reached Caspian and Anders on the far side of the yard — Anders' voice down the line telling them there was trouble at the dock before either of them understood what kind — and they ran without waiting to be asked twice. Skylar came from the break room a beat behind Moss, having heard nothing clearly except the sound of him crying out, which was enough. Between the six of us and the goat, who fought harder than anything half his size had any business fighting, we wore the creature down — Archer's fire finding it cleanest, Anders finding a blade in the dark of its own blind side, Fenwick's club landing again and again even soaked and exhausted — until it took one last swipe at whoever was nearest and slid back into the black water it had come from, and didn't come up again.

None of us died. That was the whole of the good news, and on a day like that one, it was enough.

* * *

The rest of the guard shift passed without incident, which by then felt less like luck and more like the day having spent everything it had. We were told, before we left, that the strike had ended — a small increase in pay agreed, a few more coppers a day, nothing that would have kept any of us up nights if it had been offered from the start instead of fought for. We wondered, walking back, whether that was the whole of what a hundred people standing in the rain and getting hit with tomatoes had bought them. We didn't ask out loud. Some things you already know the answer to.

It was around then that we noticed Sena had gone quiet in a different way than usual — grumpier, shorter with everyone, the kind of tired that sits behind the eyes rather than in the shoulders. Nobody thought much of it at the time. There wasn't much room left that week for thinking about anyone but ourselves.

One morning, not long after, her bunk was simply empty. Nobody in the barracks said where she'd gone, or whether *gone* was even the right word for it, and none of us thought to ask before it was too late to matter. We found out, piecing it together the way you do after the fact, that she'd refused to cross the line herself — refused to be what we'd been, that whole strike — and that refusing had cost her whatever was left of the year she had before her contract ended. We never saw her again.

We didn't talk about it much, afterward. There wasn't a version of the conversation that didn't end with someone pointing out that we'd done exactly what she wouldn't, and gotten paid for it. Nobody was ready to hear that out loud yet. Some of us still aren't.

* * *

That same week, at Low Quay, a stranger sat down uninvited while a few of us were having a drink and asked, without much preamble, whether we might be interested in some things you couldn't buy at the normal market.

He gave a name when Anders pressed him for one — Silas, he said, easily enough that it was probably even true — but somewhere in the asking somebody had already decided to call him something else, and the something else stuck before the real name had a chance to matter. *Dodgy Bob*, we called him from that evening on, and he answered to it without complaint — a nickname was cheaper than a reputation, and he clearly knew it. He wasn't guild, and he wasn't anyone's man in particular, as far as we could tell — a small, careful operator working the margins of a bigger and better-known trade than his own, tolerated rather than welcomed by the people who actually ran things in that part of town, on the theory, we'd guess, that a known face causes less trouble than an unknown one.

He had potions to sell, and wasn't particular about payment — coin, or something just as useful. Anders, never a man to leave an opening like that closed, offered him something instead of gold: word of what those heavily guarded crates had actually been carrying, off a ship none of us had ever gotten a straight answer about. He didn't know if it was true. He told it convincingly enough that it didn't matter whether it was.

Goblin prisoners, he said. Warriors, held somewhere inside a warehouse that belonged to the military and nobody else.

Bob considered that for a moment, decided it was worth more than he'd expected an evening's gossip to be worth, and handed over a small vial for it — unlabelled, faintly luminous, worth considerably more than the coin he'd first offered, if his own face was any guide. Nobody drank it that night. Nobody could tell, looking at it, what exactly they'd been handed, only that it was worth having, and that whatever Bob had traded it for, he'd been glad to make the trade.

*"You know where to find me,"* he said, before he left. As if any of us were in a hurry to.

We walked back to the barracks that night with a debt slightly smaller than it had been, a bunk in the corner slightly emptier than it had been, and a small glass vial none of us understood, tucked away for later. Somewhere in that town, we were fairly sure, somebody was still keeping their own ledger of everything we'd done to earn our keep that week. We hadn't seen the bill yet. We were starting to understand that we would.
