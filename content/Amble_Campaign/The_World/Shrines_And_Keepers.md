Halden has no state church. The white sun on oxblood is the Crown's mark, and it means government — a
licence, a writ, a soldier. It is not a religious symbol and nobody treats it as one.

What Halden has instead is the shrine up the hill. There is one in Amble and there is one in Stoneharbour,
and they are not branches of anything. Amble's faith belongs to Amble. Nobody in Aldermoot has an opinion
about it, because there is nobody in Aldermoot whose job that would be.

There is no book, no doctrine, no hierarchy above the village, and nothing to convert to. Which means
there is also nothing to be wrong about, and that shapes everything below.

## What everyone does

None of this requires believing anything in particular, and most people would be embarrassed to be asked
what they believe.

- **The doorpost mark.** A hearth-flame or a sheaf, scratched or chalked or carved by the door, recut when
  it wears. A fresh one means that house has had a scare recently. A worn one means they have been lucky.
- **A word over food.** Short, plain, addressed to nobody by name — the sort of thing that stops sounding
  like a prayer after the four-hundredth time. Skipping it is unremarkable. A guest is usually asked to say
  it, which is a courtesy and very mildly a test.
- **Two fingers to the brow.** A ward, or a blessing. Aimed at bad news, a stranger, or nothing at all.
  Most people doing it could not tell you which of the two they meant.
- **Climbing the hill when somebody is ill.** The commonest devotional act there is. You go up, you sit,
  you put something in the jar or you don't, you come down. Nobody asks what you believe and nobody
  checks.
- **The offering jar.** Coppers, an egg, a bundle of herbs. It pays for the almshouse. For most Haldeni
  this is the entire financial relationship they will ever have with faith.

## Restday

The last day of the week. A day of rest, and in practice two customs:

**The fire is not let out, and small accounts are not settled.** You do not press somebody for money on
Restday. A debt is still a debt on Firstday and everyone knows it, but today a man will wave you off and
take it tomorrow, happily, with no bad feeling either way.

**The Iron Coin Guild does not observe it.** The Guild keeps no day. Its clerks work, its writs run, and
its collections fall where the ledger says they fall. This is not a secret policy and nobody up at the
shrine is surprised by it — but it is one of the few places where the difference between the two
institutions is visible to anybody standing in the street on the right afternoon.

## Under the roof

A traveller caught out by nightfall is offered a roof. Not as charity and not as a favour — as a matter of
course, the way you would hold a door. The shrine keeper takes in anyone the households cannot.

People say somebody is *"under the roof"* rather than *"taken in"*, because the second one sounds like
something was owed.

**Turning a hungry person away is genuinely shameful.** Not illegal. Shameful — the kind of thing that
follows a family around a village for years. This is the most religiously significant custom in the country
and nobody in it calls it religious.

## The shrine, and who keeps it

A **keeper** is a job. There is no ordination, no training school and no rank above the parish. It is often
taken on at the end of another life — a retired soldier, a healer, an elf who has simply been there longer
than anyone can remember. Under the keeper are minor keepers who light the lamps and sweep the steps and do
most of the actual work, volunteers who are there most days and hold no position at all, and children
running errands for coppers.

Halden says **shrine** and **keeper**. It does not say temple, church, priest or congregation. A keeper is
not a priest and will say so if you call them one.

**Faith does not run along bloodlines here.** Stoneharbour's shrine-keeper is an elf, its almshouse keeper
a halfling, its gravedigger an elf, and the child sweeping the steps a gnome. A congregation is a parish,
not a family. Nobody finds this worth remarking on, which is itself the point.

A town shrine is the same thing as a village one with more moving parts. On
[[Stoneharbour#shrine-hill|Shrine Hill]] that means four separate places with four separate keepers, none
of whom answers to the others: the public shrine (**Selwyn Aske**), the burial ground — **the Quiet
Ground** — the almshouse and hospice — **the Resting Stair** — and **the Wayside Stone** off the main path.
The Shrine School teaches letters and numbers off the same hill, to children whose families can spare them.

**Who pays for all of it:** the jar, the occasional bequest, and whatever the Harbour Concord adds. Not the
Crown. There is no tithe in Halden. And not the Guild, which has never had any financial relationship with
a shrine at all.

## What a shrine can do for you — and what it cannot

This is worth reading before you need it.

**A shrine has less power than the Governor**, and the Governor has never once overruled a Guild recovery
writ, because she cannot. So what the Hill can actually do for somebody the Guild has taken apart is this,
and it is not nothing:

- **Feed them and give them a bed.** No questions, no fee, no limit anybody will state out loud.
- **Nurse them.** The Resting Stair takes the old and the infirm who have nowhere else — a dozen cots and
  a kitchen.
- **Bury them.** The Quiet Ground, free, and its keeper remembers every name on every stone.
- **Sit with them.** Ask nothing, offer a place to be quiet, remember the face next time.
- **Say their name out loud** after the branch has struck it off a roster.

**What it cannot do is cancel a debt, and it does not pretend otherwise.** If you climb the Hill looking
for sanctuary from a writ you will get kindness, a hot meal, and no leverage whatsoever. Nobody up there
will lie to you about that.

## The small stones

Alongside the shrine — not instead of it — sit the small devotions: a stone for people who go out on the
water, a niche in a ropewalk, a mark cut over a forge door. No keepers worth the name, no calendar, no
objection to each other.

A stevedore who leaves a coin at the Wayside Stone on a sailing day and climbs the Hill when his mother is
ill is not doing two contradictory things. He is doing the ordinary thing. The woman on the Market Steps
selling sailors bent nails as luck, perfectly aware it is nonsense, is the same instinct one notch further
down, and nobody polices the line.

## The year

Each month carries its own ordinary observance, and four of them matter enough to plan around — see
[[The_Calendar|the calendar]] for the months themselves.

- **Harvestmonth** is the busiest shrine month of the year. Sheaf marks get recut everywhere.
- **Threshmonth** is when debts and wages are traditionally settled, after the threshing. **Which is
  exactly why a Guild collection timed to this month lands as hard as it does.** The custom is older than
  the Guild; the Guild simply knows the custom.
- **Leafall** is when the dead are visited. Graves tidied, names said aloud. Quiet, unmorbid, well
  attended.
- **Hungermonth** is the lean stretch. The almshouse is fullest, the jar is emptiest, and the shrine
  matters more in that one month than in the other eleven together.

## Shrine Hill, and the old faith

> [!info]- ⚪ COMMON — no proficiency needed
> What you will hear in the street, and most of it is wrong.
>
> There is something old up on that hill, older than the town, maybe older than the Guild. They keep books
> up there and the books have things to say about blood — about horned blood especially, and what it means,
> and none of the people repeating this have read a page of it.
>
> Ask three people in Stoneharbour what the Hill believes and you will get three answers that do not
> match. Ordinary Stoneharbour does not understand its own hill and has never needed to.
>
> Whatever else is true: it is the one place in town that gives things away.

> [!tip]- 🔵 TRAINED — proficient in **both History and Religion**
> The real shape of it, which is smaller and sadder than the rumour.
>
> **It is a handful of places, not a network**, hill-sited, with no name for itself. People call it "the
> old faith" or "the old books" or nothing at all. **Nobody can tell you where it came from, including the
> people who keep it** — there is no founding story, no god named on any page, and no afterlife described
> anywhere.
>
> **What it keeps is not scripture. It is an index of signs.** Ruled into columns, annotated in many hands
> across centuries, thumb-hollowed at the entries people keep returning to, and the hands do not agree with
> each other. The belief underneath is three lines long: what a person inherits can be read; a sign is
> either a grace given or a debt carried, and the books have never settled which; and you give freely and
> you do not count what it costs.
>
> **The system reads everyone.** A caul-born baby, a seventh child, a limp, a birthmark, three good
> harvests running, a run of drownings in one household, a dwarf who can name his Delvern descent. Horned
> blood is one entry among hundreds, and the only thing that makes it notable up there is that it can be
> seen from across a room. **Nobody is being called evil. They are being filed** — gravely, courteously,
> by somebody who thinks they are doing you the service of telling you what you are carrying, and who
> never asks your name.
>
> **The reading does not come from the keeper.** It comes from the fringe: hedge-elders, lay readers who
> hold no office and were appointed by nobody, listened to because they have read the books for fifty
> years. The keeper of the public shrine has no patience for omen-talk, greets everyone the same way, and
> is entirely capable of refusing to join in and taking the consequences from his own regulars.
>
> **And Halden says they are wrong.** Tieflings are fully accepted in this kingdom. Categorising talk about
> them is a recognised rudeness that other Haldeni will call out, which makes the Hill a local oddity
> rather than the national mood. If it rattled you, that was the correct response and you are not the one
> out of step.
>
> The same hands do the good work. The people who ward at a stranger are the people who feed the girl
> sleeping rough by the almshouse and dig graves for those who left nothing to pay with. **It is the same
> people.** That is the hard part.

The old faith also writes on the road — see [[Languages#if-you-read-the-old-signs|Languages → the old
signs]] for the marks cut into gateposts and wayside stones, and what they say.

## Temples are a different thing

In this campaign a **temple** means an Aelorian building. Stoneharbour has a small Aelorian mission, and
those are temples; the Hill is a shrine, and the difference in the word is deliberate.

Aeloria is the one place on this continent with organised religion holding real power: a single god who is
genuinely there, doctrine set from the top, tithes, and caste registers. **That god has declared elves the
chosen people.** Humans, half-elves, dwarves, halflings and gnomes are tolerated. **Tieflings, orcs and
half-orcs are not accepted at all.**

Two practical consequences:

- **The temples sell casting**, 1st to 3rd level, on their own terms. They are a service, not a charity,
  and they are allowed to decline you.
- **Talking like an Aelorian about a tiefling is a faux pas in Halden**, and a recognised one. Haldeni
  notice, and some of them will say something.

## Who actually heals you

Four providers, and the terms are completely different:

| Who | Terms |
|---|---|
| **The shrine and almshouse** | Free, no billing, no means test. Nursing and shelter — food, warmth, clean bedding, somebody sitting up with you |
| **The village healer** | Informal, personal, paid in eggs or work or nothing. Herbs, bones, fevers. Not clergy, not licensed |
| **A licensed caster** | A trade, properly paid. A tradesman, not a priest |
| **The Guild** | For its own people, on its own terms — which means added to what you owe |

**The line that matters:** the free care in this country is *nursing*, and the paid care is *magic*. You
can always get somebody fed, warmed and sat with for nothing. You can never get somebody mended for
nothing. See [[Hurt_And_What_To_Do|Hurt, and What to Do About It]] and
[[What_Magic_Costs|What Magic Costs]].
