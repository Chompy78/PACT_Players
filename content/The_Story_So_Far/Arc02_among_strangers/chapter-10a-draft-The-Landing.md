# Chapter 10a — The Landing

We had been in Stoneharbour for over a year and a half, and we had worn the brand the whole time. Most of that time had gone on standing guard, carrying boxes from one cold room to another, and sorting out the guild's market stalls. It was a real hoot, if you liked boxes.

In between there had been the odd job that wasn't boring, and those had got us a reputation. Some of it was good. Most of it wasn't. There were streets a few of us no longer walked down, and a few where people were rather fond of us. We were also working out how the guild kept its books. The better we did, the more we were worth, and the more we were worth, the more it would spend on us.

At the last review, most of us stood a couple of points short of grade two. Fenwick was a point ahead of the rest. Everyone who had come into the barracks with us was grade two already.

That week we each had a few days to ourselves, and we spent them differently. Moss went up to Shrine Hill and came back knowing a few more of its ways with growing things. Fenwick went to the bakery and the soup kitchen and helped wherever something needed baking or carrying. Nobody paid him. He said it was for the people we had wronged, and he said it without a joke attached, which was how we knew he meant it.

Skylar had no plan, so she did much the same, helping out at Dela's after closing when Dela was short-handed. A flu was going round the kitchen. By the end of the week Skylar had it. She coughed, she spluttered, and for days afterwards she couldn't have crept past a sleeping dog. Anyone else's week of being kind would have ended with a warm feeling. Skylar's ended in a handkerchief.

Caspian had taken the guild's steady work and then pushed for something extra. It went well. The extra job took ten gold off his debt and brought his ranking level with Fenwick's. Anders had spent the same stretch trying to mend his standing with the guild. It didn't mend. He did get three gold knocked off his debt, which was more than anyone had expected of him.

* * *

Skylar was grumpy as hell in the mornings, and she had stopped pretending otherwise. "I'm just not a morning person," she said. "Leave me alone." After a coffee she was a different girl, so Moss suggested we carry a coffee around with us.

That morning the guild had us in the warehouses doing a stocktake, which was as exciting as it sounds. The news came from whoever happened to be standing nearest. Senna was back, the guild member who had gone missing. She'd had a year left when she vanished, and her debt had almost been paid. But the charges had kept landing on her account the whole time she was away, and the time away didn't count as time served. Now she had ten months left, and she was particularly grumpy about the money. Tam wrote from his new post to say he was enjoying the work. He had been made grade two, and he said it was much better out of Stoneharbour, in a small country town. It was a bit dangerous, he said, but a lot more exciting than endless streets.

Then word went round that a new group of indentured was coming in that day. We were to meet them, feed them lunch and show them around, which the guild called an honour, since we were still grade one.

About a dozen of them came walking in off the street into the entry yard, led by a senior guild man. One of them we knew. Bram was among them, bigger than we remembered, and Moss walked straight over and shook his hand. "Moss!" Bram said. "It's good to see a friendly face." He had always liked Moss better than most of us.

Skylar asked if there were any other girls in the group. There were a couple, and she went to make friends with them.

The man leading them in looked us over, pointed at Anders and said something we didn't catch. After that most of the new faces found somewhere else to look. Anders had made two proper enemies inside the guild, and it seemed they had been talking. Moss asked whether his own reputation hadn't come ahead of him too. It hadn't. All the rest of us had done, we were told, was be hopeless now and then.

* * *

We spent the rest of the day showing them round. Fenwick wanted to start their education early, so he asked one of the recruits to go to the blacksmith's and fetch the left-handed hammer. She laughed at him. He couldn't even remember the name of the tool he was pretending to want.

"Please say we're not assigned to your barracks," she said.

"Sure you are," Moss said. "Welcome."

Skylar told her not to worry. All the boys were like that, she said, and you got used to it. A couple of the new girls said the men who had come in with them didn't seem so bad. Typical boys, nothing obnoxious. "Give it time," Skylar said. "They'll find a way."

Moss took the interested ones up to Shrine Hill, and Skylar, who had started drawing like Wren, took the crafty ones to Dela's. Dela treated them very well, though she still hadn't got her spark back after the business with the notice. It had been months, and she was embarrassed every time she saw us. Toby was being difficult. He was angry about the whole business with Dela and the guild's debt, and he'd been seen with people Dela would rather he wasn't, and she complained about it to anyone who stood still. When we looked into the ten gold that had gone toward her debt, we found it had come from the Anchor Light shrine. Jory had raised it with his old crew, going round the Low Quay for a couple of silver here and there from hundreds of people.

Fenwick caught Bram while he was unpacking in the barracks. Bram had taken a two-hundred-gold debt and a three-year indenture. Part of it would pay for his father's care, and the rest had bought him a chain shirt and a longsword, which he showed off.

"Where did you steal those?" Fenwick asked. "I bought them," Bram said, and he'd got a good price. He had trained hard with the combat mentor back in Amble. He was bigger again, not quite Moss's size, but he'd give Moss a fair run. "I'm the same as you now," he said. "Indentured. Two hundred gold won't take long to pay off."

At dinner he wanted the highlights of our year and a half, and Skylar said she'd tell him. Moss got in first.

"I murdered two children with my goat," he said.

Bram stared at him. "We were in danger," Moss said. "And I murdered two children with my goat."

"Moss should not be telling this story, Bram," Skylar said. "Sit down and eat your dinner."

"Yes, ma'am," Moss said.

Skylar took over. We'd had a rocky start with one of our early jobs, she said, and met some very interesting people in the Ropewalks. Moss had, by his own account, murdered two small children with the goat, accidentally, on two separate occasions (one of them had been dead for a minute, but had come back), so he was in trouble for that, and children were wary of him now. The Ropewalks didn't like him either. A few of us were in trouble down there, so we kept clear. Then she mentioned the giant octopus-kraken, and Fenwick said it had been a highlight in some ways, since at least we hadn't just been moving boxes.

"I'm trying to do the highlights," Skylar said. "Not the dark bits."

Fenwick said sorry, and that he'd just eat his bread.

Bram wanted to know about Anders. He'd always thought a big city would make Anders tone it down. "The opposite," Moss said. "He found another gear." Skylar said that with the big city had come the big attitude. "It's been persistent," she said. "I don't know about impressive. But consistency is key, and he is consistent."

We told Bram what we'd been told ourselves. If you got into a scrap, you didn't kill anyone. Stoneharbour was particular about that, and it wasn't rough like some other places.

"I can vouch for that," Moss said.

"That advice still applies," Skylar said. "Some of us just didn't pay attention to it."

* * *

The next morning was warm. It was sun month, which meant summer, and everything was dry and the insects were out. We heard there was to be a hanging in a couple of days. Two people had been found guilty of murder and couldn't pay the fee for it. They were outsiders, so nobody could vouch for them, and they had killed someone on the steps in front of a crowd. Guild members were forbidden to attend. It wasn't thought proper.

A few days later we heard it had gone ahead, with a good crowd. People treated it as an entertainment, but the law was plain. If you couldn't pay off a death debt, you hanged. It did a good job of keeping violence down in the town.

The guild had paid Moss's death debt for him, which meant it had added it to his own debt. Moss wanted it noted that he had murdered two children and still didn't owe the most money. Skylar owed the most. That wasn't the killing. It was the training, which she'd done more of than any of us, and she had missed the day Wren explained how that worked.

So we explained it. The guild paid half the cost of any training and added the other half to your debt. If you broke your contract early, the half it had waived went onto your exit fee as well. Our exit fee was seven hundred and fifty gold, less whatever we'd earned, plus the training we'd taken, and if we left early it was the whole cost of the training, not just half.

Skylar said she remembered that part now.

* * *

A few days later we were sent for to Slate's office. He had a bowl of apples on his desk, and he asked if anyone wanted one. Moss took one. Anders was already eating his, and Slate looked at him the whole time he talked.

"I hear you knew one of the new indentured," he said. "The big one. I've forgotten his name. It's only two nights' work, on night shift, and for some reason you drew the short straw. I'm sure it's got nothing to do with Anders."

"All you need to do is head out to the docks and watch," he said. "Somebody is smuggling goods into Stoneharbour, and you need to find out who." They would be charms, he expected, brought in without paying the charter levy, which undercut the chartered trade. "Guess who's paying for the job."

We were to watch the landing and find out how it ran. Then we were to catch someone, alive, who might know something. "A grunt won't be any use," Slate said. "Find someone who knows what's going on, and what they plan."

"Don't bring them back here," he went on. "We want to be at arm's length from the whole thing. Find somewhere to hold them, and get some information out of them." They would be released afterwards, so we should hide who we were and not be too rough. The pay was five gold for a successful night, with follow-up work if it went well. The landing would be tomorrow night or the night after, so it was going to be a long one, and we should get some sleep. He suggested we have a word with Bram, the big one.

Bram was to come with us. So was Wren, to keep an eye on the rest of us, and she rolled her eyes and sighed at the sight of us all. Skylar leaned over and whispered, "Welcome to my world." Wren said she didn't know how Skylar did it, and Skylar said that was still a mystery to her too.

"Any questions?" Slate said. "Can anyone answer the pop quiz? Are you meant to kill anyone?"

"Yes," Moss said. Then, "No. Do not kill anybody."

"Wren, Skylar," Slate said. "Keep them in line, please. If you two manage it, I'll see you get some extra consideration."

"Well, the girls have got to keep the boys in line," Skylar said. "Surprise, surprise."

"I get to be my normal self," Fenwick said. "I'm good."

He asked whether the guild had any disguises for us. Moustaches, perhaps. Slate said he was sure we could work something out.

Then Wren asked whose job this was. It was the merchants', Slate said, to stop people buying cheap charms that saved their lives. The guild was not involved. That was why the Low Quay was so angry, we guessed. The merchants meant to put up the price of feather-fall charms, which every sailor bought at the start of the season. Nobody wants to fall out of the rigging without one. Falling hurts.

* * *

We spent the rest of that day getting ready. For the job Caspian wanted nothing on us that bore the guild's mark. We found black knitted hoods that covered all but the eyes, manacles and rope. Moss said it was a good thing we were not a very identifiable group.

Wren found Skylar before we left. "I got you something for your birthday," she said. It had been Skylar's birthday not long before. Wren wasn't indentured and had some spare money, and she had spent it on studded leather armour. "Thanks, Wren," Skylar said. "You didn't have to do that."

"Girls have to stick together," Wren said. "And let's be honest, some of them aren't always idiots, but most of them are."

"They have their moments," Skylar said.

"I thought we had a very lovely moment last time," Caspian said. "We became far better friends."

"That is true," Skylar said. "And now you're eavesdropping on a girl's conversation. So, like I said, they have their moments."

Wren was grade two now, and a grade two could draw a charm from the guild stores for a job like this. She meant to take a healing one. Moss said that was probably the move.

The guild had given Skylar medicine for her cold, but it wasn't expected to work quickly, and buying a better cure meant adding to her debt, which wasn't worth it. She would be sneezing all night. Anders asked what we were allowed to do, and Fenwick went over it for him. Detain, question, no murder. Anders said the line had been very clearly marked.

Then we told Bram. "This is so exciting," he said. "This is the first proper mission, and I've only been here a few days." He wanted to know what he needed to do to get ready. Moss said he was going to have a nap. Anders said he had nothing for him, Bram, nothing at all. Moss went over the plan again to be sure. The guild didn't want us recognised, and we weren't to bring anyone back to the guild, which was what the hoods were for.

"So we've got to wear them," Anders said. "Bram, put a hood on."

* * *

There had been some trouble in the Ropewalks, but most of us stayed clear of the Ropewalks, so that was all we'd heard. Wren came back that evening, just as the lamps were being lit in the barracks yard. We were sitting on the step with our boots off, planning to stay up late and sleep in, so we'd be rested for the night. She had river mud to the ankles and the look of someone with news. "I've been down the Ropewalks," she said. We asked why. "I like to keep an ear out," she said. She'd heard a rumour that Praed had approved a job against some guild members.

"It could be us," Caspian said.

"It could be," Wren said. "There are a lot of guild members. But they hate you down there."

"It's probably us," Moss said, and pulled his boot back on as if he might need it. Anders said, "It might not be. There's a lot of guild members." Nobody sounded convinced, least of all Anders.

Wren said the Ropewalks needed Praed's approval for any action against the guild, and she usually checked with the guild as well. So if it was us, we had upset someone. "We're not the first people to upset someone," she said. "We might just be the latest. We might be the only ones who've killed a child, though. People take that personally."

Skylar put her face in her hands. "It's not exactly the record you want to set," she said, through her fingers.

Anders said the silver lining was that the guild wouldn't give us any babysitting jobs. Skylar said, "Not unless they don't want the kid to live."

Wren said it might be worth heading it off by speaking to Praed. "Because it went so well last time," Anders said, and Caspian winced. We didn't go. Anders asked whether we were going near the Ropewalks that night, and Moss said no. We were going to the docks, Moss was told, though it wasn't really the docks, because the boats landed on the Low Quay side, in one of the river mouths. "This is why you're not entrusted with key information, Moss," Skylar said.

We stayed up late and slept into the early afternoon. Then we had a decent meal and a few hours before we left. Bram was excited, and wore his chain shirt to dinner. Anders said he had a terrible feeling Bram would be dead on his first mission, after all those years of looking forward to joining. "Hopefully not, Bram," he said. "Hopefully not."

Bram said he'd done a lot of training, thank you very much, and he was pretty sure he could hold his own. He offered to spar if Anders wanted. Anders said maybe later.

It was not Bram he should have been worried about. We didn't know that yet.

* * *

We went out there for the first time at dusk, with no earlier scouting to guide us. There were a few buildings, some patches of scrub and a lot of vegetation. The boats were due to come about a hundred yards up the river mouth and land.

Moss was the one who knew how to hide in a bush, so we followed him. Caspian looked the ground over and offered to build a blind. He found a decent spot and bent a few branches into a screen, working as the light went so that no one in the houses would see. It was a half-decent job, he said. We all squeezed in.

Skylar had brought her sketchbook. She meant to draw whoever we saw, faces and all, and Wren sat beside her to help. It was a warm night, and the sun took a long time to go down. There was barely a breath of wind. The insects were persistent, laughter and singing drifted out of the nearby houses, and every few minutes Skylar sneezed.

"At least with the hood on, you're not spreading it," Caspian said. Skylar said she hadn't put hers on yet. It would look pretty odd, walking through town in one.

Then the Anchor Light, the sailors' shrine in the Low Quay, began to flash. Long on, long off. It went on for about five minutes. Fifteen minutes later it did it again. After the second time we saw a brief flash of light far out on the water. It was very dark, there was hardly any moon, and none of us knew the sea well enough to say what it meant. We were farmers' children.

Fifteen minutes after that we saw a small boat, just a silhouette, coming up the river mouth.

We had a plan, strictly speaking. We just hadn't discussed it until the boat appeared. Moss said he had slightly planned ahead, and that he'd brought an octopus. He meant he could turn into one. Skylar said she'd been picturing him carrying one on his shoulder, like a parrot. Anders said he'd be wearing it like a helmet, with a few tentacles around his chin.

Moss said if he could slip down to the water, he could swim out and listen in on the boat. Caspian said he could take a giant crab shape, in case the worst came to it, and skulk underneath. Moss crept down to the water's edge, and there was a small splash.

Two figures were walking down to the river landing. Skylar, with Wren's sharp eyes to help, looked hard at them. One was a woman, and the other looked like a teenager. The woman walked like the keeper of the shrine, and she wore her plain shrine robes, which weren't bad for sneaking about. She was the old lady, Anders said, the one who had been questioned. Skylar looked again, and between us we worked out the girl was Nessa, Jory's sister. She wore armour, and wore it confidently, so someone was training her. The woman's name, we were reminded, was Ysolt, and she could do a little healing magic.

They carried a small lamp, unlit. We watched it flash once toward the town and once out toward the water.

Moss was in the river by then, and he had attached himself to the lead boat. It was a small rowing boat for cargo, a bigger kind of rowboat, and the men in it were talking. "There's the signal," one said. "It's all good. Let's go in."

Three boats came toward the landing. At the same time about half a dozen people came out of the dark between the buildings with handcarts. Most of them we recognised from the Low Quay. Anders said they hated us. Wren said it was the Ropewalks that hated us more. We hadn't killed anyone's child in the Low Quay. A lot of them didn't mind us at all, since we had helped with the flooding.

* * *

We argued about what to do, in whispers, with our heads together and a branch in Fenwick's ear. The blind had been built for hiding, not for six people with opinions. We were meant to take somebody of importance, someone sending or receiving the goods, without anyone knowing it was us. Caspian said we only had to confirm what was in the crates. Then we could report that the shrine people were the ones receiving them. Anders asked whether that meant we had the guild's blessing to go further, and Caspian said he was only checking what counted as enough.

Bram settled it, or tried to. He was up on his knees, and Wren hauled him down by the belt. He said we should run out there and grab that woman. Knock her out, take her and go. Anders said we didn't know she'd done anything bad. There were also a lot of people with handcarts down there. "She looks like the ringleader," Bram said. "You said she was important."

"They'd want to protect her," Anders said.

"We've got to do something," Bram said.

"Bram, when did you turn into a lunatic?" Anders asked, very quietly, which made it worse.

Skylar said our task was to bring the intelligence back, not people. "Wait, Skylar," Wren said. "You've got a good point. We bring the intelligence back. We get the information, go back to the guild, and leave all the boys here." Skylar said she was all for that, but Slate had told her to keep the boys out of trouble. "It's a really bad job," she said, and sneezed into her sleeve, and we all froze until the nearest house began singing again.

Wren said that when we found the right person and interrogated them somewhere secure, we might leave the boys there. "Do you trust the boys?" Skylar asked. Anders asked why we'd brought them at all, then said that was a good point, and he'd known it as soon as he said it. Bram, who was one of the boys, said nothing, but it was a loud nothing.

We still hadn't found out enough. Skylar said we had identified people who were involved, and she had done some pretty sketches. Anders said we were meant to find out what they were smuggling and what they planned, and leaving now wouldn't be doing the job properly. Skylar said taking the ringleader would be very obvious. It was better to grab one of the cart people. Anders said let's take Nessa, since she'd been through it once already. Skylar said no, an underling who was with the ringleader would be noticed, and we knew where to find Nessa anyway. We didn't have to take her tonight.

"Why don't we just wait and see what happens?" Anders said.

"That's the smartest thing you've said all day, Anders," Skylar said, without looking up from her sketchbook.

Caspian said he'd turn into a monkey and follow the carts back when they left. Skylar said that was better than chasing an octopus, since all of us could follow carts. That settled it. We would follow the carts.

* * *

The boats ran up onto the sand, and the men began wrestling medium-sized crates onto the shore. There were about half a dozen to a boat. Two or three trips would clear them.

Moss, still an octopus, slid up onto the lead boat to see what was in one. The crates were nailed shut, and an octopus has trouble with a nail. A man saw him, a small grey shape on the side of the boat. "Oh, that'll go well in the cook pot," he said, and stabbed at it. The knife caught Moss a little. He was over the side and under the water before the man could try again.

The men began loading the crates onto the handcarts. Caspian, as a monkey, ran ahead of the carts, jumping between roofs to keep them in sight. Then he took a wrong turn and lost them. He stayed hidden and worked out that there would be at least three more trips, so he waited where he was. From there he could see the boats, the crew, the two women talking at the landing, and something we hadn't seen before. A proper covered wagon was standing at the edge of the Low Quay, well away from the handcarts. A light flashed on the water. A light flashed back from the wagon.

Anders wondered aloud whether there were two sets of smugglers, or whether one of them was no smuggler at all, and we'd almost gone after Nessa for nothing. Skylar and Caspian could each send a few words straight into another person's head, so long as that person was close enough. Skylar passed on to Moss what was happening. Bram said we might get a bonus for catching two smuggling groups instead of one. Wren told him that was a dumb idea.

Anders said he would sneak over and see what the other cart was. He could talk to all of us from there, he said, and tell us whether to come running. Moss counted up. Moss in the water, Caspian on the rooftops, Anders in the dark, and the rest of us in the blind. That was four ways.

Anders had a bad night for hiding. He had barely got near the wagon when a voice called out, "Oi! Come out where we can see you, or we'll shoot." A second voice said it was probably some kid, and not to worry, they knew how the rules went down this way. Anders came out of the shadows with his hands up.

He said he'd found himself in the wrong place at the wrong time and wanted to go home. They did not believe him. He said he worked nights at the docks and had forgotten his lunch. He asked who that lot down at the landing were, and whether they were with this lot, because he'd seen strangers and wanted to get away. It was not very convincing. "You're from the guild, aren't you?" one of them said. Anders said he'd done some work for the guild, repairing buildings in the Low Quay after the storm.

"Tell us who the other lot are," the man said, "and we'll let you walk. We know you work for the guild. We know what you're about." Stoneharbour ran on a sort of trust in these matters. People struck bargains all the time, and mostly they kept them. Anders said he'd do it.

As he spoke, he looked them over. They were not Low Quay people. They were dressed too well, in decent armour, with crossbows, which cost money. The boxes on the wagon were empty. Then he walked back into the scrub the way he'd come.

"Sorry," he told us, in our heads. "I'm just making another group of people hate us."

"It's okay," Moss said.

Caspian had traced the carts to a small, solid warehouse in the middle of the Low Quay. A crowd stood around it. He saw people go in and come out again, but there was no way in except the door they were using. He knew a few of the faces from our long months in Stoneharbour: Ysolt, Nessa, a couple of Jory's old crew. Dozens of people had a hand in this, if not hundreds. He went back to find the others.

* * *

Anders did not go back to the others. He went into the scrub, and then he turned around.

He wanted to see what the two men were doing. He crept back around the buildings and spoke into Fenwick's head. There were two other men, he said. He didn't know what they were smuggling, but there was nothing in their wagon yet. He was going to double back and have a look. He got close enough to hear one of the men doing all the talking, telling the other about the latest fight, how rough it had been and how well someone had done in it.

Then he noticed a man about fifteen feet behind him, creeping toward him through the dark. The wagon was in front of him now and the man was behind. There was nowhere left to go. "We're coming," Fenwick said, in his head. "Maybe come up this way," Anders told him.

He turned round and told the man to hold it right there. The man looked like one of the two from the wagon. "You're a sneaky one, aren't you?" he said. "What are you doing here?"

"Tell me what's going on and you won't have a problem, friend," Anders said.

"I don't think I'm going to have a problem with you, trash."

The man called over his shoulder, and a voice answered to the name Peter. Fenwick and Bram were running toward the wagon by then. "Tell me who you are," the man said, "and what you're doing here."

"No," Anders said. "You tell me what you're doing here."

The man stepped forward and stabbed him with a rapier. The other one shot him with a crossbow. Anders went down without a word. All that came out of him was the sound of air going slowly out of a bellows.

Fenwick did not see him fall. He saw a man standing at the wagon, another man next to him who did not move, and a crossbow being fired into the dark behind them.

* * *

Fenwick went for the nearer of the two men. He jumped, bounced off the wheel and climbed up onto the cart. Bram came in behind him, yelling, and swung his longsword. The man turned round and said, "Who the hell are you?" and drew a short sword. He didn't attack. He stood ready to parry, and Fenwick, who could have struck first, didn't.

"You tell me everything about these smugglers," Fenwick said, "and I won't smack you with old Smitey here."

The man laughed. "Get lost," he said. He wore a chain shirt and he looked confident. He had dropped a light crossbow on the cart, which was not a cheap weapon.

Down at the landing, the Low Quay crowd had noticed the shouting and seemed worried. Skylar saw it first, with Wren beside her. She saw a small single-masted boat sailing in as well, with two experienced-looking people aboard. "Well," Wren said, "I don't suppose we get the extra consideration now." "The boys have done it again," said Skylar. "Surprise, surprise."

She sent a message to Moss and to Caspian to say that Fenwick, Anders and Bram were in trouble and we had to go and help. Caspian was too far away. She tried anyway. Then she began to make her way up there, as fast as she could without being seen by the people at the landing. Wren stayed at her side. She could look after herself in a fight, but she was nowhere near as good as the rest of us.

A second man came out of the darkness with a rapier in his hand. There was blood on it.

"Where's my friend?" Fenwick shouted.

"What friend?"

"The one you just stabbed. A halfling."

"Oh, him. He's back there," the man said. "Don't worry. I bandaged him up. He's not going to die. We know what the rules are in this town. You don't cross the underworld."

Bram told them to hand our friend back if they knew what was good for them. "Forgive his sprightliness," Fenwick said. "I just want my friend back."

"What's your name?" the second man asked. Fenwick gave it as Benwick, from the guild, but it didn't work. They knew Anders worked for the guild. They didn't know him.

"You're causing us a bit of trouble," the second man said. "We did give your friend a warning. Why don't we just truss you up and ask the guild for the standard reward for people who interrupt our business?"

Skylar and Wren were within thirty feet by now. Caspian, still a monkey, was somewhere close. They could see Anders lying in the gutter, roughly bandaged, breathing. His hood was on the ground next to him. Caspian sent a message to Fenwick. *Anders is stable.*

"We don't like people interrupting our business," Peter said, "with our charms."

Fenwick stared. "So you are the smugglers," he said. They hadn't said anything about charms to Anders. Now they had told us the one thing we'd been sent to find, and it had cost them nothing.

Moss was under the jetty. He had heard the men at the landing say they should get the gear to the warehouse as quickly as they could. They hadn't said charms. Somebody called out a name, and he heard Nessa saying, "What the hell?" The men decided to carry the last of the crates back to the boats and row them out to sea.

Then the second boat came in, with two men and small sealed crates. They looked at the wagon, where Fenwick and Bram were standing. "We're being busted," one said. "Should we turn around?"

"They can handle themselves," the other said. "Let's go and help them."

Moss slipped out from under the jetty and fastened himself to the new boat. He would stay on it, he said, until it reached the shore.

From the second boat, a voice called to the wagon. "Peter? Everything okay?"

"All good," came the answer. "We've just got some riff-raff here."

"Riff-raff," Fenwick said, "which may skulk off into the night, yes?"

"I suggest you grab your friend and go," Peter said. "We'll have words with you later."

"I can live with that," Fenwick said.

* * *

Moss asked if there was any news for him. "Stay hidden," Caspian told him. "They don't know about us. We can follow them."

"For once," Caspian said.

"Not for violence," Moss said. "I thought you'd turn into a giant crab and go for the whole boat."

The mob from the Low Quay began to drift away. The first boats went out toward the sea. Moss sent word that they'd taken the last of the crates with them.

"Your buddy's back here," one of the men told Fenwick. "Come and get him." Anders hung between Fenwick and Bram with the crossbow bolt still in him. Skylar made the plan, in a low voice. We had been made, she said. We couldn't learn any more tonight, but we had enough to follow up, from the people we'd recognised. She and Wren would go with Anders, Fenwick and Bram. They would go back to the guild, and she would tell Caspian and Moss to leave when it was safe.

There were about twenty crates, one to a man on each trip. Moss waited until the men were gone from the boat. The crate he picked was nailed shut, and heavy but not too heavy, only awkward. He took hold of it with a few arms, slid it off the back of the boat and under the water, and swam for the jetty. The men were going slowly and carefully toward the wagon, and nobody noticed that one was missing. He hid it and did not tell us.

When the last of the men had gone, the wagon began to move. Caspian followed it. It rolled into the Ropewalks and stopped outside a two-storey building that looked like a tavern. It was rowdy inside, and busy. A couple of men started unloading the wagon. There was a cellar door at the side, where the ale was delivered.

They did not call themselves a thieves' guild, but he suspected that was what it was. Nobody had ever quite worked out who they were. He watched people going in and out. It wasn't the chatter of an ordinary tavern. It was rougher, the patrons looked capable, and he thought it sounded more like the common room of a company of mercenaries. But most of them were laughing and joking, with music playing. Nobody was sitting around talking about stealing or murdering people. Caspian decided that was as much as he could learn, and went back to report.

* * *

We were all back at the guild by then, and Moss said, dryly, that we'd done well not to give away who we were. It was true, in its way. We hadn't captured anyone either. "Still time," Anders said, wincing. He pointed out that we knew where Nessa lived. We could kick her door down, throw a sheet over her and bring her back to the guild. Jory was in the guild too, he said, so we could tell Nessa to talk, or else Jory would pay for it. He wanted to see what happened.

Moss had a small crate that he hadn't told anyone about. When he finally got it open, there were no charms in it, only a bottle of brandy and a small box of pipe tobacco. He closed the lid again and said nothing.

Then we slept. Anders slept longest, with a rapier wound in his stomach, and said he was sure he'd be fine.

* * *

In the morning we crowded into Slate's office, which was built for three people and a ledger. He stood behind his desk with the bowl of apples and asked if anyone wanted one. Moss took a second. Anders took none, and stood stiffly by the door, still grey in the face, with a hand held flat against his side. "Give me the good news," Slate said. "First, I assume no one died."

"Unfortunately," Anders said. "I can confirm that nobody died."

"Thank God you haven't upset anyone for once."

"I wouldn't say that," Moss said.

Skylar told it, with her sketchbook open on the desk so he could see the faces. There had been two groups, she said. Anders might have been recognised, and Anders said he had been, plainly. The rest of us hadn't been seen much. Fenwick and Bram had worn their hoods, and nobody had looked closely.

Caspian had followed both deliveries. One had gone to a warehouse in the Low Quay, with plenty of people around it, so it seemed to be well known what was going on. The shrine keeper and Nessa had overseen it. The other had gone to the tavern in the Ropewalks, and Peter had told us himself that they were bringing charms. "Peter was the one that shot me," Anders said. He took the crossbow bolt from his pocket and set it on the desk next to the apples. A few hours earlier it had been in him. Slate looked at it for a moment, and moved his apple away.

"Well," Skylar said. "And no children were harmed in the gathering of this information."

Anders said we might have a spy inside the guild. Who was Nessa's brother? Jory. "A recent indenture," Slate said. Some of Jory's old crew had been there, and Caspian knew a few of the names. Anders suggested keeping an eye on Jory to see whether he knew what his sister was up to. We didn't know when the next landing was, but we knew the signals now. The shrine flashed its long lights, and the boats flashed back.

"The merchants are paying us to find out who's bringing in goods that should be taxed," Slate said. "I suggest you go and finish the job. So you didn't capture anyone?"

"We thought we'd hold back," Caspian said. "Considering Jory's gang seemed to be involved."

"I'll ask Jory myself," Slate said. "Don't worry about that."

About an hour later he sent word. Jory knew nothing.

"His gang might," Caspian said. "It might be worth sending Jory in as a spy."

* * *

That evening we sat down in the barracks to work out what came next, around the long table with one lamp and the end of a loaf. Anders lowered himself onto the bench like a man carrying a full bucket. Bram and Wren had been put at opposite ends of the table, which was only a partial help.

Skylar counted her plan off on her fingers. We could watch the tavern in the Ropewalks, where Peter's charms had gone. Or we could talk to Nessa and find out what she'd been doing at the landing. If we split up, she said, we wouldn't look like our usual gang of very small and excessively large people, easy to spot from the far end of a street.

Caspian rubbed his eyes. "Sorry, I'm a bit lost, like Moss," he said. "Our orders are still just to find out about the charms. We know who has the charms. Peter and his lot admitted it."

"That doesn't mean they're the only ones," Skylar said. The Low Quay men had only said "gear". Anders said it had to be at least nefarious, if they were importing goods in the middle of the night. "It's got to be something," he said, and shifted on the bench and regretted it.

Bram said we should just go in and bang some heads around at the tavern. "You're an idiot, Bram," Wren said, and the two of them were at each other across the loaf before anyone could stop them. Skylar moved the lamp.

Anders said he wasn't going near the Ropewalks. They hated him there, and they knew it had been him at the wagon. He had upset people down there before, and they remembered his face. "I'll go with Nessa," he said, "but I need people."

Moss had been turning an apple core over in his fingers. He reminded us what Slate had said. Slate had never wanted the guild involved. He only wanted whoever was bringing the goods in found without being tipped off, so he could catch them and carry out the warrant. That was why he wanted someone taken somewhere secure. "I'm just clarifying," Moss said, "before we inevitably don't follow through."

Then he asked what we meant to do with Nessa once we'd finished with her, because the moment we let her go she would warn the others.

"So we're going to kidnap a sixteen-year-old," Moss said. He set the core down. "And put her somewhere."

Nobody argued with the description. The lamp hissed. Skylar asked whether Nessa was still living with Dela, and she was, in the same cook shop where we ate nearly every day, and one of us worked out that the moment she went missing Dela would be the first to ask us to help find her.

Anders said Wren might get hold of a sleeping charm, and we could put it in Nessa's drink. "I'm not touching Nessa," Moss said. Caspian said he'd identified other members of Jory's old gang hanging around the warehouse, and it might be easier to grab one of them. They would put up more of a fight, Moss pointed out. Anders said Wren's sleeping charm could go in the man's food at Dela's, and we could follow him home. That was where the conversation stood when Caspian changed the subject.

We did not settle it that night.

* * *

Before the day was out, Skylar and Wren had a talk. Maybe, they agreed, Anders should be manacled to a tree in future, to stop him causing trouble. A boulder might be more effective, Skylar said.

That night, while the rest of us were still talking, Moss slipped away. He went up to Shrine Hill and climbed a tree, the sort no one would look in, and sat there with the brandy. He didn't share it. He drank all of it. Then he lit a pipe of the tobacco and smoked that too.

Down in the barracks, nobody noticed he had gone.
