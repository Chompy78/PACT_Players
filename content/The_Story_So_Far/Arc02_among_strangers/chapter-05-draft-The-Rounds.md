# Chapter 5 — The Rounds

We were sixteen, and we owed the Iron Coin Guild seven hundred and fifty gold each, and on a cold morning at the end of that winter we walked out of Amble to start paying it back.

It took the better part of three weeks to reach Stoneharbour. It took about two months more to understand what we'd actually signed. The number was never the point. I'll get to what was.

* * *

I told you about the leaving already — the thin crowd, Bram shaking each of our hands, the turn at the top of the road where the whole village fits between two fenceposts. I'll add one correction and three things I kept back, because they belong to this part of the story and not that one.

The correction first. I told you it was snowing. Fenwick says it wasn't — that the morning was cold and clear, the first clear morning in weeks, and that it had to be, because the roads had been shut and the guild caravan had only got through overnight. He's probably right. Two wagons and a handful of riders in grey coats don't arrive through weather. I've carried the snowing version all this time anyway, and I notice I don't much want to put it down.

The first thing I kept back: as we passed the shrine, the bell rang. One note, unhurried, the same note it had rung our whole lives — and every one of us looked at each other instead of at it, because Mother Yela was dead and Wren was walking beside us, and there was nobody left in that building to be ringing anything. Nobody ever told us who did. We didn't go and look. You don't, at sixteen, with a wagon waiting. You just add it to the list of things you'll wonder about at night for the rest of your life.

The second thing: the crowd was mostly children. The adults who could stand had mostly stood, but that wasn't many, and so the faces watching us go were the faces we'd grown up herding and being herded with, and they thought — you could see them thinking it — that we were off on an adventure. One of the mentors, Yalen Fane among them, told us we were guildfolk now and would be respected wherever we went. We have thought about that sentence a great deal since. Old Piper had hauled himself out too, upright and untouched, the fever having apparently declined to argue with him, and given what his nonsense had done for the village that seemed only fair. Marek bid us farewell and rode off in the opposite direction, which felt right. We'd never once been going the same way as Marek, even when we were.

And the third thing, the one I've turned over most: Old Marn stood at the treeline, well back from everybody, and watched us go, and did not come down. He was the only man in Amble who had ever worn the guild's coin himself, which is a thing I did not know until that morning and have never once been able to stop knowing since. He lifted one hand. That was all of it. When we looked again he was gone, and the wagons were moving, and not one of us said his name out loud until we were a day down the road.

On the road out Wren tried to make it a conversation, because that is what Wren does with silence. What were we going to miss most, she wanted to know. Moss said his bed — a real answer, not a joke, or not only a joke; there is not a bed between Amble and the coast built for a person of Moss's dimensions, and he knew it, and he was right. Fenwick said his parents' bakery. Wren opened her mouth, and shut it again, and we let the answer stand exactly as he'd built it. Skylar said the freedom to range around town, and Wren, who had been reading up, said Stoneharbour was a big town, ten thousand people, right on the coast — and Skylar said that sounded like too many people, and Moss pointed out that none of us had ever seen the sea, and that was the conversation done, because there was nothing in any of our heads big enough to hang the sea on.

* * *

The road was the easiest part of that whole year, and I think we all knew it while it was happening, which is rare.

The borderland gave way to country we'd never seen. Rivers frozen bank to bank, four times the width of anything we had a name for. A market town whose square could have held a couple of Ambles, and the caravan didn't even stop in it, and nobody in it looked up as we went through. Then the hills flattened out toward a grey line on the horizon, and Wren said sea before any of the rest of us would commit to it, and we spent a full day disbelieving how flat it was, as though the world had run out of ideas.

Fenwick drilled with his blade every evening of that trip, up and down beside the wagons in the frozen grass, until the second cut arrived in the time the first used to take. Nobody had asked him to. Somebody was going to be ready for whatever was coming, he said, and it might as well be the shortest of us. And Skylar — this is true, I have the evidence — took up drawing. Wren's doing. The two of them sat up on the boards sketching whatever went past worth sketching, which by Skylar's account was mostly horizon. Us chicks have got to stick together, she said, in the voice she uses when she's daring you to comment.

The journey was uneventful. No brigands thought us worth the trouble, which with hindsight was the first professional assessment anyone in the wider world made of us. Somewhere in the second week the brands stopped hurting and settled into a dull rub, and we noted it, and did not celebrate it, and stopped mentioning it at all.

* * *

Stoneharbour came up all at once. One hour there was a grey line and a smell, and the next we were in it — a working harbour crammed with masts, quays slick with fish guts and scale, gulls in their hundreds, smoke, bells, and people. People past counting. The entire population of Amble seemed to be in sight at any single moment, and then you turned a corner and there was another Amble's worth, and another. We had saved a village. It had been, for one winter, the biggest thing in the world.

Nobody in Stoneharbour spared us a glance.

Corvin Thale had ridden with the caravan the whole way, and at the guild hall he saw us to the door of the junior barracks himself. The people in charge would meet us on the morrow, he said; there was food; we should introduce ourselves to the others. Then he walked away, and that was the last we saw of him for a long while. He had carried us three weeks like freight, and he delivered us like freight, signed for and stacked, and I will say this for the man: he never once pretended it was anything else.

The others were four. Corley Finn introduced himself first, which we would learn was characteristic — a half-elf of twenty, grade two, and grade two only a couple of months, and very much intending you to know both facts. Sena Voll, a half-elf of nineteen, older in some way that had nothing to do with the year, who we would later notice marked something small on the inside of her bunk-frame every morning and never explained it, and none of us ever asked. Tomkin Reed — Tam by the end of the first hour — a halfling who wouldn't say how old he was and looked about our age, and who was friendly the whole way through, which in that place was worth more than we understood yet. And Ordis Kemp, an elf of nineteen, who gave her name like she was handing over two facts she'd rather have kept, and went back to her kit.

They wanted to know why so many of us had arrived at once. Must be quite a village, Tam said, if six of you volunteered.

Volunteered is a struggle, somebody said. Fenwick said he had been volunteered, and Skylar supplied the word he was looking for, which was voluntold. And then it turned out that most of them were the same — Sena, Corley, Ordis, indentured every one, debts of their own or their families', none of them itemised for us. Fenwick asked whether they'd saved their towns as well. Nobody laughed, and nobody answered, and Tam said — kindly, and with the authority of a man setting out the house rules — that stories had to be worth something around here, and we'd all be in this barracks a long time, so there was no sense spending the good ones on the first night.

We learned the shape of the ladder that evening too. Grade one was entry, and you stayed there two years, three, sometimes four. Grade two much the same. An indentured member would come out of their seven years somewhere around grade three, at which point — Sena's voice went particularly flat here — the guild would finally start paying you properly, now that you were free to leave. And being young, we were told, we were lucky: we'd work less and train more. To make us useful.

Somebody was going to make us useful. We'd been hearing that since we were eight. It had always been the adults we loved who said it.

* * *

Quartermaster Hobbern ran the rosters, which meant Hobbern ran our lives. Hobb, to everyone, within the week. Courier running, warehouse work, sorting gear, cleaning leather, washing — the guild had an inexhaustible supply of the sort of work you give people it owns. But twice a week, on the second day and the fifth, we didn't labour at all. We were taught. How to lift without wrecking a back, how to tie knots that held, how to stow a bedroll, what to do when something's on fire, what to do when you want something to be on fire.

Hobb was a patient teacher — more patient than any mentor we'd had at home, and without the commentary — and he had plainly turned a hundred children like us into useful people before, and would do a hundred more, and there was something in that we found steadying and did not examine too closely. Skylar asked about magic and got a whole training day of it: theory, components, experiments, real tuition. Even she admitted it was good. Especially she admitted it was good.

Moss found the edges of the place fastest, because Moss suffocates indoors. One day off a week, and he spent every one of them out at the shrine — Selwyn Aske kept it, we learned, out along the green fringe of town — and he volunteered for every gardening chore the guild had, which is how Fenwick, who is three foot four, came to spend his first months in the greatest port on the coast weeding under bushes. He had views on this. He'd come here to make money and pay off the bloody debt, he said, and instead he was doing Moss's weeding so Moss could get more free time, and the injustice of it sustained him for weeks. There was no bakery in the whole of Stoneharbour, we'd discovered — ten thousand people and not one bakery — and Fenwick looked at that fact sideways for a long time, like a man checking a purse someone has left on a wall.

The others sorted themselves out, as people sharing a room do. Ordis took to Moss — she'd ask to come along to the shrine, being an elf a long way from anything green, and Moss said he was happy to have the company, and that was the entire negotiation. Tam adopted Anders. Corley adopted Caspian. Neither of them was consulted.

And the town taught us itself, in pieces. Three taverns: the Low Tide, the Tallow Lamp out by the warehouses, and the Brass Tide, which we categorised on sight as the hoity-toity one and never went into. The Market Steps, where the daily food markets ran, and something fancier every second week. You could draw down two gold a month against your debt if you wanted coins in your pocket, the debt swelling gently to accommodate you; otherwise the guild fed you and housed you and owed you nothing it would admit to. The market days put us in mind of home — Old Piper on his stool, telling stories every one of us could recite and none of us had believed, and how one of us had once thrown him a coin for it. After that winter it didn't feel like charity anymore. It felt like a debt we'd paid early, once, by accident, to the only creditor in our lives who'd ever come out ahead by talking rubbish.

* * *

The statements came at the end of the month, at mess, handed out by a clerk who walked the tables calling names. Somebody asked, when their name was called, whether they'd won the lottery. You don't owe me, the clerk said, not even unkindly. You owe the guild, boy.

Here is what a month of our lives came to, in a hand neater than any of ours. Earnings: twenty-five gold. Deductions — lodging, meals, laundry, kit, the fees whose names we were reading for the first time: twenty-five gold. Opening debt, seven hundred and fifty. Closing debt, seven hundred and fifty.

We had worked every day of it.

Sena, who did not look up from her own bowl, offered us the good news, which is that you only pay for your initial kit the once. From next month we'd clear two gold. Skylar did the sums out loud in her driest voice: only twenty-three more months, then, until grade two. Corley told us not to worry — at grade two you make more money, he made four gold now — and he meant it generously, he truly did, and we sat there doing long division against seven years and let him mean it. There were ways to make more, the others said. Windfalls. Anything you found and handed in, you got ten per cent of, minus the administrative fee. Moss said he'd thought the ninety per cent was the administrative fee, and even Sena's mouth moved at that.

The stores came with their own paper, of course — schedules of what a member might borrow, lettered A through C, and we were authorised to Schedule B: simple arms, light armour, healer's kits, ours whenever we wanted them, and charged to us at replacement plus an administrative fee if we lost them. Moss read that last clause all the way to the end and handed the barracks its motto. We are never leaving, he said.

Fenwick passed his statement to Moss to read out, letters not being his strongest point, and Moss read it, and it said the same as everyone's. We asked, around then, about our contracts — the actual paper we'd signed — and learned that we didn't have them. The certified copies had gone to Amble, to the households. If we wanted one of our own it was five gold, authorised, from records. Fenwick, who had been told his whole life never to sign what he couldn't read, and who had signed, addressed himself briefly to the god of luck: if I ever counted on you, it's for now.

The letter came the same week, and it was Fenwick's name the clerk called. One hand for most of it, and Bram's after. Moss read this one out too, at Fenwick's nod, and the mess went on clattering around us and we stopped hearing it.

*Lads and lasses. I'm not much for letters, and Bram has asked to put something at the end, which will make it long, so I will be quick. Nothing here needs worrying about. The fever is long gone. Nobody has taken sick since you left. Nell is up and about again, thinner than she was, and cross about being told to rest. The long barn roof is mended. There's a new baby at the mill — a girl — and they've not settled on a name, so half the village is arguing about it. The smithy is still cold. I will not dress that up for you. We are managing. Your papers are safe with us. The guild man left a certified copy for the household and it is in the box with the rest of what matters. I've read it. I would not have you think nobody here bothered. It says what it says, and it's all in order, and none of it's a mistake. When you get the chance to sit with your own copy, read the back of it, not the front. The front is for signing. Eat properly. Look after each other. Look after Wren.*

Wren was two seats down when that line was read, earning her own way — the guild had put her to administrative work, and she was the only one of the seven of us whose statement showed a number moving in the right direction. She studied the far wall while we all didn't look at her.

*Bram, after. I am well. Da is not much better, but he is up and about, which the healer says is more than she expected. Everyone here says what you did was brave, and I say it too. If anybody had asked me, I would have got on that wagon with you and not thought about it twice. Nobody asked. That is all. Write back if they let you.*

Nobody asked. We sat with that a while, each of us privately confirming that we had not, in fact, asked, and each of us deciding not to say so.

It cost money to send a letter back. Of course it did; by then we'd have been suspicious if it hadn't. Somebody drafted the reply out loud — *Dear Bram. Please send money. Or objects that can be sold* — and we laughed, and it was the wrong kind of laughing, and we did it anyway.

* * *

It was after the statements that we decided to spend money we didn't have on ale, which I offer in our defence as evidence that we were, whatever else had happened to us, still sixteen. The vote was unanimous. Archer's, it will surprise nobody who remembers the winter of the watered mugs, counted twice.

Corley warned us off it with his whole heart. We weren't meant to drink until we were twenty-one; we would not get promoted with this sort of behaviour; he said it as he said everything, certain the guild was listening and hoping it was. The only place in town that would serve people our age was the Low Tide, which everybody described with the same word, and the word was dodgy. Tam said he'd come along, which told us something about Tam. Wren came too, announcing with great dignity that nobody could make her drink, she wasn't twenty-one. Fenwick, being broke, borrowed the cover of it from Caspian, who said of course he could and didn't ask what it was for. And Skylar said she'd go for a wander through the markets and down along the harbour front instead, and was not going to any tavern with the rest of us, thank you.

I have to be careful here, because this is one of the places where our memories genuinely will not agree. The markets are where Skylar swears she spent the whole evening — the stalls, then a spot she'd found above the harbour where you could sit and watch the ships come and go, which she says was the best evening she'd had since Amble, and I believe her. And some of us also remember her at the table when it happened, and those two things cannot both be true, and neither of them will give way. She was wherever she was. What happened in the tavern happened either way.

What happened was this. The Low Tide was dirty tables and watered ale and a barkeep who poured Wren something that only mostly looked like water, and halfway into the first round a drunk came and sat himself down next to Ordis. Hadn't seen an elf like her in here for a while, he said. How about somewhere quiet. Ordis was not impressed and said so with her whole face, and the man put his arm around her, and it became clear at a speed I still find instructive that he was not going to take no from her at all.

Moss got up. He did it slowly, so the room had time to take the measure of the problem, and moved seats to sit close, and told the man — twice, the second time with fewer available interpretations — to leave. The man's position was that this was his drinking hole and the lady and he were having a talk. The lady was pushing his arm off her. And here is the detail I always give when I tell this one: every head in that bar turned to watch, and not one body moved to help. We were the entertainment. The barkeep's whole contribution was to walk down the bar and move a small sign to where Moss could read it. ALL DAMAGES ARE TO BE PAID FOR.

Even Stoneharbour's kindnesses came itemised.

The man went to kiss her, and she was struggling, and Moss took his arm and lifted him off the stool the way you'd lift a sack you suspect of leaking. The man swung his mug at Moss's head and missed, and then vomited down himself, at which point Moss considered the window, found it shut, took note of the sign, and put him out the door instead. The man picked himself up off the cobbles and promised Moss he'd pay for that, you little punk — the first and last person in this story to call Moss little — then doubled over, vomited again, and took himself off into the night.

Thanks for eventually stepping in, the barkeep said, to the room's general amusement, and some of the harder men along the bar looked Moss over with the particular arithmetic faces of men measuring a very large teenager against their own evening's plans, and Ordis said, maybe we should go home. Home meaning the barracks. None of us caught that till later — that she'd said it, or that we'd all stood up at once when she did.

Fenwick never did get his ale. And Wren, it turned out on the walk back, had got a very good drawing of the man mid-vomit, which she showed around under the streetlamps to unanimous acclaim. It was the last drawing of hers any of us asked to see for a while.

* * *

A few days after that, we were summoned to Factor Slate.

Slate ran the Stoneharbour branch, and I want to describe him carefully, because we got him so wrong at first and I don't want you to. His office was small and warm and immaculate. He finished the line he was writing before he looked up, and then he did look up, properly, and got our names right on the second go, and offered us the chairs. You're the new kids, he said. I hear you've had it pretty easy. You could have warmed your hands on the man's manner, and here is the thing we could not learn until we'd paid for the lesson: it wasn't a manner.

He had a simple job for us — his word, simple, deployed several times, to see how well we worked as a team. A courier round. He handed over a satchel and a manifest: seven parcels to deliver across the districts, a token collected at each for proof, and one item, marked, to be collected from an address on Low Quay and returned to the branch. Two gold each on completion. Fenwick cross-examined him about the two gold with the thoroughness of a man who had recently learned what a statement was, and established that it went straight to the debt and could not be spent, and pronounced it all funny money. Seven years of it, mate, said somebody, and even Slate's mouth conceded the point.

Skylar read the manifest's rules aloud on the way out, in her official voice. Parcels to be carried sealed; a broken seal is charged to the courier's account. Receipt is a mark or a name. Items marked for collection are logged for return to the branch, and are not the courier's to open, weigh, or discuss. Incomplete rounds are recorded against the courier and reassigned. Queries are a records matter; a records fee applies per application. We heard it the way sixteen-year-olds hear lists, which is to say the shape of it and none of the weight.

And Slate said one more thing before we went, in the tone of a man reminding children to wear coats. There was to be no killing in town. Especially not on guild business. No unauthorised killing, only genuine defence of ourselves, and any unauthorised interaction would be punishable; there was a standard debt if it was found out. We all but rolled our eyes. We were errand-children with a satchel of parcels.

Skylar sat down with the manifest and a map before we set out, because Skylar does not walk a foot further than the town requires of her, and found that the stops were already in a sensible walking order — all but the guild quarter one, which you could do first or last. Somebody had polished this route smooth long before us. The round was a rite, we realised. We were not the first children sent to walk it, and we would not be the last, and there was a comfort in that we were wrong to take.

Wren didn't come. She'd been collared for her admin work before we left — she'd try to catch us up later, she said. So it was the six of us and the satchel.

* * *

The first stop was Dela's cookshop on the Market Steps, and the first thing that happened there is that Moss stopped being available to the rest of the conversation, because there was a dog.

It was a grey, hopeful, half-official sort of dog, and it looked at Moss and Moss looked at it, and matters were settled between them on the spot. Dela — the cookshop was hers — took one look at the rest of us and diagnosed the guild before we'd said it. You look a bit young, don't you, she said. Sit down, sit down. And she went back into the kitchen and came out with a tray of steaming bowls, no charge, I know they don't feed you that well over there — bowls very nearly the scale of Fenwick — and Moss inhaled his in a time that impressed even us, who had watched him eat for sixteen years, and she took one look and brought him another. You look like you need it, growing man.

The dog was called Fluffy, we learned from the child who eventually came to reclaim him — a kid of twelve or so who hauled him off Moss by the collar with the weary authority of long practice. He's not Dela's, and he's not the kid's either; he hangs about because Dela feeds him, which as systems of belonging go was one we were coming to understand from the inside. The kid looked up at Moss on the way out — all the way up — and delivered the verdict of the house. He likes you. You must be a nice person.

I try, Moss said. Both halves of that were true. I didn't know yet how much room there was between them.

Fenwick, working, asked whether the household ever had old cooking things to part with — pans, utensils, anything past mending — and was refused on the spot by Cass, who did the serving and, we came to learn, the worrying: they were a cookhouse, Cass pointed out, they used their things, and had Dela been giving out free food again? The bowls were empty. There was no evidence. Dela promised Fenwick she'd put aside anything that broke — if we can't get it fixed, mind, there's someone in the house with a handy little mending spell — which is the most Stoneharbour sentence in this entire chapter: even the kindness had a mending spell under it, quietly unbreaking everything before it could reach us. She took the parcel into the back and came out with our token, and shouted after us as we left that if we were ever hungry and couldn't pay, a bit of table service would cover a meal, and that with the guild discount a bowl was two coppers anyway. Even Cass allowed the two coppers. Discount for more than one, Dela called. Cass shut the door.

The Ropewalks were next, and the Ropewalks picked our pockets.

It was children — a crowd of them, out of the lanes and around us before we'd finished being charmed by it. Skylar, who notices everything, noticed nothing, twice, which we would never once let her forget if the day had stayed the kind of day you joke about. One of them had her purse away clean. The rest mobbed us for coppers with their hands out, and by the time Fenwick had been grabbed and released and Moss had walked twenty yards with an entire child attached to his leg, Moss's purse was gone as well — a gold or so of Skylar's, about the same of his, everything either of them had drawn down. Then, on some signal we never caught, they were gone, all but one, who turned at the corner and shouted back that the place we were looking for was just down that street — which it was. Thanks for the tip, miss! We'd been robbed by the most helpful guides we'd had all day. Fenwick found that harder to forgive than the purses.

The shipping agent's office on the harbour front barely looked up at us: parcel taken, guild seal glanced at, token snapped across the counter. Outside it stood a statue of a mole — an actual mole, larger than Tam — and Moss, whose day had already contained a dog, asked the receptionist about it and received the town's official version: an adventurer came through sixty years back who liked moles, there used to be a tavern here, and somehow the town owed him a statue. It was a terrible story, and she knew it was a terrible story, and she'd clearly told it too often to care. Fenwick looked the mole over and asked whether it was bolted down.

We were, I want the record to show, having a good day. Of the whole seven years, I think that morning might have been the best of it: fed by a stranger, robbed by children, arguing about a mole. The marked item was the next stop. It was just an address on Low Quay.

* * *

Low Quay is the poorest edge of the harbour, and you know it before anyone tells you. The streets narrow. The stones go mossy and unmended, potholes nobody with money ever trips in, and the sea sounds close enough to be a lodger. The address was a net-mending room, and the woman who opened the door to us was old, and small, and already smiling.

How can I help you, good young folk?

I have thought since about how much of what she was reached us in that first second, before anyone spoke — that visitors came rarely to that door, and that she had decided at sight of us that we were good news. We were six young people in guild grey with a satchel. She'd have seen the grey. She smiled anyway.

Skylar said we were here to pick up the marked item for the guild, and the smile went out like a lamp in a draught.

Oh, she said. Yes. You'll have heard about my loom — sorry. My mending frame.

We had not heard about anything. We were not the courier's to open, weigh or discuss. The frame stood in the corner of the one room, a big worn thing hung with a half-finished net, the tools laid out beside it rubbed smooth by decades of the same hands, and she looked at it and said she'd have to go pack it into the crate, and Moss asked if he should help, and she showed him how. She took the half-finished net off it first, carefully, the mending not being the guild's.

Moss forgot the rules almost at once, because they were Moss's kind of rules, which is to say somebody else's. Why are we taking this, he asked the room — what is it to the guild — and Skylar reminded him, evenly, that items marked for collection were not ours to discuss, and Fenwick observed that he couldn't see an item marked for collection in the box yet, and while we were conducting that argument in the doorway of her one room the old woman answered the question anyway, because she was the only person there entitled to.

She'd taken a loan from the guild for the frame, she said, when her last one was destroyed — hooligans, breaking in, smashing what there was to smash. And she hadn't managed to keep up the payments. That's all right, she said. I'll make do. Moss asked whether there was a way for her to get it back, and she said yes — there was, that was the mercy of the arrangement, you could always redeem it — and then told us the figure, ten gold, in the voice of a woman naming a mountain. She didn't make that much, she said. And since her husband had passed, and the children had left, it was — she used the words a bit hard. Then she straightened, because we were young and visibly sinking, and told us chin up, it wasn't the end of the world.

I've still got my hands, she said.

She showed us her hands.

She meant it for comfort. That is the part I have never got out from under — she held up two hands going stiff at every knuckle, fingers that no longer fully answered, forty years of net-mending written into every joint, and she offered them to us as the good news, and Moss, who knows hands, who was an herbalist's whole apprenticeship by then, looked at them and said nothing at all. He told her instead that we'd be careful with the frame — extra careful, all the way back — so that the option was always there. She smiled at him properly then.

Oh, I don't think it's going to matter, boy, she said. But you never know.

Wren arrived in the middle of it all — she'd finished her filing and tracked us down the manifest, and she came through the door bright and stopped inside two steps, reading the room off our faces, which with our faces was not difficult. That looks like one of the things they use for making nets, she said, of the crate. The guild wants it back? Nobody explained it to her. That was its own answer, and she put her sketchbook away.

The old woman — Sabine, we heard a neighbour call her, later, through her door — did not watch us carry the crate out. She dragged a stool to the window instead, and hauled the half-finished net across her knees, and took up mending it by hand, the slow way, the one that had cost her the hands. I've a lot to do, she said. Got to get this finished by tomorrow. She asked, while we stood there holding her livelihood in a box, whether we'd like anything to eat.

We said no. We wouldn't want to put her out.

Going out the door, Moss set down the cup of coppers he had left — all he had left, after the Ropewalks — on the corner of her table, quietly, or what Moss believes is quietly. She gave us a full smile as we left, the first one back, the good one.

Then the door shut behind us, and through it we heard her start to cry.

We stood on those green stones and listened for exactly as long as it took to make ourselves start walking. Wren wanted to know what we'd just done. The courier rounds we'd been told to do, somebody said. Why is she crying, Wren said, and Skylar said, evenly, that it would be her livelihood, and Moss said the other thing, the true thing, in a voice I hadn't heard him use before: they could have taken her hands. It'd have cost them less.

We're not meant to discuss items marked for return, Skylar said, without any conviction at all.

We didn't discuss it, said Fenwick.

We are now, said Moss.

Further along the quay a man passed us going the other way, and knocked at her door, and we heard him call — Sabine? You all right in there? — and we walked faster, all six of us, without anybody proposing it.

* * *

We had four stops left and nothing to say, so we said it about the mole.

Passing the statue again, Wren offered the version she'd heard in the counting-house: this whole quarter, once, infested by mole men. A passer-by overheard and corrected her on the spot, scandalised — it was no such thing, the mole was a wizard, petrified himself miscasting, turned himself into a mole and stone in the one mistake, a lesson about magics you don't understand. Three stories now, each worse than the last, and we laughed at that mole harder than it has ever deserved, before or since, because it was the only thing on that street that didn't owe the guild money.

Canvas & Batten, the sailmakers', was the next token: a loft above the keel yard, bolts of canvas, a floor pinned all over with half-finished sails, and the first stop of the day where somebody our own age answered. She fetched us mugs of cold water without being asked and hovered while the receipt was signed — Juna, though we didn't have the name yet — and it was she who looked past us at Wren and asked, in front of everyone, why the small one looked so upset.

It's been a tough day, said Moss. Heavy box.

We made someone cry, Wren said.

You weren't there, somebody objected, and Wren said no — she'd just watched it happen, hadn't she, which closed that line of defence for good. The sailmakers, to their credit, asked no further. You're the new lot at the guild, then, one of them said. You're not as bad as I thought you'd be. There it was again — the town's highest compliment, handed over like a token, not as bad as expected. And as we were leaving, the girl mentioned there was a dance on later in the week, out past the yards, a copper to enter, if we were interested.

We said maybe. We meant no. We were wrong about that too.

* * *

The lane between the warehouse rows ran so narrow that the stacked crates put us in single file, and Skylar, sorting us by width, told Fenwick this one was his — he'd fit. That is the last easy thing anyone said for a while.

There were two of them, waiting where the lane widened, which meant they had chosen the ground and we had walked our whole column into the neck of it. Young. Our age, or near enough that it doesn't bear thinking about, and underfed in a way we were still learning to read, with little wooden clubs. That satchel looks heavy, the first one said. We should help lighten it for you.

Skylar told them no. She told them no several times, each version shorter, and the last one was that they could walk away while they still had their legs — which I set down here exactly because we have never been allowed to forget she said it. They came on anyway. Sorry, the second one said, but we're just going to have to insist — and there was something almost formal in it, as though we were all reading from cards, as though everyone in the lane knew the usual ending: a scuffle, a snatched bag, boys running. They had picked the ground well. It was the rest of it they got wrong.

Skylar took the parcel off Fenwick and sent him forward. Fenwick stepped into the gap and drew, from his belt, his rolling pin — and I want it honestly on the record that this was funny, that we were sixteen and it was funny, for one more second. Time to roll some pastries, he said.

And Moss, three back in the file with an old woman's livelihood in his arms, said one word to the goat.

Brambleback went past our legs like water finding a gap. One of them got his club down on the goat's back, once, hard. What happened after that took less time than it takes to tell, and I am not going to make it a scene, because it wasn't one. The goat hit the boy low and did not stop, and the boy came apart against the stones, and by the time Moss got the word out of his mouth — the word was *hold* — there was nothing in the lane left to hold back from. A mountain goat at a dead run had gone through him like a cart. The second boy watched his friend die in the space of a breath and turned and ran, screaming as he went, and Fenwick — this is the part I need you to keep — went after him and struck him once, carefully, with the flat of a fighter's judgement behind it, and the boy went down and stayed down, breathing.

It was possible to do it gently. That is the whole of what I know about that lane. It was possible, and one of us proved it, seconds too late to matter.

Fenwick was on his knees by the first boy with the healer's kit open before the second had finished falling. There was nothing in that kit for what the goat had done. Six seconds, perhaps, start to end. Moss stood in the lane and looked at what his own summoned creature had made of a child, and Skylar held the satchel in the neck of the lane and did not move, and somewhere down the alley a third one — younger, I think; I have decided not to be sure — screamed. Not a warning. The other kind. Then running feet, going away, and the gulls, and us.

Fenwick went through their pockets, because the guild had taught us that too. Five coppers, between the two of them.

Five coppers, and the clubs.

* * *

We finished the round. I keep needing to say it plainly, so: we picked up the satchel and the crate and we finished the round, every stop, every token, in order, because the manifest said incomplete rounds were recorded against the courier, and we were couriers now.

Somebody was following us within a few streets of the lane — hooded, young, early twenties by the walk of them, keeping distance, keeping it well. Moss watched them about as subtly as Moss does anything. We told ourselves it might be the guild, someone with a clipboard sent to see how the new team worked, and Skylar pointed out that we had painted a laneway red, and it was a bit hard to miss the redecorating. Nobody laughed. Wren walked at the back with her sketchbook out and would not walk beside Moss — she didn't announce it; she was simply never next to him — and when she said, to nobody in particular, that she didn't think we should have killed him, there was half a breath in which the idea of asking her to keep that to herself existed among us. Skylar ended it before it drew air. Nobody was silencing Wren.

Fenwick asked Moss, quietly, whether he'd put the goat away for a while. It's kind of just a trail of gore leading back to us, he said, which was accurate. Moss didn't answer, and the goat stayed, limping along at his heel with its horns still dark, and I understood years later that Moss could no more have unsummoned Brambleback in that hour than cut off his own hand — that sending the goat away would have meant being alone with it.

We went back by Low Quay. Fenwick wouldn't go in — wouldn't come near the door, even — but he crouched at it and posted the five coppers through the gap beneath, one at a time, and walked away fast. They were the only coins the six of us had left in the world, and I have done the moral arithmetic of where they came from and where they went a hundred times since, and I am telling you plainly that it has never once come out.

The Market Steps stop was pleasant, ordinary, glad of its parcel. You lot look a bit stressed, the merchant said. A long day of walking, we said. There was muck on our boots. Then the guild quarter, grade two lodgings, stair three, whatever that meant — the addresses of that town were a filing system wearing streets — and by dark we stood in front of Factor Slate with seven tokens and one crate.

Excellent, he said. Excellent. And then, pen ready, in the voice of a man asking after the weather on the road: anything of relevance I should be aware of?

There may have been an incident, Skylar said.

Oh yes? You didn't kill anyone?

No, said Skylar, and I want you to understand that Skylar is good — good at exactly this, the level look, the bored voice — and that it did not survive even one second in that office. She tried it twice. Slate put his pen down. That was all he did. I have been in rooms with drawn steel since that frightened me less than that pen going down, and so, evidently, had Skylar, because what came out of her next was the truth, complete, in good order: the narrow lane at Warehouse Row, the two of them, the clubs, the demand for the satchel, the warnings given, the advance. Fenn and Moss moving to fend them off. Moss's goat proving — her exact words, and they have never left any of us — a little more effective than anticipated in deterring one of the humans. A laneway may have been redecorated in red, she said.

Slate picked the pen back up and wrote, and read out what he wrote, which I think was the cruellest procedural kindness available to him. So. Fatality — Moss.

An unauthorised death, he told us, without heat, had to be paid for. Paid to the victim. In this case, he said, the victim would most likely prove to be the Thieves Guild — and there it was, dropped into the office like a coin into a well: the boy had belonged to somebody. Everyone in this town belonged to somebody, and every one of those somebodies kept a ledger. There would also, Slate said, be a great deal of paperwork, and he was not happy, and unless there was anything else of relevance, we were done here.

Fenwick asked, on the steps outside, whether he could take half the blame — he'd been in the lane, he'd drawn first, he'd started the pastry line. Moss would not let him. You didn't even see what was going on, he said. My goat. It was next to me. It was defending me. Mine. And that was that; Moss has never in his life handed anyone else the heavy end of anything, and he was not going to start with a dead boy.

* * *

The price came back inside a few days, as prices there did. Fifty gold, for the death — added to Moss's side of a ledger that a month of work moved by two. But the fifty gold was not the news. The news, which found us through Corley and Sena and the sideways looks of the whole junior barracks, was what the town had heard: not that the new guild children had killed somebody, but *how*. Excessively, was the word that came back. A child, torn nearly in two, in a lane, by something the big one keeps. There were parts of Stoneharbour, we were given to understand, where we would now be unwise to walk.

Wren was not speaking to Moss, in the particular way where she spoke to him with complete politeness about nothing at all. Skylar tried the other approach, needling him toward the surface — quite the spectacular show of power, she offered — and Moss finished it for her, flat: of excessive force. On a small child. And there was nowhere for anyone to go after that, so nobody went anywhere, and the barracks was very quiet, four strangers and six of us and everybody's eyes on their own kit.

It was Ordis who moved first. Why don't we go out to the shrine tomorrow, she said to Moss — you look like you need it. That cost her more than we understood at the time; she'd spent three years being nobody in that barracks on purpose, and she stood up in front of everyone and attached herself to the boy the whole town was talking about. Moss said the only redecorating he planned from now on was his little garden of herbs. It was the nearest thing to a joke he had, and she took it, and they went to the shrine.

And Fenwick — I record this because the chapter owes it to us — chose that evening to come in having somehow acquired a ladle. Just a ladle, out of nowhere. Things find Fenwick. He'd asked Dela to put aside broken kitchen things a week and a lifetime ago, and here the world had sent him a ladle, whole, and he held it up in the doorway of that silent barracks without a word of explanation, and for one moment the room was almost all right.

A few days later someone came to the guild gate asking after Skylar. It was the girl from the sail loft — Juna, we learned properly now — wanting to know if we were still coming to the dance. The address, the field past the yards. A copper to enter. Skylar said maybe; it depended how much free time the guild left us. Wren said definitely, she'd go for a dance.

And then the campaign began to get Moss there, over Moss's dead body — I don't think he's safe in public places at the moment, Skylar said, in front of him, which was its own sort of kindness, giving him the joke to live inside instead of the fact. Moss said he wasn't dancing. Fenwick reported to the barracks at large that Moss was going. Ordis was going. Caspian was going. The matter was settled in the usual manner, which is to say around him, warmly, until resistance stopped being worth the effort.

* * *

The dance was a field with a band in it, and fifty or sixty people, and lamps, and for one evening nobody handed us a statement.

Juna met us at the gate as promised and steered us in, bright as paint, talking the whole time. Tam came, and was immediately everywhere, demonstrating halfling dances and explaining over the music that they were superior to gnomish dances, gnomes being — for their size — weirdly uncoordinated, an assessment delivered two feet above Fenwick's head with great good nature. He teased Fenwick into his shell and half back out of it. Wren and Skylar went through that crowd like they'd been issued it — between the two of them I think they met the whole field — and when people asked where we were from, we told them Amble, out near the wild, the frontier, and made it sound like a place where things happen, which we could do now with a straight face, because here nobody could contradict us. Our town's full of weirdos, Skylar told a circle of them, but the country's lovely once you get past the people. The weirdos in question were dancing behind her at the time.

Moss stood at the edge and got through a couple of cups, with the goat lying in the grass behind him like a rumour nobody would confirm, and Ordis took him out for exactly one dance, which had been the negotiated figure. He stepped on her feet. He stepped on several people's feet, and nearly went over entirely at one point, and the field's verdict, forming in real time around him, was the one verdict that could have done him any good: not a monster. An oaf. You could see the town deciding it, and you could see it reaching him, and it did more for him than anything any of us had said all week.

At some point in the evening he leaned down to Corley — Corley, who'd come along and spent the night being grade two at everyone, and who was, we were slowly working out, not actually a bad sort under the polish — and asked him straight: how much trouble am I in?

You're fucked, said Corley, pleasantly. It'll be expensive. And it sounds like you've annoyed the underworld, which is a bad idea. They won't kill you, luckily. They'll just make you pay.

That was also the night we first heard the name Odalys Praed — the person the underworld belonged to, as the branch belonged to Slate — and noticed how quietly people said it, even in a field, even over a band.

Juna kept finding reasons to be wherever Skylar was, and Skylar told her, in the friendly and completely final way she has, that she wasn't here for a date; she was here to get rid of a debt. You've got to enjoy yourself when the opportunities come up, Juna said. That's why I came out dancing, said Skylar, and went on dancing. Juna, we'd learned by then, was articled to the sailmakers — seven years, the same seven as ours, except that she had chosen hers, and paid for it, and it ended in a trade and a vote and a life. She was the same age as us and she was the road not taken, standing right there in the lamplight, cheerfully unaware of being anything of the sort, and we liked her far too much to explain it to her.

We walked back to the barracks with the band still going behind us, six of us and Wren and the goat, and I did the day's sums the guild had taught me to do. Fifty gold for a dead boy. Ten for an old woman's hands. Two coppers for a bowl, one to get into a dance, and five posted under a door that will never once come out of the arithmetic. We were learning what things cost in Stoneharbour. That was the lesson the rounds were for, I think, though I doubt the guild meant us to learn it all the way down.

And somewhere in the town that didn't know our names, somebody who knew them exactly had already opened a ledger of their own and written us into it. They wouldn't kill us, luckily.

They'd just make us pay.
