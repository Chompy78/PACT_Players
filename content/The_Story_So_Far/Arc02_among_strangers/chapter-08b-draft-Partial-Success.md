# Chapter 8b — Partial Success

The Guild called it a contract. By now we had other words for it, and we'd stopped saying most of them out loud.

It didn't start there, though. It started at Dela's, on a day off, with a plate of something hot and cheap in front of each of us, and Nessa glaring at Caspian from the kitchen door. That was ordinary. After the month we'd had, it was almost restful.

The regulars were all there, as always. Toby, the boy Dela had taken in, was hauling bowls between the tables. Dela was wiping down the counter. Nessa had worked out long ago which of us she wanted nothing to do with, and we had worked out which of us to send to the counter so she'd never have to carry our food. It was a system. It worked. It also meant that a bowl of soup at Dela's came with a small, steady dose of being disliked.

Tam was gone. His things had gone with him, and word had come down that his contract had been bought, which is the best thing that can happen to anyone in the Guild and which still felt like a friend leaving without saying goodbye. "He kept his head down and did what he was told," Anders said. "That's Tam." We agreed that was probably exactly why. Sena hadn't turned up either. We'd known she was missing for weeks now, and had done nothing about it except keep eating the goodwill lunch, and Fenwick pointed that out with his mouth full.

Dela came over when the rush eased. She told us, kindly enough, that Nessa really didn't like us, and that she wasn't being unfair about it. She'd also heard we had a few problems in a few parts of town. Low Quay, mostly. It wasn't wise, she said, to upset everyone in the place we were going to be stuck in for a good while yet.

"We're trying to make amends," Anders said. "We helped get rid of a really nasty piece of work up at the shrine."

Dela said the shrine did its own thing, and the rest of the town left it to. But she'd heard we'd worked hard during the floods, and some people remembered that. "So not all bad," she said, and went back to her counter.

"We should just cause more floods," Moss said. "More natural disasters. Be very popular."

Fenwick pointed a spoon at him and said it was the best plan anyone had come up with all month. Even Anders didn't argue. Behind the counter Dela laughed, a short, real laugh that seemed to catch her by surprise, and a while later a basket of the good bread arrived at our table that none of us had ordered. She brought it over herself, so that Nessa wouldn't have to. She didn't say what it was for, and we didn't ask. It was the first time in weeks that we'd sat down and been, for the length of a meal, only hungry and glad of it.

From the next table we could hear the regulars telling Dela off again, the way they did every week. She charged too little. She was too good to people. How, one of them wanted to know, was she going to pay for Toby's healing? Dela said something quiet back and kept ladling. We'd stopped being surprised by how much of her generosity was held together by nothing at all.

Skylar had missed the last couple of weeks of us, and wanted the whole story, so we gave it to her out of order and argued about the details. She'd been there when the cobblestones were painted red, she said, but she hadn't been there for the rest, and we told her about the girl at the door and the brother now living in our barracks in a different dorm, and the balaclavas. She heard us out. "Anyone else we've upset?" she said.

Nobody answered. Fenwick started counting on his fingers, ran out of fingers, and went back to his soup.

We told her about Sabine, because she'd want to know. Sabine had sold her memories to get by. She'd done it, and now she didn't remember she'd ever been a net-mender at all. The woman who'd bought them was up in the market, and we knew where she was. This led, as it always did, to the slivers. Anders explained them badly and Moss explained them worse, and in the end it was easier to say what we'd been told: a sliver is a piece of somebody's life, however it's taken. It might be their strength, or their years, or their memories. Anything that makes up a person can be cut out, and turned into something somebody else can swallow. One of us had once traded a rumour for one, the rumour had turned out to be false, and it had upset some people down there who did not forget. Then there were the boxes from the ship in the storm, the candle and the thick pale fluid. Anders had drunk the fluid. We had all agreed never to describe what came out of him afterwards.

That was the whole of our knowledge, laid out on a table between the bowls. It felt like a lot at the time. It was a small fraction.

* * *

The rest of the day went to whatever each of us wanted, which meant most of us wanted nothing sensible.

Anders wanted Fred. That was nothing new. He'd been told a few of the things we had borrowed hadn't come back, and he had returned every one of them, and somebody was going to pay for that. So he went to the stores and asked to see the ledger where we signed things in and out, and made a point of saying he was checking his own debt, which the man behind the counter found very funny.

The man said Fred was hardly ever there, and that he doubted Fred would have done anything of the kind. Then he opened the book anyway and asked, "Your name was?" He seemed to know who Anders was before he said it.

Anders found the line. There was the item, and there was the signature beside it, and it was his. It even looked like his. Anders is not a man with a complicated signature, just a big A and a flourish, the kind he'd probably scratch into a wall. Every borrowing was supposed to be witnessed by someone at the stores. There was nowhere in the book that said who.

"That's poor practice," Anders said. He asked to see the roster. He asked to speak to the manager. The clerk chuckled, slid the book back across the counter towards his own side, and looked at him until he left.

Anders thought about coming back later and looking around when nobody was at the desk. He thought about it for some time. Then he remembered that sneaking around the Guild's own records on a day off, and getting caught, would be about the worst thing he could do to himself. He also realised he hadn't actually asked Fred anything, and wasn't planning to. What he settled on was different. He'd become very good friends with Fred's mother.

Nobody thought that was a good plan. Nobody stopped him either.

Elsewhere in the Guild grounds, Caspian went to the library. He'd told us he wanted to learn medicine properly, and he'd had the run of the shelves for months already. Good books, quiet room, and nobody in it who knew who he was. It suited him.

Fenwick went to the weaponsmith with his rolling pin. Anders made hitting people look easy, and Fenwick's pin wasn't making it look like anything. He wanted to keep his pin, since he'd feel lost without it, but he wanted it to do more. The smith offered nails, which Fenwick liked and the smith, sensibly, did not. What he suggested instead was to core out the middle and pour in molten lead. Fenwick said, with real feeling, that it sounded mercurial. It cost five silver for the lead and five for the work, so a gold piece all told, taken as an advance.

Wren sent word that afternoon that the shrine was quite happy about how things had turned out at its door. A bit of rough justice, they apparently felt, wasn't the worst thing in the world. Anders remarked to Caspian that the druids were his people, weren't they. The rest of the day went quietly. Moss, Skylar and Archer, as far as any of us remember, did nothing at all with it, which was probably the wisest use of a day off any of us made.

* * *

The next morning the job board had a single line for us: *See Slate.*

It was pouring. That's what I remember, more than anything else. Rain on the courtyard and rain on the office window. That, and how uncommon it was. A message like that came maybe once a month, or once every two, and only when a job had something awkward inside it.

Factor Slate had the door shut and a bowl of apples on his desk. "Would anyone like an apple?" he said. Fenwick said yes. Anders, who was closer, said yes faster, and the apple was in his bag before either of them had finished the word. Fenwick took his time choosing one, and took it hard.

Slate said that, given how some of us were with excessive force, he'd thought this job would be right up our alley. Then he slid a paper across the desk, and we all read it, in a room that smelled of wet wool.

It was a proper Guild contract, with a number on it as long as a sentence. The work was at the Ropewalks. The pay was four gold pieces each, applied to our accounts, which meant, as ever, that we'd never actually hold it. The job was to go to a tannery and deal with the woman who ran things there. Slate called it an authorised termination. Skylar asked whether that meant a lawful killing, and Slate said it did, as opposed to an unlawful one. "A withdrawal of a life contract," Skylar said. Nobody laughed. It was, we all felt, a very Guild sort of phrase.

Lethal force was allowed on her, and on any of her people who helped her. Everyone else on the premises was to be brought in or let go, depending. That last part turned out to be the one that mattered. There was a clause about prisoners. Slate said the Guild suspected people were being held there against their will. The woman was, in his word, a smuggler, and what she smuggled was people.

"Well," Anders said, "this sounds like a job for Caspian. He's already dealt with a girl in a doorway."

Caspian said he had never killed anybody. He said he wasn't planning to start today. But if what she was doing was what Slate described, if she was keeping people locked up somewhere, then that changed the sum. He'd guarantee her death. Anyone else who came at us, we'd answer in kind. And anyone who wasn't fighting should be let go. Slate agreed with that, with the small, tired look of a man who had expected a longer argument.

Moss signed his copy at once. He owed the Guild too much to be choosy, and everyone in the room knew it. Skylar asked what the woman had done. Slate said it wasn't for us to know. We didn't ask questions, Moss said. We signed contracts, and we completed contracts, and we painted cobblestones.

Then Slate mentioned, as if it were nothing, that a team had gone into that tannery three weeks before, and hadn't come out.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"And you came to us," Moss said.

Slate told us, quite gently, that our standing in the Guild was not particularly high. We were, in other words, what the Guild could afford to lose. Moss said so out loud, which saved Slate the trouble. Caspian mentioned that a certain family home and a certain door still counted against him, and Slate did not disagree.

Clause five said we had to take an inventory of the goods we recovered. Inventory was Fred's department. Anders read the clause twice, and for the first time all morning he looked happy.

We were allowed our normal gear from the stores. Anders said, pleasantly, that after the business with the borrowed goods he wouldn't be taking anything at all, and he went down to make sure everyone knew it. He wrote his name and the date in the ledger, and then he ruled a line straight through the space where the items would go, and signed under it, so that nobody could add anything afterwards. The clerk read this. The clerk's name, we learnt, was Lovis, and he did not enjoy it. "If you want something, write it down," Lovis said. "If you don't want anything, don't write anything. Don't waste my time." Anders thanked him warmly and said it was so nobody would forge his signature, and Lovis gave him a look that lasted a good deal longer than anything he'd said.

The rest of us stood behind Anders with no equipment and nothing to do. Caspian asked Lovis how far off we were from better gear. Lovis said it took a promotion, and a promotion took a reputation of seven, and he turned the ledger round so we could see. Fenwick had four. Archer and Skylar had three. Anders and Moss had two.

"Two more than I expected," Anders said.

Nobody offered to lend us a spear.

* * *

We walked out to the Ropewalks in the rain. Caspian asked whether we were going to hit the place right away, or wait, or watch first. The contract said it could be done whenever we liked, at the discretion of the members. We didn't know how many people the woman at the tannery had, and we'd rather count them before we met them. So it was settled: watch first.

Caspian took a crowbar from the stores, on the principle that a door was probably involved. Nobody asked what had happened to the last team's crowbar. Someone else offered a grappling hook, and nobody wanted it. Someone joked that we already had a portable ram, or a goat. Moss said that if it came to it he could become an elk and run the door down, and we all thought that was a wonderful idea. It was, in fact, a very poor one, and we'd find that out in a couple of hours.

The Ropewalks are long, low and dirty: rope sheds and drying lines and things hung out that ought to have been kept indoors. People there watch strangers as a matter of course. They watched us more than usual. A handful of them picked us out within minutes and kept watching without pretending otherwise, and most of that was for Moss. He and Brambleback were known here. Moss was walking down the middle of the street with a large half-painted goat, which is not a way to be stealthy.

Someone suggested dyeing the goat. Caspian suggested red, which got him a look. Then Moss said if we wanted to be sneaky, the smartest thing was for him to go one way and the rest of us to go the other, and every eye in the district would follow the giant. It was a genuinely good idea. We're honestly not sure whether we did it. Our memory says we talked it over and kept walking together, but it may be that he wandered off for a while, and nobody thought to ask.

Two kids came up asking for change. Anders gave them a copper each, and offered another if they'd tell him what was in the warehouse, the one where all the people were kept. "Which one?" the kids said. There were three, apparently. Anders offered three copper for three warehouses, and the kids wanted silver, and none of us had any. Fenwick said he barely had shoes. Then Anders told them we were looking for some new friends. One of the kids said he'd had a friend once. He'd died, a while ago. He said it flatly and left it there, and after that he stood well clear of Brambleback.

Nobody said anything until he'd wandered off.

About twenty minutes later an older kid came up, wanting to know if we were looking for people in a warehouse. "Maybe," Anders said. "What's it worth?" The boy said he had information about people in a warehouse. Anders said he was really looking for a man named Nanya, and gave the last name as Business, and the boy said he hoped we'd have a good visit to the Ropewalks. He went off. We were all fairly sure we'd just been made a fool of, and even more sure we'd done the same to him.

The tannery stood on its own, the way tanneries do, because it stinks. It was old and boarded, and every window had been nailed shut. Whoever had shut it up had done it thoroughly. There was one door. Four men sat in front of it playing dice. They looked local, and bored.

We argued about how to get in, for what felt like hours and was probably about half of one. Someone wanted to watch it until dark and cause chaos then. Someone wanted a frog sent up the waste pipe, and someone else pointed out what happens when you change back inside a pipe. Moss walked a slow circle from a distance, studying the ground for a second path, and came back sure that there wasn't one. The tannery was a big workshop, probably a dozen paces across, with high ceilings and a lot of dark inside it. It had a single entrance and four men in front of it. We talked about waiting for night and we talked about the frog, and we did neither.

Then we watched three children walk up to the men at the door.

The guards knew them. You could tell by the way they waved. The kids said something, and the four men stood up and looked worried, and one of them opened the door and went in. He came out a minute later. The kids scattered.

They looked like the same kids we'd spoken to. We had been made.

That changed what we thought the men were. They didn't seem like the woman's own people. They seemed like locals who'd been paid to sit by a door and keep the riff-raff away. Moss said he didn't think we were allowed to kill them. Caspian said that if we didn't have to, we shouldn't. Anders said that was how it had started last time, and Moss said the next sentence would be *burn them all*, and for a moment no one spoke, because that was exactly what most of us had been thinking.

* * *

Anders went first, on his own, because he had an idea.

"I'll go and have a chat," he said, and set off across the open ground with a candle in his hand. It was the one he'd carried off the sinking ship, and he said he'd bet it. He told the guards he needed to earn some coin, that he was down on his luck, that nobody in Low Quay would speak to him. Did they gamble? The men said they were having a friendly game. He asked to join. They said it was a private game. He offered a gold piece, his lucky gold piece, which he apparently owned, and which the men wanted to see before they'd say a thing. He told them that if he spent it he'd be completely destitute, and might as well lose it at dice.

"Sorry, mate," one of them said. "It's a private, friendly game. You're not welcome."

Anders said that was all right, he'd really only come to speak to the woman in charge inside, and started walking towards the door. The men stepped in front of it. He told them she was expecting him and would be very unhappy with anyone who kept him out. They asked what her name was. He said that was for him to know and them to find out. They asked what the man's name was. There isn't a man, one of them said, and Anders said he thought he'd got the wrong place.

While they were watching him one of the guards turned and hammered on the door with the flat of his fist. Three heavy thumps. Someone inside was meant to hear it. Nobody came out, but Anders had heard it too.

Fenwick had promised to come running and cause a distraction if Anders put both hands in the air. Anders never did, and I'd say that was the wisest decision anyone made on that street. He told the men to keep playing, that he'd look for his friend somewhere else, and walked backwards until he was out of range, and only then turned his back and walked.

Skylar met him halfway. "That did not work," she told him, in a low voice. "Out loud, you drunk idiot. Just walk the other way."

A couple of minutes later a man came out of the tannery. He wasn't dressed like the Ropewalks. His clothes were better, and he had the manner of someone who was used to being somewhere else. He glanced towards where we were standing, which was far enough away that he shouldn't have been able to pick us out, and then he walked away down the street in a different direction. Skylar watched him go, and fixed him in her head, the coat, the walk, how he turned at the corner.

"I think they've made us," she said. "We'd better go in."

"Let's go kick a door in," Caspian said.

* * *

We didn't all go together. Fenwick had ended up talking to a local kid, one of the pair we'd been ignoring, and he was still at it. The kid said the man playing dice at the tannery was his older brother. He wasn't bad, the kid said, he just sort of looked after the place, kept the local people from being annoyed. He said the man had turned Anders around and it had been quite funny. Fenwick had liked him. He stayed and listened, and the rest of us went on.

Five of us went, then. Moss went with us but not close, half between Fenwick and the group, a big shape hanging back and watching both.

The men at the tannery were already on their feet by the time we were a hundred feet off. Caspian had his shield and his club out. One of the men stepped forward and said he didn't want any trouble and we weren't welcome. Caspian said that unless he wanted broken bones he should leave now.

Anders tried a different tack. Whatever was going on in that building didn't belong in the Ropewalks, he said, and if they stayed to guard it, the Guild would come and wipe out anybody who had. The man whistled, one long note, and the others tightened their grips. Skylar said we were here on business, and they could be part of it or step aside. They said they were paid to keep out riff-raff, and we fitted the description very well. Skylar said we were going in to speak to the people inside one way or another. And Caspian, who had apparently heard enough, leaned in and broke the nose of the man in front of him with his forehead.

He hadn't said he was going to. It happened in the middle of somebody else's sentence. The man's face came apart, and he reeled off to the side, and the others swore and reached for their clubs.

Everything after that happened quickly and out of order, and we can only give it to you as we remember it.

Caspian went for the door. It wouldn't open. It was barred, or locked, or both. He dropped his shield arm and started digging out his crowbar.

Moss was watching the boy. The boy was nowhere near the door. He was down the street, still beside Fenwick, and he had done nothing. He hadn't touched anybody. In one breath Moss decided he was a danger to us, and he was wrong, and he sent the goat back down the street.

It didn't take long to happen and it doesn't take long to tell. Brambleback went in as he always does. The boy was against a wall, and then he was on the ground, and he made a sound. Fenwick was already on his knees beside him with his kit out before any of us understood what we'd heard. We all thought the child was dying. For some time afterwards, we believed he had.

It was the second time. That's all I'll say about that.

The men at the door clubbed at us then, three of them, and at least one hit Archer, and one hit Caspian, and one caught Anders. The fourth broke and ran, shouting that they were being attacked, that someone should come. Archer put a bolt of fire into the nearest man, and the smell of it carried across the yard. Skylar's whip took another off his feet. Caspian used his club, and used it on purpose so that it would not kill. Moss lashed a man with a vine of thorns and dragged him stumbling across the dirt. Fenwick, once the boy was breathing, came up at a run, threw his club and dropped one where he stood, and afterwards had to go and pick it up. Anders threw two blades of pale humming light after the man who was running, twenty paces off and still going, and both of them landed. The man went round the corner of the building and was gone, and none of us saw him again.

When it was over, two of the men on the ground would not be getting up. A third was still breathing and didn't look as though he minded lying there. We had told them what would happen. Anders reminded us of that, twice, in the voice of a man who has already decided what he's going to feel.

* * *

The door was still shut.

Caspian worked at it with the crowbar. He'd got the end into the frame and something behind it creaked, some bar or beam on the inside, but it wasn't going to be quick. Skylar tried the windows and gave up on them. Anders searched the men for keys, and found about six silver, some clubs, and no key.

The three knocks we'd watched the guard use were no use now. Skylar pointed out that they had been heavy, and that whoever was inside would know better than to open to a crowd.

It was Moss who thought of the elk. He became one, all shoulders and antlers, and put his head down and hit the door at a run. The door did not care. He bounced. He took the whole impact himself and stepped back, looking, as much as an elk can, deeply put out. Anders had climbed onto his back for the charge. We made a great deal of noise about that, and we were still laughing when we heard the crowd.

It wasn't loud yet. It sounded like a lot of people behind us on the street, shouting, and coming together. Skylar looked over her shoulder once and said nothing.

It took Caspian something like five minutes to lever the beam apart. When the door finally gave, it went all at once.

* * *

The beam came down and the door came in with it, and crossbow bolts came out.

Two of them hit Caspian and two missed. Three hit Anders. It was over before any of us saw it happen. Both of them dropped in the doorway, one on top of the other, and neither of them moved.

Archer had put a fire-bolt through the open door, blind, and heard someone scream inside. Then he stepped out of the doorway so he wouldn't be shot, and moved to where he could still see. We could hear the mob at our backs now. It was closer, and it wasn't going to wait for us.

Moss went in as an elk. It was the only way he could. He came in past his friends, and there wasn't room to do it without stepping on them. He trampled Caspian on the way through, and Caspian, lying there, did not have much left to be trampled with. Then Moss was inside, in a big, dark workshop with the beams overhead. There were crossbows behind a barricade at the far end, and heaps of rubbish piled about to slow anyone coming in. He counted a large double door at the back, and three smaller ones in the walls.

Fenwick came in on Brambleback's back, a gnome on a goat, and as they went through the doorway he slid off and knelt beside Caspian and worked on him. Skylar was hauling Anders out of the doorway by the collar, and had no way to help him beyond that. Behind her, Archer fired another bolt into the dark. He saw the crossbowmen for a moment in the flash. He also saw an elk, and a goat, and both of them heading for the barricade.

Moss reached them. The men were well made, well armed. They weren't the town thugs from the front. These were hired, or trained, or both, and there were four of them. One of them went down when Moss and the goat hit him together. Two dropped their crossbows and drew short swords.

* * *

Then the woman came out.

She came through one of the three small doors, screaming, and I've never heard a person use that kind of language in that kind of voice. Moss, in the middle of the room, looked at her and knew something was wrong with her. Not the screaming. The feeling of her. He said later it was like a smell, or a taste on the back of his tongue. Corruption was the only word for it. Wrongness.

She had a small knife, barely a dagger. She lunged at the elk with it, and where it went in, the elk aged. We saw that. Those of us who could see in the dark saw it happen: grey coming into a pelt, the lines of an old animal round the eyes. Anders said afterwards that an elk that ages just gets bigger antlers. He said it as though it were a comfort.

Skylar put darts of light into her from the doorway, and so did Archer, and she went down. Not all the way. She was hurt, moderately, and getting up. Caspian had got back on his feet and pressed a hand to his own chest, and the pain that had been there eased enough for him to keep moving. He went in among the swordsmen, who were between him and the woman, and did what he could. Fenwick came in behind him and hit things with a rolling pin that now weighed a good deal more than it used to.

It went badly for a long time. I can't give it to you in order. Nobody who was there can.

We remember the woman vanishing. Fenwick was standing right in front of her, and then he wasn't looking at her, and she was standing beside Anders, who was still trying to get up, and she stabbed him. Anders went down again. It happened to him more than once. Three times, maybe four. Nobody has the count, and Anders least of all. She caught Skylar with the knife, and we saw the lines come into her face. Fenwick was aged by it too, slightly, and so was Moss, and it left each of us she touched a little older than we should have been.

When one of her people went down for good, she took something from him. That was how we understood it. We saw it twice before we saw it clearly. A man would fall, and she'd look at him, and something would pass out of him and into her. She'd be lying on the floor, seemingly finished, and she'd stand up.

Skylar hit her with a lash of the whip so hard it dropped her, and that time it seemed to be for good. A man near Caspian collapsed at the same moment. She stood up. She looked at Skylar and stabbed her.

Moss's elk had dropped, and he came out of it as himself, lying on the floor, quite badly hurt. Fenwick left the fight to reach him and patched him up. Then he ran to Skylar. She woke up to the sight of a gnome's face above hers.

We thought Anders was dead. It's the only word for what it looked like. He'd gone down under the knife and stayed there, and for a while none of us was in any doubt. We were wrong, or we weren't quite right. Moss got to him first, and did something with his hands and with what he knew about growing things. It brought him back to the very edge of himself. It cost him. From then on healing magic seemed to take on him only about half as well, and we didn't know how long that would last.

She came at Moss after that. She came at whoever was weakest, that was how we worked it out, the one who was closest to dying. It seemed to be what she was hungry for.

Anders got up one more time. He hit her with both blades, and one of those blows was as clean as any he'd ever landed, and she fell. Caspian shot her with his shortbow, and Skylar cracked the whip again, and that was the last of it. She dropped in the doorway and she did not move, and this time she stayed as she'd fallen.

Nobody said she was dead. We just looked at her.

"Someone decapitate the body," Skylar said, and nobody argued.

* * *

Moss put his boot down on her once, hard, the way you'd finish something you didn't trust. Then he went to heal Anders first, and that was the right order.

Anders was in a bad way. He'd gone down so often he could hardly do more than shuffle, and he was so tired he looked like a man wearing his own coat backwards. Skylar took a short sword from one of the men on the floor and went to work.

She was there a long time. It's harder than anyone tells you. It took the better part of a minute, and none of us watched all of it. When it was done she stood up and didn't say anything.

Anders found her keys. He went through the body carefully, because there might be something on it that mattered, and he found a small knife, and a pouch with five gold pieces in it. He took them both. He didn't say anything about it. He kept the knife, and for reasons of his own kept it well out of sight.

The crowd outside had reached the door. We could hear it, an animal noise.

"There are prisoners in here," Moss said.

We all knew it. Slate had said it, and the contract said it, and we'd been told to bring them out. But anyone who went through the doors in the walls was going to find the mob on the other side when they came out again. It was plain from the noise outside. If we went in to look for prisoners, we wouldn't be leaving without meeting the people outside.

So we chose. Anders said he was going to set them free. Moss said he was with him. Fenwick threw Anders a light crossbow and took one for himself. Caspian said he'd go because there were people to save. Skylar told us the mob was almost on us. She said she was going to take the hit and run, and Archer said he'd go with her.

She was hurt, and she'd been hurt for hours. She'd used the last of herself keeping the fight up. She ran. She went out through the front with the thing she was carrying held close, and Archer ran with her. A few of them chased the pair of them. Most of them, as she found out later, went straight for the tannery.

The rest of us went through a door and down into a cellar. The door shut behind us.

There were six prisoners in the cellar, chained to the wall and drugged. Two of them had the Guild brand on their shoulders, and we all looked at that a good while. Anders unlocked what he could. None of them woke. We were still getting the shackles off when the mob came down the stairs.

Caspian tried to hold the entrance. He called up vines across the opening and they held for a moment, and the first people to reach them cursed and tore through. There were so many of them. Afterwards we heard there had been about two hundred of them. Maybe that's high. We weren't in a position to count. Some of them weren't villagers at all. There were fighters among them who knew exactly what they were doing, and who'd been around long enough to know all our tricks and a few we hadn't thought of. Skylar worked out afterwards that some of them were the thieves' guild's own. The Ropewalks, it turned out, had friends.

Anders chained himself to the wall and tried to look unconscious. They saw him at once. Moss tried to become something small and go somewhere high. It made no difference. Fenwick, who'd been holding one last piece of cleverness, let it go and stood in the light as himself. He said it was all right, that it was just him, and that we should go. Then the mob got to us, and none of us were in a state to stop it.

We didn't use anything that would kill. Not even at the end. That was the one decision we made together, without discussing it.

They beat us until the room went dark.

* * *

Skylar reached the Guild with Archer at her shoulder. She could only hobble, and every time she thought about having left the others in that building she tried to hobble faster. Once she was sure nobody was following, she stopped and wrapped what she was carrying properly in a strip of cloak. She got a few odd looks. She walked straight through the Guild gate, and straight to Slate. Archer went in with her and stood by the door, and said nothing.

He looked at her and asked where the others were. She said she thought they'd been caught by a mob. Slate asked why a mob would be after the rest of us. She reminded him about the goat and the cobblestones. She said she thought we might have done it again, and upset the thieves' guild a bit more. Or rather one of us had.

"You attacked a child?" Slate asked.

"No," said Skylar. There had been men guarding the tannery, she said, and they wouldn't leave, and a child had walked up while things were starting and got in the way of a goat. She'd seen the mob coming. She'd yelled a warning to the others and been hurt. She'd left because the woman kept getting back up.

She put the cloak on his desk and unwrapped it.

"So this is the woman we were supposed to get," she said.

It wasn't until she'd got away that she understood the others hadn't followed her out. There'd been two hundred of them, she said, and she wasn't going to go back through that on her own. She'd come to find someone who could.

Slate looked at the desk for a long time.

"This is unfortunate," he said, and then, more quietly: "Odalys is going to be upset."

He dismissed her, and told her he'd want a report by the evening. Skylar went to the infirmary to be looked at, and after that she went to look for the others.

* * *

We woke up in the Guild's medical halls with our heads ringing. We'd been carried there, that was all we knew. A number of Guild members, someone said, had been rescued from a mob that was about to string them up, and were now being cared for. That was us.

Nobody ever told us who had done the rescuing. We asked, and we were told it had been handled.

We were healed as far as the wounds went, but we were wrung out, worse than before. We had concussions, too, and those didn't heal so easily. Anders was in a worse way than any of us. He was propped up like a man with both arms and both legs in plaster, and he complained about it steadily. The rest of us looked at each other. Everyone she'd touched looked about a year older. One of us looked two years older, and we've argued ever since about which, since it is hard to tell on a gnome.

There was something else about Anders, and Fenwick saw it first. A streak of hair above his left temple had gone white in the night, dead white, from the root. His hands were cold. The ward was warm and the fire was lit, and his hands were as cold as a well rope. He said it was nothing and pulled the blanket up. Nobody asked him where he'd been.

Skylar came in and sat by Fenwick's bed. He'd been the last to go down, and had seen the most. She asked what had happened, and he told her as much as he could through the fog. Then she went round to the rest of us, in turn, to be sure.

Everyone had to write a report. That was the rule. Skylar wanted to help us make the reports look as good as possible, and Anders wanted to blame Moss for everything. Moss said he'd tell the truth. He wrote that as far as he'd been aware the child was a danger to us, and that he'd been right, because we'd been attacked by a mob soon afterwards. Skylar said he'd been wrong on both counts. Anders said we should all tell the truth, because Slate would be comparing our reports side by side. Nobody embroidered anything.

We learnt two things while we lay there. The child hadn't died. Two of the men hired to stand at the front had. Anders said that if we'd all attacked the men at once, the wounds would have been hard to tell apart, and wondered if anyone would think the crowd had done it. Fenwick pointed out that the men had been fine before the crowd. Caspian told us, once more, to keep it plain.

It took a couple of days before we could stand without holding on to something. Archer had run out with Skylar and had missed the cellar and the beating. He was on his feet before the rest of us were.

When we were on our feet, all six of us were summoned to Slate's office.

* * *

Slate said, "I think you know why you're here."

He said that in some ways we'd achieved the objective. We'd dealt with the woman, and that had been the main thing. Fenwick said flawless victory, and asked to see the contract again. Slate said that in other ways we obviously knew where we hadn't. He said that being beaten unconscious by an angry mob was one of them.

"Oh, that bit," Moss said.

"We forgot to inventory the goods," Fenwick said, very sorry about it.

Slate said that was understandable. The main objective had been achieved, which was a mark in our favour. We'd also recovered the prisoners, which was good. However, Slate said, the situation in the Ropewalks, and with Odalys, was not good.

He didn't tell us what she'd done. He told us she'd like to meet us.

"Who's Odalys?" Moss asked.

"She runs the Ropewalks."

We asked whether he'd advise us to go. He said that if we didn't go, there'd be no point in our staying in Stoneharbour at all. We asked whether the Guild wanted us to go. Slate said he would prefer to keep us. If he lost us because she killed us, so be it. If he lost us because we were moved on to another guild, he'd lose us. If she met us and didn't kill us, that was a win. It was the only chance he had of getting anything back from us.

He said it flatly, and without malice. That was the worst of it.

Anders said, "Let's go. When we're healed."

* * *

They took our weapons first. Then they blindfolded us and walked us through what felt like a long series of passages, damp and turning, and when the cloths came off we were in a room with a table, and a woman sitting at it.

She had a knife and an apple. She was carving pieces off the apple and eating them, one at a time, without looking up.

"So you're the Guild group," she said, "who thought it would be a good idea to murder a child, and then several months later come back and do it again."

Skylar said it hadn't been a group decision.

Odalys said it didn't matter whose decision it was. Fair enough, the people at the tannery had been mixed up in some dodgy business, and they'd known the risks. But she'd have expected a little more effort to do the right thing. There was an understanding between the Guild and the underworld, she said. Nobody killed anybody. It was an unwritten agreement, and it had just been damaged.

Caspian said he'd used non-lethal force on the men at the front. Skylar said so had she. Fenwick said he'd done the same. Odalys asked what we had to say in our defence, and Archer explained how he'd used the firebolt, which was not very gentle. Anders said we had announced ourselves as Guild members. Skylar said he had not. He'd told them the Guild would come and kill them if they stayed. Anders said they'd attacked him first. He said he'd only spoken to them at the start, and everyone had told him he was drunk.

Then she looked at us, one by one, and gave a single nod to Caspian, Skylar and Fenwick. It was for having tried, at least, to honour the agreement. It was the only kind thing she said. Anders, Archer and Moss were told plainly that they weren't welcome in the Ropewalks, except on Guild business, and only on Guild business.

What she had really wanted to say came last.

It wasn't the fighting. Men who take a job like that know what it costs. It was that a child had been talking to Fenwick and doing nothing else, and somebody had gone out of his way to send a goat across the street and put him against a wall. That was what had turned the crowd, she said. Without that, the rest of it would never have drawn the same response.

The boy had lived, Odalys said. Serious wounds, though. He'd never trust a goat again.

"Shouldn't trust goats," Moss said. He said it quietly, to no one. "To be clear."

Odalys finished her apple.

* * *

That was all there was to it. She let us go. It was, as Slate had put it, a win, and we were too tired and too sore to think it was much of one.

Slate's advice, when we got back, was to get our contracts bought, and to leave Stoneharbour. Caspian said that wasn't our choice, and none of us were here by choice. Slate said this was a proving ground, and that people who proved themselves capable usually got their contracts bought. He suggested we stick our heads down, do a good job, and leave.

The Guild had its own opinion of us, of course, and it had shifted. It hadn't shifted evenly. The mission had been carried out, and the prisoners recovered, and for that some of us were marked up, and the rest of us were marked up and then marked down again for how we'd handled the people of the Ropewalks. Fenwick, of all of us, was closing on his next promotion. He asked what a grade two would actually get him, and nobody could tell him.

It was Anders who spoke last. He said he would go on taking nothing from the stores, or borrow Fenwick's basic gear when he had to. And when Fenwick was promoted, he'd get his trunk at the end of his bed and fill it with all the good things a grade two could keep there. It was the first thing any of us had laughed at in days.

The last thing that week was gossip. It came round the barracks in the usual way, sideways, from someone who'd heard it from someone else. Lovis at the stores, the clerk who'd hated Anders for drawing a line through a page of his ledger, was not, it turned out, particularly good friends with Fred.

Anders said he'd take it, and thanked whoever it was. It was, in the whole of that week, the only piece of news that had come to us for free. He said it with his hands in his sleeves. When he passed the goat pen, Brambleback backed away from him, and kept backing until the wall stopped him.

We didn't know yet how long the Ropewalks would remember us.
