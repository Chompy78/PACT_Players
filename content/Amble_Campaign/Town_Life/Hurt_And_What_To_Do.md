Most injuries in Stoneharbour are treated by somebody who is not a healer, for a few coppers, badly.

Read the box your character qualifies for — see [[What_Your_Character_Knows|What Your Character Knows]].

> [!info]- ⚪ COMMON — no proficiency needed
> **A bonesetter** is in every working district, usually with another trade. A silver for a limb set and
> splinted, three for a bad one. They are good at this and they do several a week.
>
> **Ellery Ward, upper Market Steps**, is the town's proper physicker. A gold for a visit. He can name what
> is wrong with you, which is not always the same as being able to fix it.
>
> **The Resting Stair on Shrine Hill** takes people who have nowhere else. It is an almshouse, not a
> hospital, and they will not turn you away.
>
> **Magical healing costs what a year costs.** Fifteen gold to close a wound. Forty to lift a disease. A
> potion is fifty. See [[What_Magic_Costs|What Magic Costs]].
>
> So people mend slowly, or they mend wrong. A limb that set badly ten years ago is the commonest
> permanent injury in this town, and half the dockers have one.

> [!tip]- 🔵 TRAINED — proficient in **Medicine** and **Insight**
> What can actually be done without magic: bleeding stopped, bones set, wounds cleaned, fevers watched,
> poisons made survivable if you are there early.
>
> What cannot: anything inside the chest or the skull, anything already infected for days, and anything
> that has taken a limb.
>
> **Time is the treatment.** A broken arm is six weeks whoever sets it, and two days of rest gets you back
> on your feet rather than back to work.
>
> **Going down and getting back up costs you.** Anyone brought back from the edge is exhausted afterwards
> in a way that sleep alone does not fix, and doing it twice in a week is how people die of something
> unrelated a month later.
>
> Knowing when to spend the fifteen gold is the whole skill. Most of the time the answer is not yet.

> [!warning]- 🟣 RARE — **expertise in both Medicine and Insight**
> You can tell how somebody was hurt, not just that they were. Defensive wounds, a fall against a blow, a
> burn that went on too long to be an accident. The Watch cannot do this, which is occasionally useful.
>
> You can also tell when a body is wrong in a way injury does not explain: somebody young who is tired in
> an old way, a wound that closed too fast, a person who has lost something the year has not taken back.
>
> Do not say any of that in front of the person. Say it afterwards, to whoever needs to hear it.
