# Chapter 9 — Watch and Learn

The job was simple, and we were told so three times. We were to walk an agent of the guild across Stoneharbour, stand where she put us, watch her serve a notice, and keep our hands where everyone could see them. The guild liked things said three times. It liked them written down afterwards too, and by the end of that day we had written down a great deal.

Factor Slate sent for us before the sun was properly up, which was the first bad sign. The man who came to wake us was a friend of Fred's, and he wanted us to know it. He made remarks about our appetites all the way out of the barracks, and kept glancing at Moss's middle as if it might answer back. We skipped breakfast.

Slate was behind his desk when we came in. He did not look up straight away. "You're here finally," he said. "I said to come straight after you woke. It's quite late."

"Sorry, Slate," Anders said. "The man who came for us didn't pass the message on fast enough. We came as soon as we heard."

"There's a job," Slate said. "Agent Blane is waiting in the courtyard. You escort her to a debt recovery at Market Steps. Simple. Nobody needs hurting. If you're attacked, you defend yourselves, but you avoid killing anyone unless there's no other way."

He tapped the desk. "We've got a good arrangement with most of the people down there. There's a scuffle now and then, and everyone walks away. It's only when someone dies that it gets expensive. Especially for children, people take it personally."

We could all see he was leaving something out. He had the look of a man who had been handed a job and had not enjoyed passing it on.

The agent in the courtyard was a middle-aged woman with a worn satchel on her hip. She looked at us as though we were late, which we were. She asked for the passcode. Skylar said, "Here's your passcode," and elbowed Anders hard in the ribs.

"I am Agent Dorra Blane," she said. "This is your first recovery notice, I understand." She read an address off the folded paper in her hand without looking up. "Number eight, Market Steps."

"I recognise that," Moss said.

We all stopped at once, as if we had all had the same thought.

"It might be Dela's," Skylar said.

"It's a place that keeps giving free meals to everybody," Moss said. He thought about it. "To us."

Anders said that explained why Slate had been uncomfortable. He had not wanted to do it himself, so he had sent us. We were the ones who had been eating at Dela's for a year and a half, and she had fed us most days, at cost. Nobody said so out loud, but we knew it was the reason.

"All we have to do is turn up and keep our mouths shut," Caspian said.

"Good luck with that," Skylar said, "when Dela's all friendly and offering food."

Blane looked at us over the top of the paper. "Be kind to the people you talk to. It's stressful, and it's more stressful for them than it is for you. If you do everything as simply and kindly as you can, nothing goes wrong."

"What happens to them?" Skylar asked. "If it's a default."

"Their goods get seized and sold. If that doesn't cover it, they go into indentureship."

Moss said, "Would it be easier if we just didn't talk?"

"No. You're here to learn and to listen. It's just paperwork. It's unpleasant, but it isn't complicated. It's just unpleasant."

Anders asked whether we could go to the barracks first and collect our kit. Blane said to be quick, so we went. Caspian came too, and said nothing the whole way, which was probably the most useful thing he did that morning.

* * *

At the barracks, Anders said he wanted a word with Jory before we went down.

Skylar said no. We had no time, and Jory was not on our side. "I really don't think you're on our team," Skylar said. Anders's denial came with an awkward pause.

Anders said it was only a heads-up. "He's one of us now," he said. "We ought to make a bit of amends for the bad stuff."

He went anyway. We told Blane we would be a minute, and Blane, who had seen a great many people go off to do things, told us to be quick.

Jory was getting into his gear for a training day. He did not want to hear from us, and said so. Anders told him plainly that his sister was living at Dela's, that the recovery was going there that morning, and that we had been sent to meet an agent none of us knew. Jory heard him out, or some of him. We never found out what he did with it.

Anders caught us up at the barracks gate. "Alright," he said. "We're gone."

On the walk, Skylar kept at Blane about the notice. "There's a rule about goods," she said. "You can't take anything off a property without a warrant. Right?"

"Right," Blane said. "You write down what's there. The warrant comes later, and then the guild comes to collect."

"So she can hand stuff over to us today," Skylar said, "but if it comes to us seizing it, we only write it down."

"That's it." Blane shifted the satchel on her hip. "People can always pay what they owe. It's the simplest way out of any of it."

She took us to a pancake shop and bought breakfast for everyone. The place smelled of butter and hot syrup, and it was the first kind thing the guild had done for us in a while. Moss had his mouth full when he asked whether a person could pay seven hundred and fifty gold and be done with the whole thing.

"A contract's exit debt would be a great deal more than that," Blane said, "once you looked at the detail."

Moss put his fork down. "Then I'd rather not look at my contract."

"Probably sensible." Blane cut into her pancake. "A normal debt comes to three or four hundred, over four or five years. Seven hundred and fifty is a lot."

* * *

We knew the smell before we saw the sign. We had known it for a year and a half.

Dela came out from the kitchen with her arms already open. She counted us off as we came in, one at a time, as though she had been waiting for the number to come right. "Moss! Anders! Skylar! Archer! Caspian! You're early, Caspian. You're never early for breakfast. Sit down, sit down."

"This isn't a social call, Dela," Skylar said. "This is Agent Blane from the guild. It's a work call."

Then she saw Blane, and her face changed, though she kept smiling for a while longer.

Toby was at the end of the counter, eating his breakfast, and he looked up at us the way a child looks at men who have come to take the furniture. Nessa stood beside him. She gave us the look you would give a stray dog that had got into the bins.

Blane read out the notice. "Unfortunately, you have defaulted against your loan agreement with the guild. As you are well above the hundred-gold threshold, we require either immediate payment to bring the sum below it, or an inventory of your goods on these premises."

Dela sat down on the nearest stool and began to cry, without making any noise at all. Nessa came round the counter and held her. Toby got down from his stool and went to stand against the two of them, and the three of them stayed like that while Blane waited.

None of us said anything. It was strange, seeing her like this at all, after how carefully she'd always managed everything else — the free bread, the extra helpings, the bowl that turned up with no explanation attached. We had never once seen Dela unable to do something.

Blane looked at us. "Do we have your permission to do the inventory?" Dela nodded.

"Nobody say anything," Archer said quietly. For a while, nobody did.

We started. There was a large cooking pot on the range, with porridge bubbling in it and no fire under it, and we decided not to think about that. Bowls, sacks and jars of salt and spice lined the shelves. Anders had the pencil and a rather unnatural love of neat columns. He wrote, we called out the numbers, and the list grew.

When we came to the pot, Toby came over and told us in a flat voice, the voice of someone who had been told to say it. "It's magic. It belonged to her family for generations. You can't take it."

"We're not taking anything," Caspian said.

"We're writing it down," Skylar said. "That's what an inventory is. Somebody has to say what it's worth."

"Why?" Toby asked.

"Because you fell into debt," Caspian said, and then looked as though he wished he hadn't.

"Anything written down goes for its written value," Blane said. "Write down *large cooking pot* and it brings a few silver. Half a gold at most."

Anders had a plan. "We lift the pot off the range and write down an ordinary pot," he said. "Then when the goods are sold, we buy it back for Dela. Far cheaper than her buying back a magic pot."

"You can't guarantee we'll be able to buy it," Skylar said.

"It'll still be cheaper than her buying back a magic pot."

Then he worked out the money. Two gold a month each, he said, so sixteen between us. He started again, lost count somewhere past ten, and started a third time.

In the end Skylar said the honest thing was the only thing. "It's a pot that's magic," Moss said. Then he said he was going to put Fluffy outside. He did not sound like a man who was going to stay. He got up too fast, nearly knocked the stool over, and went out of the room as though the walls had got closer. Fluffy went with him. For a while after that he was not in the room.

Cass came back from the kitchen before we could stand up properly. She put a bowl in front of each of us, as if we were the ones who needed looking after. She had already served Blane, she said, and she was so sorry we had come on such a morning. She gave second helpings to anyone who finished too quickly, which was Moss, again. Then she sat down herself and told us we were such kind people, and that was the worst part of it.

* * *

We finished the count in the storeroom, all of us except Moss. The shelves ran to the ceiling, crowded with jars and tins, and the air was cold and stale. The bottle was on a shelf at the back. It was unlabelled and ordinary, the kind of bottle you would find at the back of any cook shop, and every one of us looked at it at the same moment.

It reminded us of the woman in the warehouse, the one who had kept making us old. Skylar had put her down for good, and she had carried the head to the guild, and the guild had paid us our way out of the thieves' guild. Skylar said the bottle had the same feeling about it. Bad energy, she said. We all agreed, because we did.

Anders decided to ask Dela directly. He shut his eyes, reached into his own head, and spoke to her without a sound. It was strange to hear her voice in there, saying things nobody else could hear. Did she want them to log the bottle? Or could they just write it down as a random bottle, like all the others?

"It's a bottle," Dela said, inside his head. "It was given to me by my grandfather. He said never to open it until the time came when I'd know that I needed to."

"Right," Anders said, out loud, to nobody. "We'll write it down as a random bottle. I'll bring one from the guild stores and swap it in, so you don't lose it."

I didn't know that yet, but we would be carrying that bottle around for the rest of the day, like a debt nobody would take off our hands.

Fenwick came running into the shop about then, coat flapping, with no notion of what he had walked into. Dela was serving someone a bowl, as she always was. He jumped onto a stool and started talking. Then he saw that everyone was a little bit down.

"Fen," Skylar said. "This isn't a meal trip."

"Slate asked us to escort Agent Blane here to serve a notice," Anders said. "A debt recovery. We'll fill you in."

Fenwick looked at his bowl. He had pushed it away and was no longer hungry, he said. Dela had looked at him and asked whether he always ate her food, and whether it was not good any more. "It's lovely," Fenwick said. "I'm conflicted."

Blane handed the satchel to Skylar. In it were the signed notice and the inventory. Take them back to the guild, Blane said, and she said it kindly. "It's paper," Skylar said, weighing it on her hip, and she was right. It was paper.

Blane stayed behind with the woman. It was kinder, she said, for someone to be with her, and nobody argued. Fluffy wandered back in just then and sat down at Fenwick's feet. Moss came in after it.

"Is Fluffy part of the inventory?" Fenwick asked. Nobody knew. Nobody wanted to be the one to say.

* * *

Anders had stopped at a stall on the way out and bought a plain bottle for a copper, which he said was cheaper than fetching one from the guild stores. We had barely reached the end of the street when a man stepped into our path. There were more behind him, close enough that we could hear them breathing. He looked at the satchel, then at Skylar, and smiled as if he had been looking forward to it.

"That'll have coin in it," he said. "Or something worth coin."

"It's paper," Skylar said.

"Well, that's worth coin too. Hand it over and we can all stay friends."

"You're from the Ropewalks, right?"

"How d'you know that?"

"You've got the air about you." Skylar shifted the satchel. "Look, this isn't going to go the way you want. Let's just walk away from this one. We know how it goes when we deal with each other."

"Yeah, we know," the man said. He looked past her, at the goat. "We recognise the goat."

"I'm not with the goat," Skylar said.

Then they told us what they wanted. "Something from Moss," the man said. "Something from Archer. Something from you, Anders, too." He shrugged. "Anything valuable. Then you walk." They did not sound as if they meant to kill anyone. They sounded tired, like people whose job has dragged on too long.

"We're splitting up," Skylar said, low. "I take the satchel to the guild and tell them the three of them got stopped by the roadblock men. Then somebody comes out before anyone gets hurt." Fenwick went with her, close at her elbow.

We were free to walk on with the other two, the man said, or to stay where we were.

"I will stay with my colleague," Caspian said. He had the voice of someone who had decided something on the spot and was not going to be talked out of it. "We're still in the market, aren't we?"

"The whole town's our territory," the man said. "It's just that most of our people are down the Ropewalks."

"Then I'm happy to stay," Caspian said. "It's fair game, as far as I'm concerned."

Anders stepped forward. He said that if we had taken so much from their organisation, it was only fair that they asked something back, and he took the plain knife out of its sheath. It looked like nothing. That was the joke, and he had clearly been waiting all morning to make it. "This is a healing knife," he said. "It heals you. It's very special."

The man took the knife and threw it as far as he could.

"I'll go and get it," Anders said, already moving. "I don't want it ending up in the wrong hands."

Caspian looked up then. There were figures on the rooftops on both sides of the street, crouched behind the chimneys. They had been there for a while. Nobody had mentioned them.

The man turned back to Caspian. "This is your last chance to walk, friend," he said. "While we can still call you friend."

"I'm not much of a friend if I just let you take my friend away, am I?"

"We're not taking them away," the man said. "They've never said they were taking anybody. They've said they want something. Give us something, or you can't pass."

Anders came back with the knife, dusty from the gutter, and held it out again. It was worth more than it looked, he said. The man said he had been to the market and could buy his own knives there, thank you, and threw it again. He did not want any of Anders' pretend knives, he said, and Anders had a fairly outrageous story and no chance of convincing anyone with it.

"How many of us are there?" the man asked.

"Four," said Moss, who then said he spoke Common, Goliath and Gnomish. Nobody asked him to prove it.

They counted. A quarterstaff. A club. A goat. Caspian had a dagger, and said so.

"Give us your daggers and you can go," the man said to all of us, and Anders handed over his dagger, and then the one copper he had left, because the other had gone on the bottle. Moss had no coin to hand over, only an empty purse, and he threw it at them from twenty feet away. It fell short. Archer gave up his dagger. Caspian looked at the man for a long moment and then gave up his.

"Fine," he said. "Here's my dagger."

The men backed away into the alley, not hurrying. "Thanks for your business," their leader said. "See you next week."

* * *

Six guild men came out of a building halfway back to the guild, and one of them looked us over with some interest. "We heard you were being attacked," he said.

"You don't look very attacked," Anders said.

"We had to give up all our possessions," Anders said. "Because you took your time."

The leader of the guild men looked hard at Anders for some time, and then he nodded.

"You're Anders, isn't it?"

Anders said he was afraid so.

"Well known in the guild," the man said, looking at Anders the way you'd look at a stain you'd already given up on getting out. "You've upset the response team," he said. "You've upset more people than everyone else put together, and in an organisation this size, that takes a particular kind of effort."

* * *

We were back at the guild before long. The quartermaster took the inventory, then sent us along to the records clerk, Isabet Marrow. She took the ledger, turned it over, checked the front and back, and thanked us. We asked whether we were to take anything back to the agent. Nothing, she said, unless we had asked for the list of debts for the recovery. We had not.

We ate at the guild that afternoon, at the long table by the wall, where nobody could see us going back to Dela's. Wren came over while most of us were eating. She asked whether we didn't normally eat at Dela's at lunchtime.

"I don't think we're welcome at Dela's any more, Wren," Skylar said. "She got served with a debt recovery this morning."

"Oh, really?" Wren said. "Those things are always wrong. Did you look at the statement of account?"

"We didn't have time to look at anything," Skylar said. "We had a recovery detail," Anders said. "We don't work in admin like you."

Anders wanted to know who usually produced the statements. Wren said the records office always made mistakes, and that she had noticed some in the ledger herself. "Way too late for that one, Wren," Skylar said.

Wren came back about fifteen minutes later with a copy of the statement, already frowning. "The mistakes are all over it," she said. The statement said Dela owed a hundred and forty-six gold. Once an account went over a hundred, a referral was automatic. So the notice had been right, at least that far.

Seventy-five gold of it was things Dela had put down against other people's accounts. A funeral shroud. A roof patch after the storm. A healing for somebody's cold. A healing for somebody having a baby. Toby's poison had its own line, a cure at twenty gold.

"We should have a proper auditor," Fenwick said. He had the expression of someone who had read a very long document and found it cruel. "This makes no sense."

Caspian went through the numbers twice, one finger on the page. "Dela's real debt is nearer a hundred and twenty," he said. "That leaves about twenty-six gold of overcharging. Which still puts her over the hundred." The recovery stood, then. The only question was where the money went. We carried the page to the front of the records office, where Blane was waiting. She had come back in through the gates.

"Get it under a hundred gold," Blane said, "and Dela can have a payment plan. Stay over, and she finds another twenty gold from somewhere, or her goods go and the recovery goes ahead."

Isabet Marrow adjusted the statement. The adjustment was real, she said, but it was still nineteen gold over. She printed a new one and asked us to take it to Blane. We did not have nineteen gold between us, and everyone knew it.

"Could we pay it ourselves?" Anders asked.

"Of course you can," Wren said.

That was the easy part. We did not have the coin to do it, and we had just said so. Anders suggested putting the knife back on Dela's shelf and telling the guild it had been planted on her. Moss said that would end well for Dela. Anders agreed that it would. Nobody did it, and then the subject was dropped, and Anders looked for a while as though someone had taken his best idea and lost it.

* * *

We went back to Dela's for lunch that afternoon, because nobody could think of anywhere else to go. Anders had swapped the bottle on her shelf for the one he had bought. He had done it while she was busy at the stove, and the old one was inside his coat. Caspian went in first. "We've got some semi-good news," he said, "and it's still not the best news." Dela said she would hear it over her bowl.

"I have a way to find the gold," Caspian said. Dela told him not to worry. She had an appointment in a couple of days with the memory-keeper in the market, and it would all be settled by then. She had a week to find the nineteen gold.

"Are you going to sell your memories?" Skylar asked.

"I'm not selling anything," Dela said. "I'm only going to see her."

"Why don't you ask people to pay when they eat?" Skylar asked. Dela said it was a novel idea, and that Cass had said the very same thing. She would start charging more.

"You have to actually charge people," Skylar said.

"I know," Dela said. "I'll do it."

Skylar asked what we owed for lunch. "Three copper," Dela said, "for all of us." Moss had nothing on him, he said, just to be clear.

Then Dela took a silver coin out of her purse and pushed it across the counter at us. We pushed it back. She pushed it across again, harder. We had debts enough of our own, she said — we should spend what little we had on ourselves, not on her. It was all the coin she had left. Nobody argued with her after that.

Fenwick suggested a donation tin on the counter for anyone who could use it. Dela had one out before he'd finished saying it, with a card already written in careful letters — *donations for the soup kitchen in the Low Quay, please donate freely* — as if she'd been keeping it under the counter for weeks, waiting for a reason. The money, we were told, was for the soup kitchen, and not for her. Moss pointed out, as gently as he knew how, that she was the one with a notice on her own counter. Dela said the soup kitchen people had it worse, and that was the end of the conversation.

It was Skylar who worked out what the bottle was. She turned it in the light for a long time before she spoke. "It's a sliver," she said at last. Someone's life, or memory, or whatever made them who they were, had been drawn out and bound into the glass. Slivers were illegal, unless you were rich and powerful, and even then you had to be careful. The memories were the valuable part. A person could not choose which ones went in, we were told, because you could not give up meaningless ones.

Anders asked Dela whether she would sell it. She would not. It was the only thing she had left of her grandfather, she said, and she said it as though the matter were closed.

"If it's a sliver," Dela said, "then it should be destroyed, even if it came from my grandfather." Anders gave her his word that he would destroy it. Dela thanked him a good deal more than he had earned. Moss held out his hand, and Anders gave him the bottle, and Moss put it inside his coat.

* * *

Wren was on the street outside when we came out. Skylar reminded us that the notice said nothing was to leave the property without the warrant. Wren said a sliver would not be on any inventory at all, because they were illegal.

Moss took the bottle out of his coat and held it up the way Skylar had, turning it slowly in the light, as if he might see something in it worth knowing before it was gone for good. Whatever had been drawn out of Dela's grandfather and bound into that glass, none of us would ever know what it was now — and it occurred to more than one of us that maybe that was exactly as it should be. Moss looked at it a moment longer. Then he smashed it on the paving outside the cook shop.

It broke with a small, ordinary sound — nothing like what you'd expect from something that had once been a piece of somebody's life. None of us said anything for a moment. Then we kept walking. That was the end of it.

* * *

Moss went up the hill to the Anchor Light shrine on his own. The rest of us waited at the guild, and Anders waited with us, silent for the whole time, which was unusual enough that we noticed.

When Moss came back down, he gave us the shrine's answer in as few words as he could. "The shrine can't take coin," he said. "Its charter doesn't allow it. They only pay for things that help the shrine grow. Seed, food, farms." He rubbed the back of his neck. "But they'll help with Dela's ingredients, and send a regular shipment down to the cook shop. I asked if the box for the soup kitchen could come down for now. The keeper said no. It's Dela's say. You don't go against someone who's trying to do the right thing."

"It's the best I could do," Moss said. "It's the one good connection I've got."

After that, Caspian went round the local businesses, with Anders beside him for the bits that needed a friendly face. He told each one the same thing, straight. Dela was nineteen gold and some silver away from a payment plan. It had to be done quietly, he said, because she was a proud woman and would not thank anybody for shouting it in the street. "Would you help?"

The first man handed over a gold and a half there and then. He turned round and bellowed for his teenage son. "Dela's in trouble," he told him. "So we're making rounds." Where did they want the money? Caspian said to bring it to him at the guild. "Not a problem," the man said.

* * *

The next morning there was a parcel waiting at the guild, and it held fifty-six gold.

Caspian opened it. For a moment nobody said anything.

We went to the clerk. "The money goes against Dela's account," Caspian said. "It moves her onto a payment plan." The clerk said that was all in order. He would redo the document. Were we able to deliver the new one? We were. Caspian said yes, and then asked whether we could have a receipt. There was a receipt.

"Third-party donation," the clerk said, tapping the line, because things had to be done accurately. Caspian ran his finger down the new statement and stopped. A second donation. Ten gold, from the Anchor Light shrine.

Anders said that maybe that had come about because we had told Jory about Nessa and Dela, that morning, before any of it had gone wrong, and that Jory might have arranged it. Nobody could say for certain. We did not say it was so.

We took the new statement to Dela that afternoon. Fifty-four gold still owing, by Skylar's count, which was a number Dela could say out loud without crying. Moss went into the cook shop first, with a bowl of stew in his hand, and sat down as if he had been there all his life.

Dela had her hands in a sink of water. She told us the shrine had come down to see her and had allocated their donations for the week to her, which was most kind, and she was glad nobody was making a fuss about it in the street. Then she read the statement. She stopped reading. She looked at it again.

"This can't be right," she said. "Who would donate this much gold?"

"It's people paying for all the free meals you've given them over the years," Skylar said.

Dela looked as if she had been caught stealing, and the more we tried to make it better, the worse she looked. Moss put the case as well as it could be put. "The shrine up on the hill will help with your food costs for a while," he said. "They'll send a shipment down, and it won't all be coming from us." Dela said she had never wanted anyone to know. She had put the box out herself, she said, for the soup kitchen. She was embarrassed. After a while she let us sit with her, and she was quiet.

Down in the Low Quay, the soup kitchen had a new heating pot. It was magic, and it was steaming away on the back bench with a great deal of food beside it. Someone had donated the lot. Nobody could say who. "There's a benefactor in this town none of us know about," Anders said. Skylar said it made her think somebody had hired Anders to sabotage the whole town. Anders said no, he was only stoking the fire.

There was an ever-burning lantern in the shrine's loft, too. Skylar said we were not grabbing it.

The recovery was cancelled. There was a payment plan now, and Dela still charged too little for her food, which some people said loudly. From then on, we agreed, we would pay for our meals straight to the guild, against her account. Whatever we were allowed to draw each month would go the same way.

* * *

The rain had stopped by the evening. The town had gone dark and wet, welcoming to some people and not to others. Dela had nearly gone the same way as us, into the guild's indentureship, and she had not. No one died this time.

Moss said that was true, and we all agreed it was, and we all knew there had been other times it wasn't.

Dela was quieter than she had been. She was not as sharp with her tongue, and she did not make her jokes. All afternoon people had argued back whenever she tried to do the right thing, and it had worn her down. "Everyone's meals were a little colder," Moss said. Then he thought about it. "Only ours."

We ate them anyway. She would have been hurt if we hadn't.
